# Where should we put 16 sampling sites?

Open with the [location map](sensor_layout_comparison.png). Each white circle is one cell where we can read concentration. The same blue map sits behind all three layouts. Darker cells fell more often inside the area holding 90% of contaminant mass at the time of sampling. Ask what we want the network to detect.

1. **Warn that the plume is about to shrink.** In 5,000 simulations, the 16 spread-out sites caught none of the 142 large contractions. Sampling 16 places near the center caught 105. False alarms rose from 229 to 261 among the 4,858 other cases. The layout toward the edges never fired. We selected the central and outer layouts after seeing these simulations, so central placement needs a fresh test.
2. **Show where the footprint changed.** In cases with a large contraction, the average footprint fell from 572 to 353 cells. In the other cases it fell from 914 to 782. Blue in the difference maps means fewer cases included that cell in their footprint at the later time. [Footprint maps](ensemble_footprint_occupancy.png)
3. **Compare with seeing the whole plume.** The earlier change in footprint size detected 140 of 142 large contractions and gave 230 false alarms. This comparison uses the entire map and measures footprint size directly. [Whole-map comparison](monitoring_trigger.png)
4. **Ask whether the sites also catch a bad forecast.** Among the 733 cases with saved forecasts, the central sites caught 21 of 24 large contractions and 11 of 45 large forecast errors. Only six cases had both outcomes. Another saved time from 716 of those simulations appeared in model training. [Forecast-error comparison](alarm_and_forecast_error.png)
5. **Connect the result to model updates.** In Yilin’s separate study, mixing older examples into training on new cases reduced rare-case error from 10.04 to 1.21 µg/L. Historical error rose from 0.58 to 1.03 µg/L. Whether updating after a monitoring alarm would improve later forecasts still needs testing.
