If we can sample only 16 places, where should we put them?

Each white circle below is a place where we read the contaminant concentration in a groundwater simulation. We tried three ways to place the same number of sites. The blue background is identical in all three panels. Darker cells more often fell inside the plume footprint, the smallest set of cells that together held 90% of the contaminant mass.

Moving the sites inward caught 105 of 142 large contractions

At each site we took two successive readings. Each layout raised a warning when the combined concentration changes across its 16 sites were unusually large. We then checked the next plume map. A large contraction meant the footprint shrank by about a third or more.

The spread-out sites caught 0 of 142 large contractions. The central sites caught 105 of 142, with 261 false alarms compared with 229 for the spread-out sites among 4,858 other cases. The sites toward the edges gave no warnings. We chose the central and outer layouts after seeing earlier results from these cases. Testing the same layouts on new simulations would show how well that placement transfers.

Where did the plume shrink?

In the 142 cases with large contractions, the average footprint fell from 572 to 353 cells (38%). In the other 4,858 cases it fell from 914 to 782 cells (14%). The maps group cases by what happened later. The warnings used only the two earlier readings.

What could we detect if we could see the entire map?

Tracking the earlier change in footprint size across the whole map caught 140 of 142 large contractions and gave 230 false alarms. This comparison uses both more spatial information and a different measurement. It shows how well an earlier change in footprint size anticipated the next one.

How did we choose the warning thresholds?

Each eSTOMP simulation supplied three saved concentration maps. We used the change from the first to the second to warn about the third. The 16-site score was the root-mean-square (RMS) of the 16 concentration changes, which gives more weight to larger changes. Simulated readings had no added measurement noise. The whole-map score was the absolute relative change in the number of cells containing 90% of the mass (Mass90).

Of 10,000 cases, 5,000 set the thresholds and 5,000 tested them. The largest 3% of footprint changes in the first group set the large-change cutoff at 34.30%. All 142 qualifying changes in the test group were contractions. Each warning fired above its own 95th percentile among threshold-setting cases without a large change. Tightening that percentile to 99 reduced whole-map false alarms from 230 to 36 and contractions detected from 140 to 93. The median change score across the spread-out sites was 15.1 µg/L for cases with a large contraction and 27.5 µg/L for the other cases. That helps explain why an alarm looking for a large score missed every contraction at these sites.

Would we choose the same sites to catch a bad forecast?

For 733 of the test cases we also had a forecast from the U-Net, a model trained to predict the next concentration map. The central sites warned of 21 of 24 large contractions in this smaller group, but only 11 of 45 large forecast errors. Only six cases had both a large contraction and a large error.

How did we measure forecast error?

We compared the predicted and simulated concentrations cell by cell across the 128 × 100 grid, excluding padding. We squared the differences, averaged them, and took the square root. This gives the root-mean-square error (RMSE), in micrograms per liter (µg/L). A separate set of 758 cases set the large-error cutoff at 1.164 µg/L, its 95th percentile.

We recovered 1,491 archived test forecasts and checked their ordering by reproducing predictions for 16 cases. Of the 733 cases used here, 716 had a different saved time from the same simulation in the model’s training data. Performance on entirely new simulations remains an open question.

What happens if we update the forecast model?

Yilin’s separate study tested one way to update the model while helping it remember earlier conditions. During training, each update mixed older examples with new ones in a fixed proportion. This method, called fixed replay, reduced error on rare cases from 10.04 to 1.21 µg/L. Error on historical cases rose from 0.58 to 1.03 µg/L.

For monitoring design, the next test is to choose the sites and the warning rule in advance, then score both outcomes on new simulations. Detecting a shrinking plume and deciding when a forecast model needs updating are two different jobs for a monitoring network.
