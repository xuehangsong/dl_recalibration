Their value for long-term remediation depends on maintaining reliability as the conditions they predict change.Pumping systems, contaminant sources, boundary conditions, and conceptual site models can evolve over the years and decades typical of DOE environmental management. A surrogate trained on historical conditions may then encounter behavior outside its evaluated domain. Monitoring and evidence-based review are needed to determine whether it remains suitable for use.This report connects model maintenance to the governance and quality assurance expectations of environmental decision support: when to intervene, how to assess an update, and what evidence permits deployment.This revised report develops that process through a three-pillar framework and a controlled pump-and-treat U-Net case study. The central questions are when an update is warranted, how the update can preserve useful historical behavior, and what evidence is required before the candidate may be used. The three complementary pillars are:Governance defines acceptance criteria, recalibration triggers, configuration control, and decision authority. Surrogates support screening and planning; high-fidelity physics-based analysis remains authoritative for final regulatory and remedy decisions.Modeling engines provide physics-based reference predictions and fast surrogate approximations. Published applications motivate their potential efficiency, while the end-to-end cost of the present recalibration workflow remains to be measured.Recalibration mechanisms provide uncertainty quantification, drift detection, continual learning, and active learning. Their suitability must be evaluated for the intended conditions.The pillars are connected through MONITOR → DETECT → DECIDE → PREPARE → UPDATE → CALIBRATE → DEPLOY. Continuous monitoring and human review identify whether intervention is warranted; a triggered response prepares data, updates the model, calibrates uncertainty, and evaluates the candidate for promotion. Acceptance criteria and documented decisions connect each step.The U-Net case study provides measured evidence for the update decision. In one reported comparison, fixed replay reduces rare-regime RMSE from 10.04 to 1.21 µg/L while historical RMSE rises from 0.58 to 1.03 µg/L. A separate controller comparison identifies two candidate outcomes that satisfy illustrative adaptation–retention conditions. These results show why rare-regime improvement must be assessed together with historical retention; the comparisons are not a single verified test protocol.The report therefore advances from a conceptual framework and prototype plan to an evidence-backed account of a controlled model-maintenance decision. Prospective drift detection, independent uncertainty calibration, and untouched final-test promotion remain undemonstrated. The candidates are held for further evaluation, and the report specifies the evidence needed to complete a reproducible, governed lifecycle.OverviewDeep-learning (DL) surrogates can accelerate groundwater flow and transport predictions, making large scenario sets and uncertainty analyses more practical. Their reliability can change as pumping systems, source conditions, boundary conditions, or conceptual models change. This report connects a governance-aware framework for managing those changes to a controlled U-Net update case study for pump-and-treat plume prediction. The intended role remains screening, scenario exploration, and planning support within DOE environmental management.The proposed framework is organized around three pillars: Pillar A, Governance, establishes the regulatory alignment, acceptance criteria, configuration control, and decision authority needed to manage adaptive models in compliance-driven environments. It is grounded in U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) environmental modeling guidance and DOE EM-QA-001, which incorporates ASME NQA-1 quality assurance principles for environmental management software and modeling activities. Pillar B, Modeling Engines, encompasses both the physics-based numerical simulators that remain the authoritative source of truth and the DL surrogate models that provide fast approximate predictions. Physics-based models such as PFLOTRAN, MODFLOW, STOMP, and TOUGH2 provide the high-fidelity reference standard, while surrogate architectures such as Fourier neural operators and diffusion models enable rapid inference for screening and planning. Pillar C, Recalibration Mechanisms, provides the technical methods needed to maintain surrogate reliability over time, including uncertainty quantification, drift detection, continual learning, and active learning.Within this framework, surrogate models are explicitly limited to screening-level analyses, scenario exploration, and planning support. High-fidelity physics-based models remain the authoritative basis for final regulatory and remedy decisions. When surrogate validity is in question, governance procedures require withdrawal of surrogate use and escalation to a controlled retraining process, thereby preventing unmanaged risk. This conservative posture is consistent with CERCLA protectiveness expectations and DOE quality assurance requirements.The framework has four objectives. First, it defines a governance structure that aligns surrogate deployment and maintenance with established regulatory and quality assurance requirements. Second, it establishes systematic methods for monitoring surrogate performance and detecting degradation before decision support is compromised. Third, it provides efficient recalibration procedures to restore surrogate accuracy within predefined acceptance criteria while minimizing the burden of new high-fidelity simulations. Fourth, it operationalizes these capabilities through a structured workflow with explicit decision points, documentation requirements, and configuration control to support auditability, reproducibility, and independent review.The workflow has seven distinct steps. MONITOR collects performance and uncertainty evidence; DETECT evaluates possible degradation; DECIDE records the human judgment that an update is warranted. PREPARE assembles traceable data; UPDATE produces a candidate; CALIBRATE assesses and adjusts its predictive uncertainty; DEPLOY applies an independent final test and records the promotion decision. These are framework requirements, and the case study explicitly identifies which requirements have evidence and which remain to be demonstrated.The case study uses reported U-Net/adaptive-replay results to examine rare-regime recovery and historical retention. Mass90 response changes define a retrospective stress condition, and reported update comparisons quantify the resulting tradeoff. An illustrative decision screen retains selected controller outcomes for further evaluation. No interval-calibration or final-promotion artifact is available, so the supported endpoint is a candidate awaiting calibration and final validation.The report makes three connected contributions: a three-pillar governance and methods framework, a quantitative offline update case study mapped to its seven steps, and a concrete evidence specification for advancing toward an operational demonstration. The contribution is the connection between measured performance, decision criteria, and the next required evidence.The current maturity is a defined framework with controlled update evidence. Prospective detector performance, independent uncertainty calibration, final-test promotion, and long-duration use with field data remain unverified. Physics-model limitations, data independence, measurement noise, irregular sampling, and operational complexity must be addressed as the framework progresses. The roadmap prioritizes those gaps while preserving human decision authority.AcknowledgmentsThis work was performed under funding by the Environmental Management Headquarters Technology Operations Office (EM-3.21) to Pacific Northwest National Laboratory. Instead of repeatedly running computationally expensive models (e.g., groundwater flow, contaminant transport, or reactive chemistry), a surrogate model is trained on simulation or observational data to learn the input–output relationships and then provide rapid predictions.These models are particularly valuable for tasks like uncertainty quantification, optimization, and real-time decision support, where thousands to millions of model evaluations may be required. By significantly reducing computational cost while maintaining acceptable accuracy, deep learning surrogates enable faster scenario analysis, integration into AI-driven workflows, and scalable system-level optimization.Cleanup programs at U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Environmental Management (EM) sites such as Hanford, Savannah River, and Los Alamos operate on timelines spanning decades, during which hydrogeological conditions evolve, remediation systems are modified, monitoring networks are updated, and conceptual site models are revised in response to new data. Throughout these extended campaigns, site managers must evaluate alternative remediation strategies, assess system performance, and demonstrate continued protectiveness of human health and the environment under various regulatory frameworks such as requirements established under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) and reinforced by EM quality assurance expectations (NRC 2013; EPA 2001). The purpose of this project is to establish a robust, defensible framework for deploying and maintaining artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled deep learning (DL) surrogate models as reliable decision-support tools over the long-term lifecycle of EM cleanup activities. Physics-based numerical models are the established foundation for developing the technical basis for supporting decision-making throughout the long cleanup timeframes. High-fidelity simulators coupling groundwater flow, contaminant transport, and geochemical reactions provide the defensible, process-based predictions on which regulatory and remedy decisions depend. However, these models are computationally expensive to develop, run, maintain, and update. These computational burdens limit the pace and scope of analyses that site teams can practically perform within project timelines and budgets.DL surrogate models offer a path to overcoming these computational constraints and significantly accelerating and improving modeling efforts, and ultimately, decision-making at sites. By learning the input-output relationships of physics-based simulators from training data, surrogates can approximate numerical model predictions at inference speeds 103 to 105 times faster than the original simulators. This capability enables rapid scenario evaluation, interactive planning, and large-scale uncertainty exploration that would be impractical using physics-based simulation alone. Recent advances in neural operator architectures, convolutional encoder-decoder networks, and physics-informed training strategies have demonstrated surrogate accuracy sufficient for meaningful decision support across a range of subsurface modeling applications.While surrogate models offer significant improvements in computational efficiency, they—like purely physics-based models—must still be rigorously verified, calibrated, and continuously updated as new data become available. A surrogate trained at a single point in time encodes the conditions, parameters, and conceptual understanding reflected in its training data. As site conditions change — through natural system evolution, operational modifications, updated boundary conditions, or revised parameter estimates — the surrogate’s training distribution diverges from the conditions it is asked to predict. This divergence, termed distribution shift or concept drift, causes surrogate accuracy to degrade progressively. Without systematic detection and correction, this degradation can proceed silently, producing predictions that appear reasonable but are no longer reliable.The practical challenge is to connect established machine-learning methods with the governance and quality assurance requirements of environmental decision support. Uncertainty quantification, drift detection, continual learning, and active learning each address part of surrogate maintenance. Their integration must establish when action is justified, how a candidate is evaluated, and who can approve its use. This report addresses that connection for EM groundwater applications.The report defines a structured framework for surrogate monitoring and lifecycle management and evaluates its update decision using available pump-and-treat U-Net evidence. It distinguishes completed offline comparisons from the prospective detection, uncertainty calibration, and independent promotion records needed for operational use.Framework ObjectivesThis report presents a recalibration framework designed to maintain DL surrogate accuracy over the multi-year to multi-decade timelines typical of EM groundwater remediation. The framework has four objectives:Define governance structures that align surrogate model deployment with established regulatory and quality assurance requirements, including CERCLA expectations, U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) environmental modeling guidance, and DOE EM-QA-001. Within this governance structure, surrogate models are explicitly limited to screening-level analyses, scenario exploration, and planning support. High-fidelity physics-based numerical models remain the authoritative basis for final regulatory and remedy decisions.Establish systematic methods for monitoring surrogate performance and detecting degradation before it compromises decision support. These methods integrate UQ, statistical drift detection, and residual analysis into a continuous monitoring process that runs passively during routine surrogate operation.Provide efficient recalibration procedures that restore surrogate accuracy to within defined acceptance criteria upon detection of predictive degradation. Such recalibration employs continual-learning methods to mitigate knowledge forgetting across successive training cycles, and active-learning strategies to identify maximally informative simulation candidates and thereby minimize the computational burden of new physics-model simulations.Operationalize these capabilities through a structured workflow with defined steps, decision points, acceptance criteria, and documentation requirements that support auditability, reproducibility, and independent review.The framework is organized around three pillars – Governance, Modeling Engines, and Recalibration Mechanisms – that together address the coupled technical and institutional requirements of deploying adaptive models in compliance-driven environments.Report Organization The remainder of this report is organized as follows.Section 2.0 presents the framework overview, introducing the three-pillar architecture and the seven-step, two-phase recalibration workflow that operationalizes the framework.Section 3.0 details the methods underlying each pillar, including governance procedures and regulatory alignment, surrogate and physics-model architectures, and the UQ, drift detection, continual learning, and active learning methods that constitute the recalibration mechanisms.Section 4.0 describes the implementation roadmap, outlining how the framework components will be developed, integrated, and validated over progressive phases.Section 5.0 presents the controlled pump-and-treat U-Net case study, connects the reported update results to all seven lifecycle steps, and identifies the evidence required beyond the present calibration and deployment hold.Section 6.0 discusses the measured findings, current limitations, remaining validation tasks, and conclusions. Appendix A provides the curated surrogate-literature table supporting Section 3.2.3.Framework Overview Three Pillars StructureThe proposed recalibration framework is organized around three core pillars that together provide the foundation for maintaining DL surrogate accuracy over operational timescales. These pillars function as management domains – organizational containers that hold and maintain specific concerns essential to the framework’s operation. Each pillar constitutes a distinct functional area with defined responsibilities: Pillar A establishes the rules and policies that govern operations, Pillar B manages the computational physics-based and surrogate models that generate predictions and ground truth, and Pillar C provides the adaptive monitoring and recalibration mechanism that monitor performance degradation and restore surrogate accuracy within accepted thresholds.The three pillars define what the framework manages, while the recalibration workflow described in Section 2.2 defines how these elements interact during operations. Each workflow step draws resources, methods, or constraints from one or more pillars, but no single pillar owns the workflow. This separation of concerns ensures that governance policies, modeling capabilities, and adaptive methods can evolve independently while remaining integrated through the operational workflow.Pillar A: Conceptual Design & GovernancePillar A serves as the design authority for the framework, defining the rules under which all operations proceed. This pillar establishes the objectives, performance thresholds, success metrics, and regulatory requirements that guide surrogate development, deployment, evaluation, and maintenance. It specifies the conditions and thresholds that initiate recalibration, defines what constitutes acceptable performance, and prescribes how decisions must be documented for regulatory compliance and independent review.The contents of Pillar A include trigger policies that specify criteria indicating when recalibration should be evaluated, including thresholds on predictive uncertainty, prediction–observation residual, and drift metrics. These policies define the conditions under which the framework transitions from routine monitoring to active evaluation of update needs. Pillar A also contains acceptance criteria – quantitative standards that define acceptable performance for operational deployment. These typically include error statistics at priority monitoring locations such as root mean square error (RMSE) targets, calibration of uncertainty estimates with prediction interval coverage probability, and spatiotemporal consistency with observed system behavior.Rollback procedures form another essential component of Pillar A, providing protocols for responding when an attempted surrogate update fails to meet acceptance criteria. As defined in Section 3.1.5, rollback in this framework doesn’t mean reversion to a prior surrogate version; instead, it means withdrawing the surrogate from operational use and escalating to full retraining. This distinction reflects the conservative approach required for high-consequence environmental applications. Audit requirements ensure that all recalibration decisions are traceable and defensible through documentation standards that include logging trigger rationale, data sources used, evaluation results, and verification outcomes, consistent with DOE quality assurance requirements.Data policies within Pillar A specify which measurements to assimilate, quality assurance and quality control standards, and data provenance requirements, ensuring that only validated data influence surrogate updates. Interpretability standards establish requirements for model explainability that support governance review and stakeholder confidence, following principles articulated in environmental modeling guidance. Success metrics provide alignment criteria that connect surrogate performance to DOE mission goals and site-specific compliance needs.Governance does not only constrain the framework – it defines what the other pillars do. Pillar A determines the trigger thresholds that Pillar C monitors, selects which learning strategies are appropriate for different situations, and decides whether results from Pillar B are acceptable for operational use. It is the design authority, not simply a set of guardrails.Pillar B: Modeling EnginesPillar B provides the computational foundation for the framework, managing the models that generate predictions and establish ground truth. This pillar comprises both the high-fidelity physics-based simulator that serves as the authoritative reference and the DL surrogate that enables fast operational inference.The physics simulator represents the high-fidelity numerical model – such as PFLOTRAN, MODFLOW, STOMP, or TOUGH2 – that serves as the truth generator for the framework. These simulators provide physically consistent predictions but require substantial computational resources, with individual runs potentially taking hours to days depending on model complexity. When surrogate correction is required, the physics model generates new training data at conditions where predictive degradation has been detected. All recalibration updates are driven by high-fidelity simulation outputs rather than extrapolation.The DL surrogate is the fast inference model trained to emulate the physics simulator’s input-output behavior. Architectures may include U-Net (encoder-decoder convolutional network, named for its U-shaped architecture), U-FNO (U-Net Fourier neural operator), long short-term memory (LSTM), or diffusion models, depending on the prediction task. Surrogates can generate predictions orders of magnitude faster than direct physics simulation, enabling real-time decision support. However, surrogates interpolate within their training domain and are subject to distribution drift when conditions evolve beyond their original training envelope.Pillar B also needs to maintain clear input-output contracts that define the interface between physics models and surrogates to ensure that data formats, coordinate systems, and variable definitions are consistent across model types. Versioned artifacts provide model version control and management systems that track surrogate versions, training configurations, and associated metadata, enabling reproducibility and supporting rollback procedures when needed. Training data archives serve as repositories of physics simulation outputs and field observations used for surrogate training, organized with provenance metadata to support audit requirements.The physics model remains the authoritative truth source throughout the framework’s operation. The surrogate is a sophisticated emulator that provides computational efficiency, but its validity is always assessed relative to physics-based predictions and field observations. When the surrogate predictions extend beyond the conditions represented in its training domain, the physics model provides the authoritative reference simulations needed to support correction and retraining.Pillar C: Recalibration MechanismsPillar C supplies the adaptive intelligence for the framework, managing the techniques used for monitoring surrogate performance, detecting degradation, and orchestrating corrections. This pillar comprises the methods library that the operational workflow draws upon to maintain surrogate accuracy over time.UQ methods within Pillar C provide techniques for estimating predictive uncertainty, including Monte Carlo dropout (MC-Dropout), deep ensembles, Bayesian neural networks, and conformal prediction. These methods provide the diagnostic self-awareness needed to identify when surrogate predictions become unreliable. Drift detection algorithms offer statistical methods for identifying performance degradation, including residual monitoring, uncertainty inflation tracking, distributional drift tests such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and representation-level drift detection in latent spaces. These algorithms implement the trigger logic that initiates governance review.Active learning strategies enable scenario selection methods that identify the most informative new physics simulations to run when correction is needed. Rather than random sampling, active learning targets conditions at decision boundaries, rare scenarios, or regions of high uncertainty where new data will maximally improve model performance. Continual learning methods provide techniques for updating the surrogate without full retraining, including elastic weight consolidation, experience replay, and layer freezing. These methods enable incremental updates that preserve previously learned knowledge while adapting to new conditions, thereby avoiding catastrophic forgetting.Multi-fidelity data fusion approaches combine information from multiple sources – field observations, physics model outputs at different resolutions, and surrogate predictions – to refine estimates and reduce uncertainty. Probability calibration encompasses post-hoc methods for ensuring that surrogate uncertainty estimates are well-calibrated, including temperature scaling, isotonic regression, and reliability diagram analysis. These techniques ensure that stated confidence intervals accurately reflect true prediction reliability.In general, Pillar C provides methods that the operational workflow uses. It serves as a toolkit from which workflow steps draw appropriate techniques based on the site situation. The workflow (Section 2.2) orchestrates when and how these methods are applied; Pillar C ensures the methods are available and properly maintained.Complementary Roles of the Three PillarsThe three pillars are co-essential to the framework’s operation, each playing a distinct and complementary role. Pillar A is the design authority: It defines the rules, sets the thresholds, and determines what constitutes acceptable performance. Without governance, the framework would lack the constraints needed for regulatory compliance and responsible deployment. Pillar B provides the computational foundation: It supplies the physics models that establish ground truth and the surrogates that enable fast inference. Without modeling engines, the framework would have nothing to maintain or correct. Pillar C supplies adaptive intelligence: It provides monitoring, detection, and correction methods that keep surrogates accurate over time. Without recalibration mechanisms, the framework could not adapt to changing conditions.No pillar should be viewed as more fundamental than the others; they are interdependent. Governance without models has nothing to govern; models without governance lack accountability; models without adaptation will drift; adaptation without governance may be inappropriate. The framework’s value emerges from their integration. Figure 1 illustrates the high-level architecture./Figure 1. Three complementary management domains. Phase 1, Continuous Monitoring, encompasses MONITOR, DETECT, and DECIDE. Phase 2, Triggered Response, encompasses PREPARE, UPDATE, CALIBRATE, and DEPLOY and begins when governance approves an update. The figure describes the intended operational workflow; the evidence status of its implementation is assessed in Section 5.0.This two-phase structure reflects a key principle from the governance framework described in Section 2.1.1: The presence of a recalibration trigger does not automatically mandate recalibration. Instead, the framework requires formal justification that an update is necessary, proportional, and expected to improve performance. The explicit DECIDE step (Step 3) implements this governance requirement. /Figure 2. Seven-step recalibration workflow. MONITOR, DETECT, and DECIDE form continuous monitoring; PREPARE, UPDATE, CALIBRATE, and DEPLOY form the triggered response. DECIDE is the human governance gate. A successful deployment returns to monitoring. Full retraining is a governed escalation path, not an additional lifecycle step.Phase 1: Continuous MonitoringPhase 1 runs continuously during site operations, providing ongoing assessment of surrogate performance without modifying the operational model. These steps implement the monitor-detect-decide logic that determines whether intervention is needed.Step 1: MONITOR The purpose of the MONITOR step is to track surrogate predictions against available monitoring data and compute diagnostic metrics. This step compares surrogate predictions to field monitoring data as observations become available, calculates prediction-observation residuals at priority monitoring locations, and computes uncertainty metrics using embedded UQ methods such as MC-Dropout or deep ensembles. The step also tracks prediction interval coverage and calibration statistics, logging all comparisons for trend analysis and audit purposes.The MONITOR step draws from Pillar A for data policies specifying which observations to assimilate, quality assurance and quality control standards for data validation, and specifications for what metrics to track. It draws from Pillar C for UQ methods used to compute predictive uncertainty and confidence intervals. The outputs of this step are time series of residuals, uncertainty metrics, and coverage statistics that feed into Step 2.Step 2: DETECTThe purpose of the DETECT step is to identify when surrogate performance has degraded beyond acceptable thresholds using automated diagnostics. This step applies drift detection algorithms to residual and uncertainty time series and compares current metrics against predefined trigger thresholds from Pillar A. The step evaluates multiple detection criteria, including predictive uncertainty exceeding confidence bounds, systematic bias in prediction-observation residuals, uncertainty inflation relative to historical baselines, distributional drift in inputs or outputs using tests such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and extrapolation beyond the original training domain.The DETECT step draws from Pillar A for trigger thresholds, persistence requirements (e.g., 60-90 days), and criteria for state transitions. It draws from Pillar C for drift detection algorithms, statistical tests, and resilience metrics. The outputs of this step are the current trigger state and an evidence package documenting the detection rationale.Step 3: DECIDEThe purpose of the DECIDE step is to conduct human governance review of detection evidence and formally determine whether an update is warranted. This step is critical because it implements the governance requirement that triggers do not automatically mandate recalibration. A human reviewer or governance committee, depending on site procedures, must evaluate the evidence and make a formal determination.The DECIDE step reviews the evidence package from Step 2, including residual trends and uncertainty metrics, drift detection results and statistical significance, documented operational changes such as new wells or pumping schedule changes, and comparison to historical performance patterns. The reviewer assesses whether the observed degradation is meaningful (large enough to affect decision quality), persistent (not a transient anomaly), and addressable (likely to be corrected by an incremental update). Based on this assessment, the step determines the appropriate response. If an update is warranted, the system proceeds to Phase 2. If an incremental update is unlikely to succeed, the system escalates directly to full retraining.When an update is approved, the DECIDE step also selects the update pathway. Pathway A, the observation-driven approach, is selected when recent field observations provide sufficient new information for fine-tuning. Pathway B, the simulation-driven approach, is selected when active learning should identify new physics simulations to generate targeted training data.The DECIDE step draws exclusively from Pillar A for governance policies, formal justification requirements, approval workflows, and documentation standards. The outputs of this step are a formal decision record documenting the determination, rationale, and selected pathway if an update is approved.Phase 2: Triggered ResponsePhase 2 is activated only when Step 3 (DECIDE) approves an update. These steps implement the prepare–update–calibrate–deploy sequence that modifies the operational surrogate.Step 4: PREPAREThe purpose of the PREPARE step is to assemble the dataset that will be used to update the surrogate. The specific activities depend on the pathway selected in Step 3.For Pathway A (observation-driven updates), the step compiles recent field observations that were not in the original training set, applies quality assurance and quality control procedures per Pillar A data policies, and formats data for compatibility with the surrogate training pipeline.For Pathway B (simulation-driven updates), the step generates a candidate pool of operationally relevant simulation scenarios and applies active learning algorithms to score candidates based on predictive uncertainty in the candidate region, residual patterns relative to recent observations, and diversity constraints covering spatial, temporal, and parameter dimensions. The step then selects 6 to 10 high-value scenarios that maximize expected information gain, executes targeted physics simulations for selected scenarios, and combines new simulation outputs with the replay buffer.The PREPARE step draws from Pillar A for data policies and quality assurance standards, from Pillar B for the physics simulator when executing new runs under Pathway B, and from Pillar C for active learning strategies used in scenario selection. The output of this step is a prepared dataset with provenance documentation. Step 5: UPDATEThe purpose of the UPDATE step is to fine-tune the DL surrogate using continual learning methods to incorporate new information while preserving previously learned knowledge. This step configures the continual learning approach based on the update context, selecting from methods such as elastic weight consolidation to penalize changes to weights important for previous tasks, experience replay to include historical samples in each training batch, and layer freezing to preserve learned representations in early encoder layers.This step sets conservative hyperparameters, including a small learning rate to prevent catastrophic forgetting, L2 regularization anchored to pre-update weights, and early stopping based on validation performance. The step then fine-tunes the prepared dataset while monitoring training for convergence and stability.A key principle of this step is that it performs an incremental update, not full retraining. The goal is to adapt the surrogate to new conditions while preserving its validity in previously learned domains. Full retraining is reserved for cases where incremental updates repeatedly fail. The UPDATE step draws from Pillar B for the surrogate model to be updated and training infrastructure, and from Pillar C for continual learning methods including elastic weight consolidation, replay, and layer freezing. The output of this step is an updated surrogate model in candidate status, not yet operational.Step 6: CALIBRATEWhereas the UPDATE step optimizes model weights for predictive accuracy, the CALIBRATE step addresses the statistical reliability of the model’s expressed uncertainty.The purpose of the CALIBRATE step is to perform post-hoc calibration of the updated model’s uncertainty estimates to ensure stated confidence intervals accurately reflect true prediction reliability. This step is separate from UPDATE because the constraints applied during continual learning can distort the relationship between ensemble spread and true predictive uncertainty. Probability calibration techniques are post-training activities that correct this distortion without modifying the model’s point predictions.CALIBRATE fits or assesses the uncertainty procedure using held-out calibration data that are separate from training and controller selection, then reports empirical coverage, interval width, and reliability across relevant regimes and locations. Calibration parameters and the applicable assumptions are archived. An untouched final test remains reserved for DEPLOY.The CALIBRATE step draws from Pillar A for acceptance criteria for uncertainty calibration such as coverage targets, and from Pillar C for probability calibration methods and reliability diagram analysis. The output of this step is a calibrated surrogate model with documented uncertainty characteristics.Step 7: DEPLOYThe purpose of the DEPLOY step is to validate the updated model against acceptance criteria and, if successful, promote it to operational status with full governance documentation.The validation phase evaluates the calibrated model on held-out test data not used in training or calibration. Performance is compared against predefined acceptance criteria from Pillar A, including error statistics at priority monitoring locations, uncertainty calibration metrics, spatial and temporal consistency, and performance relative to the pre-update baseline. All validation results are documented.If the model fails validation, the updated model is not deployed. The step assesses whether failure indicates (a) a need for a larger update batch, in which case the workflow returns to Step 4 with expanded scope; or (b) a fundamental domain shift requiring transition to the ESCALATE state for full retraining. Failure rationale and next steps are documented.If the model passes validation, the validated model is promoted to operational status. The previous operational model is archived but not deleted. Model version records in Pillar B are updated. The step generates comprehensive governance documentation, including trigger rationale from Step 2, decision justification from Step 3, data sources and provenance from Step 4, training configuration from Step 5, calibration parameters from Step 6, and validation results from Step 7. The trigger state machine returns to the OK state.DEPLOY draws from Pillar A for acceptance criteria, review authority, audit requirements, and withdrawal procedures, and from Pillar B for model versioning and artifact management. Its output is either an approved model with a complete record or a documented hold or escalation decision. Routine calculations may be automated, while intervention and promotion remain governed decisions.Update PathwaysThe framework supports two update pathways selected during Step 3 (DECIDE) based on the nature of the detected degradation. Both pathways proceed through the same Phase 2 sequence (Steps 4–7); the distinction lies in how Step 4 (PREPARE) assembles the update dataset.Pathway A (Observation-Driven Update) is appropriate when recent field observations provide sufficient new information to correct the surrogate without additional physics simulations. This pathway offers faster turnaround, because no physics simulation wait time is required, and lower computational cost. It is appropriate when degradation is due to gradual drift within the existing parameter space and relies on the assumption that field observations adequately represent the conditions causing degradation. The workflow proceeds from Steps 1–3 through Step 4 (compile observations) to Steps 5–7.Pathway B (Simulation-Driven Update) is appropriate when the surrogate has encountered conditions outside its training domain and targeted physics simulations are needed to generate appropriate training data. This pathway requires longer turnaround due to physics simulation execution and incurs higher computational cost. It is appropriate when degradation is due to operational changes such as new wells or changed boundary conditions, or regime shifts. Active learning ensures simulations target the most informative conditions. The workflow proceeds from Steps 1–3 through Step 4 (active learning plus physics runs) to Steps 5–7.The choice between pathways is a governance decision made in Step 3 based on the evidence and operational context.Pillar Integration Across the WorkflowA defining characteristic of the framework is that no single pillar owns the workflow – each step draws resources, constraints, or methods from one or more pillars, and the pattern of these dependencies shifts as the workflow moves from continuous monitoring into triggered response. The purpose of this governance pillar is to ensure that surrogate modeling is scientifically defensible, operationally stable, and aligned with existing regulatory and quality-assurance expectations, while allowing efficient scenario analysis and adaptive planning over multi-year remediation timelines.The governance framework is designed to be applicable across all EM programs and sites, and is grounded in established regulatory and quality standards, including EPA CERCLA guidance, DOE quality assurance orders, and EPA modeling and data quality manuals. Within this framework, surrogate models are treated as decision-support tools that primarily support screening-level analyses, planning, and scenario exploration, while high-fidelity physics-based numerical models remain the authoritative basis for final regulatory and remedy decisions.Regulatory Alignment and Quality Assurance FoundationsGovernance is anchored in federal environmental modeling and quality-assurance guidance. Under CERCLA, remedial decisions must remain protective of human health and the environment over long operational horizons, and analytical tools used to support these decisions must be transparent, defensible, and periodically evaluated (EPA 2001) . The framework adopts principles from EPA environmental modeling guidance (EPA 2009), which emphasizes clear documentation of model purpose, assumptions, uncertainty, validation, and limitations before models are relied on for decision support. These principles directly inform the governance requirements for surrogate use, update triggers, and performance acceptance.At the current prototyping and drafting stage, governance for surrogate modeling activities is aligned with DOE EM-QA-001 (DOE EM 2019), which incorporates ASME NQA-1 standards and applies them to environmental management activities, including software and modeling systems. DOE EM-QA-001 establishes a management system requiring defined acceptance criteria, performance monitoring, corrective action, and continuous improvement for analytical tools used in mission-critical applications.These regulatory and quality standards establish the expectation that surrogate models must be managed as controlled analytical software, with formal verification, validation, documentation, and configuration management processes that support regulatory confidence and long-term stewardship.Model Usage ScopeA central governance principle is the explicit definition of how surrogate models may be used within EM decision workflows. Surrogate models are intended primarily to support screening-level analyses, including rapid evaluation of alternative remediation scenarios, sensitivity analyses, uncertainty exploration, and preliminary planning studies. In these contexts, surrogates provide substantial value by enabling fast exploration of large scenario spaces that would be impractical using high-fidelity numerical models alone.For formal regulatory or final remedy decisions, surrogate model outputs are not used as standalone evidence. Instead, surrogate results may inform and prioritize analyses, guide the selection of scenarios for further evaluation, or support interpretation of system behavior, while final decisions remain grounded in high-fidelity physics-based modeling and empirical data. This distinction aligns with EPA remedial investigation and feasibility study guidance (EPA 1989), which emphasizes that models must be used in a manner consistent with their purpose and limitationsGovernance therefore ensures that surrogate models function as advisory, efficiency-enhancing tools, rather than replacements for established numerical simulators. Any application of surrogate outputs beyond screening-level support requires explicit documentation of assumptions, uncertainty, and corroboration with physics-based results, consistent with EPA modeling guidance and DOE quality expectations.Acceptance Criteria and Recalibration TriggersThe governance framework establishes predefined acceptance criteria and recalibration triggers that determine when surrogate models are considered reliable and when updates are warranted. These criteria are defined prior to deployment and are based on project-specific data quality objectives and decision needs, consistent with EPA quality assurance guidance for modeling applications (EPA 2002).Acceptance criteria define the quantitative performance standards a surrogate model must satisfy to be considered fit for operational use. They represent the bar the model must clear before deployment and after updates. Typical acceptance criteria include error thresholds at priority monitoring locations (for example, RMSE below a site-specific limit), calibration of uncertainty estimates such that prediction interval coverage probability remains near 90%, spatial and temporal consistency between surrogate predictions and physics-model reference simulations, and absence of systematic bias in predicted concentration trends at compliance boundaries. Acceptance criteria are evaluated during initial deployment validation (Step 7 of the workflow) and again after every recalibration cycle to confirm that the updated surrogate remains fit for purpose.Recalibration triggers are defined to detect meaningful degradation in surrogate performance or applicability, including sustained increases in prediction residuals, inflation or miscalibration of uncertainty estimates, and documented changes in boundary conditions or operational regimes that move the system outside the surrogate’s original training domain. These triggers are evaluated using objective, documented thresholds rather than ad hoc judgment, consistent with DOE quality assurance requirements for corrective action when performance expectations are not met (DOE EM 2019). Importantly, the presence of a recalibration trigger does not automatically mandate recalibration. Instead, governance requires formal justification that an update is necessary, proportional, and expected to improve performance. This conservative approach aligns with best practices for model lifecycle management and reduces the risk of destabilizing otherwise reliable surrogate model behavior.Operational Monitoring and Review ScheduleThe governance framework defines a structured but adaptable monitoring schedule for surrogate model oversight (Table 2). Table 2. All surrogate model versions are subject to formal configuration control, including version tracking, documentation of training data and assumptions, and preservation of prior approved models.Each recalibration or significant update produces a documented governance record that includes the trigger rationale, data sources used, evaluation against acceptance criteria, and verification results. Rollback is invoked when an attempted incremental update fails to meet predefined acceptance criteria, indicating that the surrogate’s training domain or structure is no longer scientifically valid.Under a rollback condition, neither the updated surrogate nor the prior surrogate is permitted for continued screening or planning analyses, and surrogate-based decision support is therefore explicitly suspended. In addition, analytical support reverts exclusively to the high-fidelity physics-based numerical model, and rollback automatically triggers escalation to a governed full retraining process. Full retraining develops a new surrogate baseline from scratch using updated physics-model ensembles, revised assumptions, and refreshed validation datasets. It should be conducted under configuration control and subject to the same verification, validation, uncertainty calibration, and acceptance criteria as an initial deployment. The retrained surrogate is promoted to operational use only after all governance requirements are satisfied.This definition of rollback reflects DOE expectations for conservative software lifecycle management in high-consequence environmental applications. Incremental updates are preferred when scientifically defensible but continued use of any surrogate is not permitted once validity is in question. By redefining rollback as withdrawal and retraining rather than version reversion, the framework prevents unmanaged risk and preserves regulatory confidence in long-term remediation planning.Governance Essentials SummaryIn summary, the governance framework defined in this section ensures that DL surrogate models are integrated into EM groundwater remediation workflows in a manner that is controlled, transparent, and regulator-aligned. Governance is grounded in established EPA and DOE quality assurance standards, clearly limits surrogate use to screening- and planning-level decision support, and preserves the primacy of high-fidelity physics-based numerical models for final regulatory and remedy decisions. Objective acceptance criteria, documented recalibration triggers, and formal configuration control ensure that surrogate models are used only within their validated domain. When surrogate validity is in question, governance procedures require explicit withdrawal of surrogate use and escalation to a controlled full retraining process, preventing unmanaged risk. Collectively, this governance foundation enables efficient use of advanced modeling tools to support long-term remediation planning while maintaining scientific rigor, operational stability, and regulatory confidence.Modeling Engines This section describes the physics-based numerical models and DL surrogates that form Pillar B of the recalibration framework. Physics models provide high-fidelity reference simulations, while surrogates provide fast inference for screening and scenario analysis.Site Challenges and Modeling NeedsNumerical groundwater and reactive transport models provide the high‑fidelity representation of site‑specific subsurface processes that underpin Pillar B. At EM sites, these models describe how contaminants move and transform in complex hydrogeologic settings, and how engineered controls, such as pump‑and‑treat systems, can influence plume evolution. The physical phenomena represented typically include advection and dispersion of dissolved species, sorption onto solid phases, and associated reaction processes (Šimůnek and van Genuchten 2016; Sethi and Di Molfetta 2019), and non-Fickian plume spreading arising from heterogeneity and diffusion-controlled mass exchange between transmissive and low-permeability zones (Guo et al. 2021). Back diffusion from fine grained or low-permeability units is recognized as a major control on plume persistence after source removal (Yang et al. 2016, 2017). Density-driven flow occurs in systems impacted by dense nonaqueous phase liquid / light nonaqueous phase liquid (Huling 1991; McCaulou 1995; Newell 1995) or by seawater intrusion (Werner et al. 2013; Xu et al. 2019). Engineered operations – pump and treat (P&T) wells, injection/extraction networks, permeable reactive barriers, and hydraulic barriers – must also be represented, enabling planners to evaluate how alternative configurations and engineered controls affect plume pathways and concentrations at compliance points. Physics-Based Numerical ModelsNumerical models are indispensable for EM stewardship because the central questions are inherently predictive and counterfactual: Decision-makers need to understand how plumes will evolve over decades under alternative remediation strategies, monitoring designs, and future forcing scenarios (e.g., pumping schedules, land use change, climate variability) questions that observation alone cannot resolve (Neuman et al. 2003). Analytical approximations cannot capture the coupled effects of aquifer heterogeneity, complex geochemistry, and engineered infrastructure (Bear and Cheng 2010; Zheng and Wang 1999). High-fidelity simulators fill this gap by enabling systematic exploration of scenarios and assumptions, quantifying potential outcomes before implementation, and supporting design and regulatory decisions with transparent, physics-based predictions. Simulator selection is governed by dominant physics at a given site.Translating these simulators into credible site predictions requires calibration: Hydraulic conductivities, recharge rates, boundary conditions, and transport parameters must be adjusted until simulated heads and concentrations adequately reproduce field observations. This has traditionally relied on a blend of expert judgment and automated inverse methods such as PEST or the Design Analysis Kit for Optimization and Terascale Applications (DAKOTA) (Adams et al. 2020; Doherty 1994; Hill and Tiedeman 2007), while sites with dense time-series records increasingly employ ensemble-based data assimilation (DA) – most notably the ensemble Kalman filter – to update model parameters and states in a statistically coherent framework (Evensen 2009; Hendricks Franssen and Kinzelbach 2008). Regardless of the technique, every calibration cycle demands repeated forward simulations and careful tuning, making the process labor-intensive and computationally expensive, with costs that compound each time new monitoring data arrive and the model must be updated.Despite their fidelity, these simulators impose significant computational constraints. Realistic EM applications typically involve three-dimensional (3D), heterogeneous domains, long simulation horizons, and tight numerical tolerances to preserve mass balance and stability, so individual forward runs can take from hours to days on standard computing resources (Hammond et al. 2014; Maxwell et al. 2015). Many of the planning tasks, however, require ensembles of simulations, Monte Carlo realizations of hydrogeologic variability, scenario sweeps over remediation designs, or sensitivity analyses with respect to uncertain parameters (Rubin 2003). The number of model evaluations needed for robust decision support can easily reach the hundreds or thousands. As a result, it became impractical for planners to interactively explore design space or to fully propagate uncertainty using only the high-fidelity model for large complex sites. These limitations motivate the data-driven surrogate modeling engines that sit on top of the numerical model.Surrogate Model LandscapePublished groundwater and subsurface applications demonstrate the breadth of DL surrogate methods and their potential to accelerate numerical-model predictions (Luo et al. 2023; Xu and Zhang 2024). Convolutions preserve interfaces, sharp fronts, and channelized structures, making these networks well suited for high-resolution spatial prediction. Representative applications include attention U-Net architectures for steady-state hydraulic head fields (Taccari et al. 2022), image-to-image regression for multiphase flow (Mo et al. 2019b), autoregressive residual U-Net for dynamic multiphase flow and solute transport (Jiang et al. 2021), and U-Net surrogates for transient groundwater flow inverse problems (Lauzon 2024) and P&T optimization (Song et al. 2025).Family 2: Operator-Learning ModelsArchitectures such as Fourier neural operator (FNO), U-FNO, and deep operator network (DeepONet) learn the solution operator of a partial differential equation in function space rather than at fixed grid points, yielding surrogates that can be evaluated across discretizations, subject to architecture and training-domain limits (Li et al. 2020). Notable subsurface applications include U-FNO for CO2-water multiphase flow (Wen et al. 2022), DeepONet for confined aquifer systems (Taccari et al. 2024), the GeoFUSE framework for seawater intrusion achieving approximately 10⁵ speedup per inference relative to PFLOTRAN (Jiang et al. 2025), and physics-informed U-FNO for climate stress testing at the Savannah River Site (Wang et al. 2022).Family 3: Sequence ModelsLSTM, gated recurrent unit (GRU), and Transformer variants are designed for low-dimensional time-series outputs such as breakthrough curves and well responses monitored at high frequency. These architectures capture long-range temporal dependencies essential for forecasting contaminant evolution and groundwater level trends. Applications include GRU-based surrogates for groundwater levels and water quality (Chen et al. 2021), encoder-decoder LSTM for contaminant source identification (Anshuman and Eldho 2023), and DSCNN (deep separable convolutional neural network)-GRU surrogates for transient groundwater head fields (Li et al. 2025).Family 4: Generative ModelsVariational autoencoders, generative adversarial networks, and diffusion models can represent ensembles of states or parameter fields for probabilistic inversion and uncertainty quantification. Representative applications include Bayesian inversion of generative models for CO₂ storage (Graham and Chen 2020), diffusion models for multivariate subsurface property fields (Miele and Linde 2025), pretrained diffusion for subsurface flow (Wang et al. 2025b), and conditional diffusion for geological CO₂ storage prediction (Wang et al. 2025a). Physical and observational consistency depend on the model construction and validation.Family 5: Classical Regression Surrogates Classical regression and statistical surrogates including Gaussian processes, Kriging, and shallow artificial neural networks remain powerful for low-dimensional mappings from design variables to scalar metrics such as total cost, cleanup time, or risk index. These methods are especially valuable when training data are limited and interpretability is important. The literature includes comprehensive reviews of surrogate models in groundwater applications (Asher et al. 2015), reduced-dimensional Gaussian process regression for groundwater allocation (Siade et al. 2020), Gaussian process surrogates for surfactant-enhanced aquifer remediation (Shams et al. 2021), and advances in Gaussian process surrogate construction and validation (Marrel and Iooss 2024).Cross-Cutting PatternsFigure 6 summarizes the 45 retained entries by primary family, application, and project domain. Training datasets are generated by sampling parameter and boundary condition ranges, commonly using Latin hypercube or quasi-random designs, and running the corresponding high-fidelity simulations to produce input-output pairs (Asher et al. 2015; Razavi et al. 2012). The number of simulations required varies by problem complexity and surrogate architecture but generally falls in the range of hundreds to low thousands of realizations (Mo et al. 2019b; Wen et al. 2022; Zhu and Zabaras 2018). Input fields that span orders of magnitude, such as hydraulic conductivity, are typically log-transformed prior to training (Tang et al. 2020), and all features are standardized to stabilize optimization and ensure consistent inference (Mo et al. 2019b). Hold-out realizations spanning distinct parameter ranges or time windows are reserved for testing to guard against overfitting (Jiang et al. 2025; Wen et al. 2022). A growing number of studies incorporate physics-informed loss terms, embedding partial differential equation residuals or conservation constraints alongside data-driven objectives to improve generalization and physical consistency, particularly in sparse-data or extrapolation regimes (Xu and Zhang 2024; Zhu et al. 2019). For transient applications, autoregressive rollout strategies feed the surrogate’s own predictions back as inputs for subsequent time steps, requiring careful attention to error accumulation during both training and validation (Jiang et al. 2021). Most studies report runtime comparisons between the full simulator and the trained surrogate alongside accuracy metrics to quantify practical speedup (Taccari et al. 2022).Modeling Engine SummaryThe modeling engine layer described in this section links site-specific physics to fast surrogate inference. Site challenges and modeling needs (Section 3.2.1) establish the subsurface processes that must be captured, including advection, dispersion, sorption, back diffusion, density-driven flow, and engineered operations. Physics-based numerical models (Section 3.2.2) encode these processes in high-fidelity simulators that serve as the authoritative reference for Pillar B, but impose computational costs that preclude interactive scenario exploration, particularly for large, complex sites where robust decision support may require hundreds to thousands of forward evaluations. The surrogate model landscape (Section 3.2.3) addresses this bottleneck through five families of data-driven emulators, each matched to a distinct input-output structure and decision use-case, with cross-cutting training practices drawn from the reviewed literature. Together, the numerical simulators supply training data, physical grounding, and validation targets, while the surrogates reproduce key results at a fraction of the computational cost. The recalibration mechanisms described in Section 3.3 govern how these surrogates are kept aligned with evolving site conditions and new observations over time.Recalibration Mechanisms The recalibration component operates as a single, governed loop designed for stability, transparency, and regulatory credibility. During routine operations, the surrogate remains unchanged; the team conducts monthly monitoring to track accuracy relative to the most recent stable baseline and calibration of stated uncertainty. Operational changes (e.g., revisions to pumping schedules or boundary conditions) are logged because they can move inputs outside the surrogate’s original training setting. On a fixed decision window (e.g., quarterly), the program determines whether an update is justified. Action is taken only when evidence of drift has persisted for 60–90 days or when a documented regime change has occurred. When action is justified, the framework does not undertake a whole retrain; instead, it executes a small, targeted update supported by a limited number of physics simulations and the most recent field observations. The revised surrogate is then evaluated against pre-registered acceptance criteria, including accuracy, uncertainty coverage, and performance at priority wells or zones. Throughout, the high-fidelity physics model remains the authoritative reference used to inform and validate changes.Uncertainty Quantification of Surrogate Models Uncertainty quantification supports evaluation of predictive reliability in both surrogate modeling and data assimilation. A trigger logic framework activates model recalibration to maintain model trustworthiness and regulatory compliance. The framework combines statistical thresholds with domain-informed criteria to support adaptive learning while maintaining model interpretability and performance.The drift monitoring framework consists of multiple complementary metrics organized into four categories (Table 6). By combining complementary signals that capture loss of dynamical stability, increasing model uncertainty, and structural changes in data and learned representations, the framework allows early, interpretable, and statistically robust detection of regime drift to support timely model recalibration.Category 1: Tipping Point-Inspired Resilience MetricsThese metrics capture early signs of loss of dynamical stability, motivated by concepts from tipping point science. They are applied to model outputs, residuals, and latent representations, without implying the existence of physical tipping points in the underlying system. Rolling-window estimates of variance and lag-1 autocorrelation are used to detect increasing fluctuation amplitude and memory, consistent with reduced resilience. Higher-order moments, including skewness and kurtosis, are monitored to identify asymmetric error growth and heavy-tailed behavior indicative of excursions toward unstable regions. Category 2: Performance and Uncertainty-Based MetricsThese metrics focus on direct indicators of model reliability under deployment. Prediction residuals are monitored using adaptive thresholds derived from rolling statistics, such as exceedance of multiples of the rolling standard deviation. Persistent threshold violations or clustered spatial–temporal error patterns are treated as evidence of localized or systemic degradation.For probabilistic models, predictive uncertainty is explicitly tracked using measures such as variance or entropy. Sustained increases relative to historical baselines are interpreted as growing epistemic uncertainty and declining confidence in model predictions. Category 3: Distributional and Representation-Level Drift DetectionThese diagnostics target statistical and structural changes in data and learned features that may not immediately manifest in errors or uncertainty. Distributional drift is assessed by comparing incoming input and output distributions to reference distributions using nonparametric statistical tests, such as the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Statistically significant deviations indicate changes in the data-generating process that may compromise model validity.In parallel, drift indicators are applied in learned representation spaces, such as latent embeddings from diffusion models or bottleneck features from convolutional architectures. Deviations from the training manifold in latent space provide early detection of regime mismatch in high-dimensional settings where marginal statistics may be insufficient.Category 4: Distributional and Representation-Level Drift DetectionThe final category integrates all diagnostic signals into a robust decision-making layer. Rather than relying on single-metric thresholds, the framework employs Bayesian inference to combine evidence from resilience, performance, uncertainty, and distributional indicators with prior knowledge and alternative explanations (e.g., data quality changes).Model recalibration or adaptation is triggered through composite gating mechanisms, implemented either as Boolean rules (requiring concurrent exceedance across multiple metric families) or as score-based aggregation into a unified drift risk index. This integration reduces false positives due to noise or transient anomalies and ensures that interventions are driven by consistent, multi-source evidence.Table 6. The framework employs a conservative, stability-focused approach that fits DOE operational requirements.Mature continual learning approaches fall into two widely adopted categories: (1) regularization-based techniques, which constrain parameter updates to avoid overwriting previously important knowledge, and (2) replay-based techniques, which maintain stability by including a curated set of historical samples during updating. Elastic weight consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017) is a prototypical regularization strategy, penalizing deviations from weights identified as influential in previous training. Replay-based strategies are widely applied across engineering and environmental forecasting domains to reduce the risk of catastrophic forgetting when conditions shift incrementally (De Lange et al. 2021). Continual-learning reviews describe opportunities and limitations in predictive maintenance and energy-management applications (Hurtado et al. 2023; Sayed et al. 2025). Groundwater surrogate reviews and active-learning studies provide complementary context for model refinement (Asher et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2023). These sources motivate evaluating update strategies; they do not establish that a particular replay ratio, update schedule, or computational saving transfers to groundwater plume prediction. Section 5.0 provides the report-specific update evidence.Fine-tuning is initiated only after documented indicators of drift demonstrate meaningful degradation – such as sustained increases in predictive error, divergence between modeled and observed temporal trends, or reduced empirical coverage of uncertainty intervals (e.g., under coverage in UQ metrics). Once triggered, the update process proceeds through several structured steps. Step 1: Assemble Update Dataset. Assemble a traceable update dataset from relevant new simulations or validated observations and historical replay. Approximately 70–75% historical replay is the framework’s illustrative starting guidance, not a universal optimum or a verified composition for the present study. The ratio should be assessed against paired historical/new validation outcomes; replay itself is supported by the continual-learning literature (De Lange et al. 2021).Step 2: Selective Layer Updates. Early encoder layers are frozen to preserve structural representations learned during original training, while later layers are updated using a reduced learning rate and a regularization penalty. The penalty may take the form of elastic weight consolidation, which protects weights identified as influential during prior training (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017), or L2 anchoring toward the pre-update parameter values, following recommendations from continual learning studies in dynamic engineering systems (Hurtado et al. 2023).Step 3: Validation and Early Stopping. Validation performance is monitored on a held-out set that includes both recent observations and replayed historical cases. Metrics tracked include RMSE at priority wells, empirical coverage of predictive intervals, and spatial error patterns across the model domain. Training is stopped once improvements plateau over successive evaluation cycles to prevent overfitting to the update batch (Sayed et al. 2025).Step 4: Uncertainty Recalibration. Recalibrate predictive intervals using independent held-out data and evaluate empirical coverage and interval width. Deep ensembles or other uncertainty estimators supply candidate predictive distributions; calibration and final testing remain distinct tasks. Apply the surrogate-level UQ methods in Section 3.3.1 under their stated assumptions (Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017; Zhu and Zabaras 2018).Step 5: Governance Review. Prepare a record of pre- and post-update performance, trigger evidence, data provenance, and uncertainty diagnostics under Section 3.1. Promote the candidate only after all applicable acceptance conditions are met. A failed candidate remains unapproved; when the incumbent’s operational validity is in question, surrogate use is withdrawn and the governed retraining policy in Section 3.1.5 applies. Archiving a prior model does not authorize its continued use.Active Learning Active learning identifies new physics simulations that provide the highest expected improvement in surrogate accuracy, stability, or uncertainty reduction. These approaches are interpretable and well-aligned with DOE operational requirements, where simulation budgets are limited and every model run must be justified.Three complementary strategies guide the selection of new simulations (Table 7).Table 7. In groundwater flow and reactive transport applications, fidelity levels correspond to differences in grid resolution, process representation, or data source. Low-fidelity sources – such as coarse-grid PFLOTRAN or MODFLOW simulations with simplified boundary conditions or reduced-process representations – are inexpensive to generate and provide broad characterization of system variability across the parameter and operational space. Highfidelity sources – including fine-grid simulations with full reactive transport chemistry, locally refined meshes around critical features such as source zones or compliance boundaries, and validated field observations – are computationally expensive or limited in number but anchor predictions to physically accurate behavior where precise results are required. Combining low-fidelity ensembles with targeted high-fidelity data can reduce data-generation cost when the fidelity relationship is informative. The achieved accuracy and savings depend on the fidelity gap, validation design, and computational budget and must be measured for the application. Low-fidelity runs can be used to rapidly screen candidate regions before committing expensive high-fidelity resources to the most informative configurations. The fused surrogate provides improved baseline predictions that enhance drift detection sensitivity and reduce false-positive recalibration triggers, supporting the conservative update philosophy established in the governance framework (Section 3.1).Full RetrainingFull retraining constructs a new surrogate baseline from scratch when the existing model is no longer representative of the physical system or operational domain. Whereas continual learning (Section 3.3.3) applies targeted, incremental corrections to an otherwise valid surrogate, full retraining acknowledges that the original training domain has been fundamentally superseded and that no sequence of fine-tuning updates can restore reliable performance. This mechanism serves as the final safeguard in the recalibration framework, providing a clean, traceable reset that aligns with DOE expectations for lifecycle documentation and reproducibility in high-consequence environmental applications.Full retraining is warranted when the drift diagnostics and trigger logic described in Section 3.3.2 indicate that incremental updates can no longer restore surrogate performance, or when external changes to the modeling system itself render the original training domain obsolete. Specific conditions include fundamental regime shifts or major forcing changes that move the system outside the original training domain, redefinition of the underlying physics model through revised geologic interpretations or updated reaction networks, major reconfiguration of pumping infrastructure or boundary conditions, recalibration of the numerical model after incorporation of new hydrogeologic data, and repeated fine-tuning failures as described in Section 3.3.3. The concept of drift literature reinforces that full retraining is appropriate when foundational assumptions underlying the surrogate no longer hold.The retraining process proceeds through a formal sequence of steps consistent with the structured workflows established in Sections 3.3.3 through 3.3.5.Step 1: Verify Retraining Triggers. Verify that one or more of the conditions listed above has been documented through the governance and monitoring procedures described in Sections 3.1 and 3.3.2. This step ensures that full retraining is initiated based on objective evidence rather than precautionary judgment alone.Step 2: Redesign the Training Domain. Define updated ranges of hydrofacies, operational scenarios, and boundary forcings, and parameter distributions consistent with the new conceptual understanding. This redesign reflects the changed system state and any revisions to the underlying physics model, ensuring that the new training ensemble spans the conditions the surrogate will encounter during its next operational period.Step 3: Ensemble Generation. Generate a new ensemble of high-fidelity simulations covering the redesigned training domain. Where appropriate, supplement with low-fidelity runs using the multi-fidelity strategies described in Section 3.3.5 to maintain broad parameter-space coverage while managing computational cost. Recent field observations are integrated to anchor the surrogate to current empirical system behavior.Step 4: Surrogate Training and Uncertainty Calibration. Train a new surrogate from scratch, applying the architecture selection, preprocessing, and training practices described in Section 3.2.3. UQ is calibrated using the methods described in Section 3.3.1 to ensure that predictive intervals are well calibrated from initial deployment.Step 5: Validation and Acceptance. Validate the retrained surrogate against withheld high-fidelity simulations and independent observational periods. Performance is evaluated against the predefined acceptance criteria established in Section 3.1.3, including accuracy at priority wells, empirical coverage of uncertainty intervals, and consistency with known physical behavior. The retrained surrogate is promoted to operational use only after all acceptance criteria are satisfied.Full retraining is conducted under the configuration control, acceptance criteria, and archival procedures defined in Sections 3.1.3 and 3.1.5. The retraining governance package follows the same verification, validation, and acceptance review requirements as an initial deployment, with the additional requirement of documenting the specific trigger rationale and archiving the retired surrogate and all associated artifacts.Implementation RoadmapThis section describes how the three-pillar framework (Section 2.1) and seven-step workflow (Section 2.2) translate into an operational system. Rather than prescribing specific software tools or platforms, we focus on what capabilities are needed and how they should be developed over time. The goal is to provide a roadmap that can guide implementation while remaining flexible to site-specific constraints and evolving technology.The implementation must satisfy two demands. First, it must support the scientific workflow of monitoring, detecting drift, and executing recalibration when needed. Second, it must meet DOE governance requirements for traceability, reproducibility, and quality assurance. The design outlined here attempts to balance both demands while maintaining consistency with the framework architecture established in Section 2.0.System RequirementsOperational ModesThe system operates in two distinct modes – offline and online – that place different demands on the architecture and reflect different phases of the surrogate lifecycle.Offline mode encompasses initial surrogate development: generating training ensembles with the physics model, training the surrogate, calibrating uncertainty estimates, and validating against held-out data. This phase is computationally intensive, potentially requiring thousands of physics runs and graphics processing unit (GPU)-hours of training, but is not time-critical. Offline work typically occurs on high-performance computing (HPC) resources with batch scheduling. In terms of the framework, offline mode establishes the initial Pillar B modeling engines before operational deployment. The methods described in Section 3.2 (physics-based models and surrogate architectures) and Section 3.3.1 (UQ) are applied during this phase to produce a validated surrogate ready for deployment.Online mode is the operational phase where the trained surrogate supports decision-making. The surrogate generates predictions on demand, the monitoring system tracks performance through the seven-step workflow, and recalibration is triggered when needed. Online operations require faster turnaround –  predictions in seconds to minutes, diagnostic updates on a regular schedule such as daily or weekly, and recalibration decisions on a governed timeline such as quarterly review. Online components may run on more modest computing resources, though recalibration itself may require temporary access to HPC for new physics runs. The methods from Section 3.3.2 (trigger logic) and Section 3.1 (governance) are continuously active during online operations.The architecture must support smooth transitions between modes. A surrogate developed offline must be deployable online without modification. Data collected during online operations must feed back into offline analysis when full retraining is needed. This bidirectional flow ensures that operational experience improves subsequent surrogate generations.Pillar-Based CapabilitiesEach pillar of the framework requires specific capabilities for implementation. Understanding these requirements helps ensure that the system architecture supports all aspects of the recalibration framework.Pillar A (Governance) requires infrastructure for configuration management that tracks all surrogate versions with their training data, assumptions, and validation results. The system must maintain audit logging for all decisions, particularly at the DECIDE step, where human judgment authorizes recalibration. Documentation generation capabilities must produce regulatory-compliant records that satisfy DOE quality assurance requirements. Human review interfaces must present diagnostic evidence clearly so that decision-makers can evaluate whether recalibration is warranted. The governance methods detailed in Section 3.1 – including regulatory framework alignment, acceptance criteria, monitoring schedules, and configuration control – define what this infrastructure must support.Pillar B (Modeling Engines) requires a physics model interface capable of communicating with high-fidelity simulators such as PFLOTRAN, MODFLOW, STOMP, or TOUGH2. This interface handles input file generation, job submission including HPC scheduling where needed, output parsing, and result archiving. The system must also provide surrogate training and inference infrastructure that can train neural network architectures (such as those described in Section 3.2.3), store trained models with version control, and generate predictions efficiently during operations. Data management capabilities must handle both simulation outputs and field observations, maintaining provenance for all data used in training or validation.Pillar C (Recalibration Mechanisms) requires infrastructure for UQ computation using the methods described in Section 3.3.1, including MC-Dropout, deep ensembles, or conformal prediction. Drift detection and monitoring capabilities must implement the trigger logic from Section 3.3.2, tracking performance metrics and evaluating drift indicators against governance-defined thresholds. When recalibration is triggered, the system must support active learning for scenario selection (Section 3.3.4) and continual learning for model updates (Section 3.3.3). Multi-fidelity data fusion (Section 3.3.5) and full retraining (Section 3.3.6) capabilities complete the recalibration toolkit.Workflow SupportThe implementation must support both phases of the seven-step workflow defined in Section 2.2.Phase 1 (Continuous Monitoring) implements Steps 1 through 3 of the workflow. The MONITOR step requires automated comparison of surrogate predictions against field observations, computing residuals and tracking uncertainty metrics over time. The DETECT step evaluates drift indicators against predefined thresholds, distinguishing transient anomalies from persistent degradation using the persistence filtering described in Section 3.3.2. The DECIDE step packages diagnostic evidence for governance review and records the authorization decision. Throughout Phase 1, the system maintains the trigger state logic – OK, WARNING, UPDATE, or ESCALATE – that guides operational decisions.Phase 2 (Triggered Response) activates only when the DECIDE step authorizes an update, implementing Steps 4 through 7. The PREPARE step assembles training data, potentially using active learning to select high-value scenarios for new physics simulations. The UPDATE step fine-tunes surrogate weights using continual learning techniques that preserve previously learned knowledge. The CALIBRATE step ensures that uncertainty estimates remain well-calibrated after the update. The DEPLOY step validates the updated surrogate against acceptance criteria and, if successful, deploys it with full documentation. If validation fails, the system supports rollback procedures as defined in Section 3.1.5.The methods detailed in Section 3.0 provide the technical foundation for all workflow steps. Rather than re-explaining these methods here, the implementation draws on them as needed: Section 3.3.1 for UQ, Section 3.3.2 for trigger logic, Section 3.3.3 for continual learning, Section 3.3.4 for active learning, Section 3.3.5 for multi-fidelity fusion, and Section 3.3.6 for full retraining when incremental updates are insufficient.Human Decision PointsNot everything should be automated. The framework includes explicit decision points where human judgment is required, reflecting Pillar A’s emphasis on governance and accountability. The software architecture should make these decision points visible and provide the information needed to make good decisions.Recalibration ApprovalThe most important decision point occurs at Step 3 (DECIDE) of the workflow. Even when automated diagnostics indicate drift, the decision to update a surrogate should involve human review. This is both a governance requirement under DOE quality assurance standards and a practical safeguard against false alarms. The monitoring system provides the evidence – drift metrics, historical trends, uncertainty inflation, and comparison to acceptance criteria – but humans decide whether to proceed with recalibration.The decision options at this point include approving the update and proceeding to Phase 2, deferring pending additional evidence while continuing intensified monitoring, or rejecting with documented justification if the evidence is insufficient or the timing is inappropriate. Each decision must be recorded with rationale for regulatory transparency.Acceptance Criteria DefinitionAcceptance criteria define what “good enough” means for a given application – the error thresholds, uncertainty coverage requirements, and performance metrics that determine whether a surrogate is fit for purpose. Setting these criteria requires domain expertise that cannot be automated. Humans must understand site-specific risks, regulatory context, and the consequences of prediction errors to define appropriate thresholds.The system evaluates surrogate performance against these criteria, but the criteria themselves emerge from human judgment informed by site characterization, regulatory requirements, and operational experience. Section 3.1.3 describes the types of acceptance criteria (accuracy metrics, uncertainty coverage, spatial and temporal consistency), but the specific threshold values must be determined by qualified personnel for each application.Rollback DecisionsWhen an updated surrogate fails validation at Step 7 (DEPLOY), a rollback decision is required. As defined in Section 3.1.5, rollback means withdrawing the surrogate from operational use and reverting to physics-only analysis – not reverting to a previous surrogate version, since that version has already been flagged as degraded. This is a significant operational change that requires human authorization and documentation.The decision to roll back acknowledges that the recalibration attempt was unsuccessful and that the surrogate cannot currently meet acceptance criteria. This may trigger retraining or, in some cases, a fundamental reassessment of whether a surrogate approach is appropriate for the application.Full Retraining TriggersThe most consequential decision arises when incremental updates repeatedly fail or when site conditions change fundamentally. The decision to abandon the current surrogate and retrain from scratch involves judging whether the surrogate’s foundational assumptions remain valid. Perhaps the training distribution no longer represents current site conditions, or perhaps the surrogate architecture is inadequate for the prediction task.Full retraining has major resource implications – potentially thousands of new physics simulations and significant computational effort – and should not be automated. Section 3.3.6 describes the technical process for full retraining, but the decision to initiate it requires human assessment of whether the investment is justified and whether the underlying approach remains sound.Development PhasesImplementing the full framework is a multi-year effort. A phased approach delivers value incrementally while building toward the complete vision. Each phase builds on the previous one, with lessons learned informing subsequent development.Phase 1: Prototype DemonstrationThe first development phase aims to complete a reproducible lifecycle demonstration on a controlled test case. Section 5.0 supplies the current offline U-Net update evidence; prospective detector evaluation, independent uncertainty calibration, and final-test promotion are the remaining milestones.The current prototype uses the pump-and-treat U-Net trained on synthetic physics-model outputs. The next increment will preserve trajectory identity, resolve the time axis and sampling protocol, freeze detection and acceptance criteria, and compare candidate updates under a common evaluation design. It will then calibrate predictive uncertainty and evaluate an untouched final test. These steps can be executed manually with reproducible scripts and an explicit reviewer record before a persistent monitoring service is developed.The next deliverable is a reproducible evidence package containing executed configurations, split manifests, predictions, detector diagnostics, update comparisons, calibration outputs, and the final decision. A candidate that fails a gate remains unapproved. Completion is defined by a documented and scientifically valid evaluation, not by assuming that every candidate must pass.Phase 1 success requires traceability across MONITOR → DETECT → DECIDE → PREPARE → UPDATE → CALIBRATE → DEPLOY and a justified promotion or hold decision. The present study reaches a quantitative update comparison and a calibration/final-validation hold; the missing evidence is listed in Section 5.0.Phase 2: Integrated FrameworkBuilding on the prototype, the objective of Phase 2 is to create reusable software components that can operate on realistic site data with governance-compliant documentation.The scope expands to include modular Python packages for each core capability, with documented interfaces and test coverage. These packages can be installed and used independently or together. Automated drift detection runs on a schedule and generates diagnostic reports without manual intervention. A scripted recalibration workflow executes the necessary steps with minimal manual effort once a decision to update has been made. Configuration management tracks model versions, training data provenance, and parameter settings. Multiple surrogate architecture options are supported, allowing comparison of different approaches for a given prediction task.Deliverables include installable packages that implement the framework components, automated diagnostic reports that summarize surrogate performance and drift indicators, and a governance dashboard prototype that presents decision-relevant information for human review. The system produces documentation that meets DOE quality assurance requirements for traceability and reproducibility.Success is measured by the framework operating on representative site data – either from an actual EM site (e.g., Hanford) or from realistic synthetic scenarios – with all governance requirements satisfied. At this phase, the system still requires a technically skilled operator comfortable with Python and commandline tools, but routine operations are largely automated. The operator’s role shifts from executing steps to reviewing outputs and making decisions.Phase 3: Operational SystemThe full vision is a system that can be deployed at an EM site and operated by staff who are not machine learning specialists. The objective is acceptance for operational use under DOE quality assurance requirements.The scope includes a persistent monitoring service that runs continuously, ingests new observations as they arrive, and maintains an up-to-date picture of surrogate performance. A user interface – likely web-based – presents diagnostics, alerts, and decision options in an accessible format so that analysts can review model status, examine historical performance, and initiate recalibration workflows without writing code. The system supports multiple surrogates for different prediction tasks with coordinated monitoring and updating. Integration with site data systems allows observations to flow automatically from field sensors and laboratory results into the monitoring pipeline.Deliverables include a production-quality implementation meeting all DOE software quality requirements, training materials for site operators, and maintenance and support procedures. Full regulatory documentation demonstrates compliance with DOE EM-QA-001 requirements.Success is measured by the framework being accepted for operational use at one or more EM sites, with site staff able to operate the system for routine monitoring and governance personnel able to make informed recalibration decisions based on system outputs. This phase requires significant software engineering effort and should be informed by experience from earlier phases. Specific technology choices should be deferred until requirements are better understood through operational experience.Technical ConsiderationsComputational RequirementsThe framework spans a wide range of computational demands that must be accommodated by the implementation of the architecture.Physics simulations and surrogate training require resources matched to problem size and solver or network configuration. Large training ensembles should run through HPC batch scheduling. The simulation-driven PREPARE pathway uses approximately 6–10 targeted runs as an illustrative initial batch, with expansion governed by the evidence and budget. Training, update, inference, and uncertainty-processing times must be benchmarked on specified hardware; the current adaptive-replay evidence does not supply an end-to-end cost comparison.Inference and diagnostic calculations may be less costly than physics simulations, but their operational cost depends on model size, monitoring frequency, ensemble size, and data volume. Resource planning should use measured throughput and include uncertainty-processing and data-transfer costs.The architecture should not assume that all components run on the same hardware. Physics simulations may run on one HPC system, training on another, and monitoring on a third. Data must flow between these environments reliably, with appropriate handling of different file systems, network configurations, and security boundaries.InteroperabilitySimulator coupling presents a practical challenge because physics simulators were not designed with machine learning workflows in mind. The framework must work with simulators as they are, typically through file-based interfaces: generate input files according to simulator specifications, run the simulator as a subprocess or batch job, and parse output files to extract results. This approach is less elegant than API-based coupling but far more practical given the diversity of simulators in use across EM sites. The Physics Model Interface component must accommodate different simulators – PFLOTRAN, MODFLOW, STOMP, TOUGH2, and others – without requiring changes to downstream components.Data formats should follow existing conventions where possible. Simulation outputs are often in domainspecific formats such as PFLOTRAN HDF5 or MODFLOW binary files. The framework should read these directly rather than requiring format conversion that could introduce errors or lose information. For internal data – trained models, diagnostic logs, configuration files – standard formats such as JSON, YAML, and PyTorch checkpoints provide adequate interoperability.Reproducibility requires that every result can be regenerated from archived inputs. This means versioning not just code but also training data, model weights, configuration files, and random seeds. The data management layer must support this requirement without imposing excessive overhead on routine operations. Every prediction should be traceable to the specific surrogate version, training data, and configuration that produced it.Quality Assurance AlignmentDOE quality assurance requirements apply to software used in decision support for environmental management. These requirements do not dictate specific technologies but do constrain how the software is developed and operated. The architecture should make compliance straightforward rather than burdensome.Configuration control requires that every deployed component has a defined version, that changes be documented and reviewed before deployment, and that the system be able to report exactly what software versions produced a given result. This applies to surrogate models, monitoring algorithms, and the framework software itself.Verification and validation require that each component be tested against defined acceptance criteria before deployment, with test results documented and retained. For surrogate models, this means validation against properly held-out data and comparison to physics model predictions. For software components, this means unit tests, integration tests, and documented test coverage.Traceability requires that audit logs record what data was used, what computations were performed, and what results were produced. These logs must be retained for the project lifetime – potentially decades for long-term remediation sites. The data management layer must support efficient storage and retrieval of audit information.Corrective action procedures must define how problems are diagnosed, how fixes are implemented, and how effectiveness is verified. When a surrogate fails validation or produces unexpected results, the system must support root cause analysis and documented resolution.SummaryThis implementation roadmap translates the three-pillar framework and seven-step workflow into operational capabilities through a structured development approach.The system requirements are organized around what each pillar needs for implementation, with clear distinction between offline development and online operations. Pillar A requires governance infrastructure for configuration management, audit logging, and human review interfaces. Pillar B requires physics model interfaces and surrogate training infrastructure. Pillar C requires monitoring, drift detection, and recalibration capabilities. The workflow support ensures that both continuous monitoring (Steps 1-3) and triggered response (Steps 4-7) phases can be executed with appropriate automation and human oversight.Four explicit human decision points preserve governance accountability: recalibration approval at the DECIDE step, acceptance criteria definition, rollback decisions when validation fails, and full retraining triggers when incremental updates are insufficient. These decision points ensure that human judgment remains central to surrogate management despite automation of routine operations.The phased development approach – from prototype demonstration through integrated framework to operational system – provides progressive capability building with lessons learned informing each subsequent phase. This approach manages risk while delivering value incrementally.Technical considerations address computational requirements spanning HPC physics simulations to lightweight inference, interoperability with existing simulators and data systems, and alignment with DOE quality assurance requirements. The architecture accommodates site-specific constraints while maintaining consistent workflow and governance structure.The roadmap defines how adaptive surrogate maintenance could be implemented with appropriate scientific and governance controls. Its operational benefits remain objectives to be demonstrated through the phased evidence requirements. The current case study supplies update comparisons and identifies the remaining validation work.Prototype Demonstration and Pump-and-Treat Lifecycle Case StudyThis case study connects the seven-step framework to a concrete model-maintenance decision: how to recover accuracy on rare plume behavior while retaining performance on historical conditions, and what evidence is needed before an updated surrogate can be used. The available experiments provide a controlled, offline comparison of U-Net update strategies. They support a quantitative update assessment and a retrospective stress test; prospective monitoring, uncertainty calibration, and deployment remain requirements for the next demonstration.The quantitative source is the internal manuscript Adaptive Replay for U-Net Groundwater Plume Surrogates: Learning Rare Pump-and-Treat Dynamics Without Forgetting (September 9, 2026 draft), referred to below as the adaptive-replay study. Values are transcribed from its displayed results, and percentage changes are calculated from rounded figure labels. They have not been independently reproduced from trajectory-level predictions. Source figures are identified explicitly as manuscript figures to distinguish them from the figures in this report.Prototype context and case-study purposeThe study describes a two-dimensional pump-and-treat U-Net on a 128 × 100 grid, with one to five extraction wells, varied pumping rates, and a dataset described as 10,000 physics-based simulation runs. The model builds on the analytical-solution-enhanced surrogate of Song et al. (2025). It combines concentration information with a Thiem-equation representation of pumping influence to predict the subsequent concentration field. In manuscript Figure 4, historical root mean square error (RMSE) is 0.58 µg/L and rare-regime RMSE is 10.04 µg/L. The rare-regime result is approximately 17.3 times the historical value. Reported rare plume-mass error is 32.30%, peak-concentration error is 14.20%, and Mass90 error is 7.11%. These results identify a substantial reliability difference between the two selected response regimes.The plume metrics make the maintenance problem relevant to groundwater interpretation: concentration-field error is accompanied by discrepancies in predicted mass, peak concentration, and plume footprint. Those discrepancies motivate a review of the surrogate's applicability. They do not establish errors at actual compliance wells or demonstrate a measured change in remedy performance.Operational MONITOR would instead produce ordered prediction–observation comparisons and uncertainty diagnostics as new observations become available. The reported aggregates do not provide that record, trajectory-level error distributions, repeat-run variability, or a verified untouched test partition. They justify investigating the high-change regime while leaving uncertainty around the reported averages unquantified.DETECT: identifying the high-change stress conditionThe study selects regimes using the relative change in the number of grid cells needed to contain 90% of reference plume mass between consecutive states. Manuscript Figure 1 gives a 40th-percentile cutoff of 0.133 for historical cases and a 97th-percentile cutoff of 0.329 for rare cases. Their ratio is approximately 2.47. The bottom 40% and top 3% of the response-score distribution therefore define the historical and rare subsets used in the update comparison.This Mass90 change score is a retrospective response descriptor. It requires the reference plume at t + 1 and cannot supply a prospective warning before that state is available. Because it counts cells rather than tracking their locations, it can also miss translation or rearrangement that leaves the footprint size unchanged. Selection by this score does not establish whether the underlying cause was a pumping change, a source change, or another operational or hydrogeological process.The evidence supports a controlled stress condition for DETECT. It does not provide sensitivity, specificity, false-alarm frequency, or warning lead time. The framework's additional diagnostics—rolling residual variance, lag-1 autocorrelation, skewness and kurtosis, uncertainty inflation, input-distribution distances, and latent-space indicators—remain candidates for prospective testing. The available short sequence and aggregate results cannot substantiate the illustrative 60–90-day persistence rule. Future detector evaluation must use ordered trajectories, known change conditions, thresholds fixed before evaluation, and a documented time axis.DECIDE: screening the adaptation–retention tradeoffDECIDE translates the reliability concern into an explicit requirement for an update. An acceptable candidate must improve performance in the regime under review while retaining enough historical accuracy for its intended use. The adaptive-replay study presents several illustrative acceptance examples rather than one approved, preregistered deployment contract.For a transparent numerical screen, manuscript Figure 5 uses at most 30% historical RMSE degradation and at least 85% rare-regime RMSE improvement. The framework uses coverage near a nominal 90% level as an illustrative objective; the applicable coverage tolerance and acceptable width must be set for the intended decision.The adaptive-replay study provides no prediction intervals, ensemble or dropout samples, conformal calibration residuals, calibration parameters, or reliability diagrams. Consequently, the candidate outcomes that pass the Figure 8 screen remain awaiting calibration. A deterministic surrogate could use residual-based conformal calibration if suitable independent data exist. Its usual marginal-coverage guarantees require the relevant exchangeability assumptions and cannot be presumed under distribution shift or within temporally dependent trajectories.The next evidence package should keep controller-selection data, uncertainty-calibration data, and untouched final-test trajectories distinct. It should report coverage and width separately for historical and rare regimes and at decision-relevant locations. Those requirements establish the next experiment; no numerical calibration result can be inferred from the RMSE reductions already reported.DEPLOY: final validation and promotion decisionDEPLOY requires an untouched final test and a versioned acceptance record. Evaluation must address the frozen historical/rare performance contract, priority-location error limits, uncertainty coverage and width, spatial and temporal consistency, and systematic bias near decision boundaries. The record must identify the model, preprocessing, training and calibration configurations, data provenance, reviewer, and promotion decision.Those promotion artifacts are not available for the adaptive-replay study. The case-study outcome is therefore a documented hold for calibration and final validation, with no demonstrated deployment. Candidate rejection does not authorize use of an invalid incumbent: if operational validity is in question, the withdrawal and governed retraining policy in Section 3.1.5 applies. The offline screening results in this case study are not themselves an operational rollback event.Integrated lifecycle resultThe seven steps form a connected decision argument. This section interprets that evidence, distinguishes the remaining scientific and operational requirements, and connects the next validation tasks to the roadmap in Section 4.0.LimitationsDependence on Physics-Model QualitySurrogate accuracy is fundamentally bound by the quality of the physics-based models used to generate training data. Errors or biases in the numerical models – whether from simplified boundary conditions, incomplete process coupling, or uncertain parameterization – will propagate to the surrogates. The recalibration framework can detect when surrogate predictions deviate from new physics-model runs, but it cannot correct for systematic deficiencies in the physics models themselves. Translating simulators into credible site predictions requires that hydraulic conductivities, recharge rates, boundary conditions, and transport parameters be carefully adjusted until simulated heads and concentrations adequately reproduce field observations. Whether calibration relies on automated inverse methods such as PEST or DAKOTA (Adams et al. 2020; Doherty 1994; Hill and Tiedeman 2007) or ensemble-based DA (Evensen 2009), ensuring that the underlying numerical models remain well-calibrated is a prerequisite for effective surrogate maintenance.Computational Requirements for Ensemble MethodsEnsemble-based UQ requires training and maintaining multiple surrogate instances, increasing computational and storage demands relative to a single deterministic model. While surrogate inference remains orders of magnitude faster than physics-based simulation, the aggregate cost of ensemble training, storage of model checkpoints, and periodic retraining across all ensemble members must be accounted for in resource planning. Efficient alternatives such as MC-Dropout or spectral-normalized approaches may reduce this burden but require further evaluation in the groundwater context.Validation StatusThe framework is defined and the U-Net case study supplies controlled update evidence. The available results remain rounded aggregate manuscript values rather than independently reproduced trajectory-level tests. Prospective detection, an independent calibration result, a verified untouched final test, and a model-version promotion record are absent. Field validation with measurement noise, irregular sampling, data gaps, and site complexity also remains to be demonstrated. A retrospective response-based stress partition does not by itself establish an operational regime change.Areas for Further InvestigationThe roadmap in Section 4.0 defines development phases, and Section 5.0 identifies the current evidence and its limits. The immediate scientific priorities are to verify the time axis and trajectory split, reconcile panel-specific model configurations, evaluate a matched fixed-replay control, and complete uncertainty calibration and independent final testing. Broader questions concern detector design, multi-fidelity fusion, transferability, and scalability.Trigger-Threshold CalibrationThe drift-detection and recalibration trigger logic described in Section 3.3 relies on thresholds for uncertainty growth, residual divergence, and input-distribution shift. Optimal threshold values are likely site-specific, depending on historical variability, measurement frequency, and the consequence of delayed recalibration. Developing general guidance – or adaptive threshold methods – that reduce the need for manual tuning would improve framework usability across sites. Established drift-detection algorithms provide starting points, but environmental applications may require domain-specific adaptations given the slower timescales and sparse observations typical of groundwater monitoring networks.Multi-Fidelity FusionCombining information from models of varying fidelity and computational cost – for example, coarsegrid screening models, refined local models, and DL surrogates – offers a path toward more efficient generation of recalibration data. Multi-fidelity methods have shown promise in related domains, but their integration into the recalibration workflow for groundwater transport requires further development, particularly regarding how fidelity levels map to the governance-tier requirements defined in Pillar A.Transfer Learning Across SitesDeploying the framework at a new DOE site currently requires site-specific training data from local physics models. Transfer learning could reduce this data requirement by leveraging surrogate knowledge from previously characterized sites with analogous hydrogeological settings. Key open questions include how to quantify transferability between sites, how to define domain similarity for heterogeneous aquifer systems, and how governance procedures should account for transferred vs. locally validated model components.ScalabilityRealistic EM applications typically involve 3D, heterogeneous domains, long simulation horizons, and tight numerical tolerances to preserve mass balance and stability. Extension to these fully 3D domains with finer spatial resolution and more complex multi-species reactive transport will stress both the surrogate architectures and the recalibration methods. Whether the continual-learning and active-learning strategies described in Section 3.3 scale gracefully – or require architectural modifications – is an empirical question that the prototype phase will begin to address.Regulatory Acceptance PathwaysAdaptive machine-learning models introduce a paradigm shift for regulatory review: The approved model is no longer static. Engagement with regulatory agencies is needed to establish acceptable review and approval procedures for models that update over time. The governance structure in Pillar A (Section 3.1) is designed to facilitate this engagement, but the specific regulatory pathway remains to be negotiated on a site-by-site basis.Expected ImpactOperational BenefitsPotential operational benefits include faster scenario exploration and a reduced need to regenerate complete training ensembles when a targeted update is sufficient. Published surrogate applications motivate that possibility, but the current study does not quantify full-lifecycle speedup, physics-run savings, memory, or training cost. Those benefits should be evaluated under a common workload by comparing physics-only analysis, a static surrogate, and the hybrid recalibration workflow.Scientific ContributionsThe report connects three management domains and seven lifecycle steps to an evidence-based model-maintenance decision. The measured case study shows a material tradeoff between rare-regime recovery and historical retention. The uncertainty and governance gates specify why a candidate that improves point predictions may still require further evaluation. The scientific contribution is this explicit connection between performance evidence, acceptance conditions, and the next action.Broader ApplicabilityThe framework may be adapted to other contaminants, sites, and scientific surrogates, but transferability requires application-specific data, error limits, and governance decisions. Long-horizon forecasting and digital-twin applications are possible extensions rather than results established here. Reusable code and demonstration datasets, when documented and cleared for release, can support independent evaluation and broader adoption.ConclusionsFive conclusions follow from the framework and controlled update evidence:Surrogate maintenance is a lifecycle problem. Monitoring, evidence-based intervention, uncertainty assessment, and review must accompany model updating when conditions move beyond the evaluated domain.Physics-based analysis remains authoritative for final remedy decisions. Surrogates support efficient screening and scenario exploration, while their approximation errors and the limitations of the underlying physics model must both be assessed.Historical retention and rare-regime recovery must be evaluated jointly. In the reported comparison, fixed replay reduces rare RMSE from 10.04 to 1.21 µg/L while historical RMSE increases from 0.58 to 1.03 µg/L. That tradeoff makes a pre-established acceptance contract necessary.The illustrative controller screen identifies candidates, not approved deployments. Two displayed controller outcomes satisfy the 30%/85% conditions, but uncertainty, matched-control, and independent-test evidence remain necessary to interpret and confirm their performance.A documented hold is a valid lifecycle outcome. The current candidates remain awaiting uncertainty calibration and final validation. Completing those records, together with a traceable model version and reviewer decision, is the next step toward a reproducible operational demonstration.ReferencesAdaptive Replay for U-Net Groundwater Plume Surrogates: Learning Rare Pump-and-Treat Dynamics Without Forgetting. 2026.
