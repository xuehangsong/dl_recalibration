81 [Heading-FrontTOC] Summary
82 [BodyText] Deep-learning surrogate models can reduce groundwater simulation times from hours or days to seconds, making it feasible to rapidly evaluate remediation alternatives, explore uncertainty, and support planning decisions that would otherwise be constrained by computational cost. For U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Environmental Management (EM) sites managing complex, long-duration groundwater cleanup, this capability offers substantial operational value.
83 [BodyText] However, surrogate models are trained on data that represent site conditions at a particular point in time. Over the years and decades typical of EM remediation, those conditions may change because of natural system evolution, modifications to pumping or treatment systems, new monitoring data, or revisions to the conceptual site model. As a result, surrogate accuracy may degrade over time. If such degradation is not detected and addressed, surrogate-informed decisions may no longer be reliable. In regulatory settings governed by Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) and DOE quality assurance requirements, that risk is unacceptable.
84 [BodyText] Although interest in surrogate modeling is growing within the environmental modeling community, no established methodology currently exists for maintaining surrogate accuracy over multi-year operational periods while preserving the governance, documentation, and quality assurance standards required for EM applications.
85 [BodyText] The purpose of this project is to establish a robust and defensible framework for deploying and maintaining artificial-intelligence-enabled deep-learning surrogate models as reliable decision-support tools throughout the lifecycle of EM groundwater remediation. This mid-year report presents the conceptual design and prototype plan for that framework, which addresses the long-term surrogate maintenance challenge through three core components:
86 [ListBullet] Governance defines the rules for surrogate use, including alignment with CERCLA, U.S. Environmental Protection Agency environmental modeling guidance, and DOE EM-QA-001; acceptance criteria; recalibration triggers; and configuration control. Within this framework, surrogate models are limited to screening, scenario exploration, and planning support, while high-fidelity physics-based models remain the authoritative basis for final regulatory and remedy decisions.
87 [ListBullet] Modeling engines include both the physics-based numerical simulators that provide reference predictions and the deep-learning surrogates that provide fast approximate results. Surrogate models are data-driven approximations of the physics-based models and can reproduce simulator behavior at speeds 103 to 105 times faster, while the physics-based model remains the ground-truth reference throughout framework operation.
88 [ListBullet] Recalibration mechanisms provide the technical methods needed to monitor surrogate performance, detect degradation, and execute targeted updates that restore reliability without requiring routine full retraining.
89 [BodyText] These three components are connected through a structured, seven-step workflow with two phases: continuous monitoring that runs passively during routine operations, and a triggered response that activates only when monitoring identifies meaningful degradation and governance review authorizes an update. Every step includes defined decision points, acceptance criteria, and documentation requirements to support auditability and independent review.
90 [BodyText] Two proof-of-concept implementations are in preparation: a Fourier neural operator surrogate for cross-section contaminant transport, and a convolutional encoder-decoder surrogate for pump and treat plume prediction. These prototypes will exercise the full recalibration workflow on representative groundwater modeling problems, validating that the framework can detect surrogate degradation, execute targeted corrections, and restore accuracy with minimal computational cost.
91 [BodyText] The framework contributes a structured, governance-aware approach to surrogate maintenance that is currently absent from both the machine-learning and environmental-modeling literature. By integrating adaptive modeling methods with DOE quality assurance standards and CERCLA expectations, it addresses a key barrier to operational adoption of surrogate models at EM sites: the need for sustained confidence that fast predictions remain reliable over the long timelines that define DOE groundwater remediation.
92 [Heading-FrontTOC] Overview
93 [BodyText] This report presents a framework for deploying and maintaining deep-learning (DL) surrogate models as reliable decision-support tools for long-term groundwater remediation within the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Environmental Management (EM) complex. DL surrogates can accelerate predictions of groundwater flow and transport by factors of 103 to 105 relative to physics-based numerical simulators, making it feasible to evaluate large scenario sets, explore uncertainty, and support planning decisions that would otherwise be computationally impractical. However, surrogates trained at a single point in time may lose accuracy as site conditions evolve, remediation systems are modified, conceptual models are refined, or new field data reveal behavior outside the original training domain. Without a systematic process for detecting and correcting such degradation, surrogate-based decision support may become unreliable, which is unacceptable in compliance-driven settings governed by the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) and DOE EM-QA-001 quality assurance requirements.
94 [BodyText] The proposed framework is organized around three pillars: 
95 [ListBullet] Pillar A, Governance, establishes the regulatory alignment, acceptance criteria, configuration control, and decision authority needed to manage adaptive models in compliance-driven environments. It is grounded in U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) environmental modeling guidance and DOE EM-QA-001, which incorporates ASME NQA-1 quality assurance principles for environmental management software and modeling activities. 
96 [ListBullet] Pillar B, Modeling Engines, encompasses both the physics-based numerical simulators that remain the authoritative source of truth and the DL surrogate models that provide fast approximate predictions. Physics-based models such as PFLOTRAN, MODFLOW, STOMP, and TOUGH2 provide the high-fidelity reference standard, while surrogate architectures such as Fourier neural operators and diffusion models enable rapid inference for screening and planning. 
97 [ListBullet] Pillar C, Recalibration Mechanisms, provides the technical methods needed to maintain surrogate reliability over time, including uncertainty quantification, drift detection, continual learning, and active learning.
98 [BodyText] Within this framework, surrogate models are explicitly limited to screening-level analyses, scenario exploration, and planning support. High-fidelity physics-based models remain the authoritative basis for final regulatory and remedy decisions. When surrogate validity is in question, governance procedures require withdrawal of surrogate use and escalation to a controlled retraining process, thereby preventing unmanaged risk. This conservative posture is consistent with CERCLA protectiveness expectations and DOE quality assurance requirements.
99 [BodyText] The framework has four objectives. First, it defines a governance structure that aligns surrogate deployment and maintenance with established regulatory and quality assurance requirements. Second, it establishes systematic methods for monitoring surrogate performance and detecting degradation before decision support is compromised. Third, it provides efficient recalibration procedures to restore surrogate accuracy within predefined acceptance criteria while minimizing the burden of new high-fidelity simulations. Fourth, it operationalizes these capabilities through a structured workflow with explicit decision points, documentation requirements, and configuration control to support auditability, reproducibility, and independent review.
100 [BodyText] These objectives are implemented through a seven-step, two-phase workflow. The first phase, continuous monitoring, runs during routine surrogate operation and includes monitoring performance indicators, detecting potential drift, and determining whether observed changes warrant action. The second phase, triggered response, is activated only when monitoring indicates meaningful degradation and governance review approves an update. It includes preparing new training data, updating surrogate parameters, validating the revised model against predefined acceptance criteria, and deploying the approved version under formal configuration control. Throughout this process, governance records document trigger rationale, data provenance, validation results, and deployment decisions to support transparency and auditability.
101 [BodyText] The report describes the methods underlying each pillar, including governance procedures aligned with EPA and DOE quality assurance standards, surrogate and physics-model architectures, and recalibration techniques drawn from the continual-learning and concept-drift literature. It also presents an implementation roadmap and a prototype demonstration plan featuring two proof-of-concept applications: a Fourier neural operator surrogate for two-dimensional cross-section transport and a U-Net surrogate for pump and treat plume prediction. These demonstrations are designed to test the complete recalibration cycle under controlled synthetic drift conditions.
102 [BodyText] This work makes three main contributions. First, it proposes a structured, governance-aware approach for maintaining surrogate models over long operational periods, addressing a gap in both the machine-learning and environmental-modeling literature. Second, it integrates uncertainty quantification, drift detection, and continual learning into a single operational workflow rather than treating them as isolated technical methods. Third, it establishes a governance template for applying adaptive machine-learning models in regulatory contexts, helping address a major barrier to operational adoption at EM sites.
103 [BodyText] The framework remains at the concept and design stage. Current limitations include dependence on the quality of the underlying physics-based models, the computational cost of some uncertainty quantification methods, and the need for validation under real field conditions with measurement noise, data gaps, and site complexity representative of EM applications. Prototype development and testing on representative groundwater remediation problems are the next steps toward eventual operational deployment.

262 [Heading1] Introduction
263 [Heading2] Motivation and Problem Statement
264 [] Deep learning surrogate models are AIenabled, data-driven approximations of complex physical, numerical, or computational models, built using deep neural networks to emulate the behavior of high-fidelity simulations. Instead of repeatedly running computationally expensive models (e.g., groundwater flow, contaminant transport, or reactive chemistry), a surrogate model is trained on simulation or observational data to learn the input–output relationships and then provide rapid predictions.
265 [BodyText] These models are particularly valuable for tasks like uncertainty quantification, optimization, and real-time decision support, where thousands to millions of model evaluations may be required. By significantly reducing computational cost while maintaining acceptable accuracy, deep learning surrogates enable faster scenario analysis, integration into AI-driven workflows, and scalable system-level optimization.
266 [BodyText] Cleanup programs at U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Environmental Management (EM) sites such as Hanford, Savannah River, and Los Alamos operate on timelines spanning decades, during which hydrogeological conditions evolve, remediation systems are modified, monitoring networks are updated, and conceptual site models are revised in response to new data. Throughout these extended campaigns, site managers must evaluate alternative remediation strategies, assess system performance, and demonstrate continued protectiveness of human health and the environment under various regulatory frameworks such as requirements established under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) and reinforced by EM quality assurance expectations (NRC 2013; EPA 2001). The purpose of this project is to establish a robust, defensible framework for deploying and maintaining artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled deep learning (DL) surrogate models as reliable decision-support tools over the long-term lifecycle of EM cleanup activities. 
267 [BodyText] Physics-based numerical models are the established foundation for developing the technical basis for supporting decision-making throughout the long cleanup timeframes. High-fidelity simulators coupling groundwater flow, contaminant transport, and geochemical reactions provide the defensible, process-based predictions on which regulatory and remedy decisions depend. However, these models are computationally expensive to develop, run, maintain, and update. These computational burdens limit the pace and scope of analyses that site teams can practically perform within project timelines and budgets.
268 [BodyText] DL surrogate models offer a path to overcoming these computational constraints and significantly accelerating and improving modeling efforts, and ultimately, decision-making at sites. By learning the input-output relationships of physics-based simulators from training data, surrogates can approximate numerical model predictions at inference speeds 103 to 105 times faster than the original simulators. This capability enables rapid scenario evaluation, interactive planning, and large-scale uncertainty exploration that would be impractical using physics-based simulation alone. Recent advances in neural operator architectures, convolutional encoder-decoder networks, and physics-informed training strategies have demonstrated surrogate accuracy sufficient for meaningful decision support across a range of subsurface modeling applications.
269 [BodyText] While surrogate models offer significant improvements in computational efficiency, they—like purely physics-based models—must still be rigorously verified, calibrated, and continuously updated as new data become available. A surrogate trained at a single point in time encodes the conditions, parameters, and conceptual understanding reflected in its training data. As site conditions change — through natural system evolution, operational modifications, updated boundary conditions, or revised parameter estimates — the surrogate’s training distribution diverges from the conditions it is asked to predict. This divergence, termed distribution shift or concept drift, causes surrogate accuracy to degrade progressively. Without systematic detection and correction, this degradation can proceed silently, producing predictions that appear reasonable but are no longer reliable.
270 [BodyText] Despite this clear need, no established framework exists for systematically maintaining surrogate model accuracy over multi-year operational periods while preserving regulatory confidence. The machine-learning literature offers individual methods for uncertainty quantification (UQ), drift detection, continual learning, and active learning, but these techniques are typically developed and evaluated in isolation, outside the governance and quality assurance context required for environmental compliance applications. The environmental modeling community, in turn, has well-established governance practices for physics-based models but lacks guidance for managing adaptive machine-learning models that update over time. Therefore, this report addresses the gap between the technical capability to build surrogates and the operational infrastructure to maintain them.
271 [BodyText] The objective of the report is to define a structured, end-to-end framework for the deployment, monitoring, and lifecycle management of DL surrogate models within EM decision processes. It translates emerging machine learning capabilities into a practical, auditable system aligned with site technical needs, and regulatory and quality assurance expectations.
272 [Heading2] Framework Objectives
273 [BodyText] This report presents a recalibration framework designed to maintain DL surrogate accuracy over the multi-year to multi-decade timelines typical of EM groundwater remediation. The framework has four objectives:
274 [BodyText] Define governance structures that align surrogate model deployment with established regulatory and quality assurance requirements, including CERCLA expectations, U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) environmental modeling guidance, and DOE EM-QA-001. Within this governance structure, surrogate models are explicitly limited to screening-level analyses, scenario exploration, and planning support. High-fidelity physics-based numerical models remain the authoritative basis for final regulatory and remedy decisions.
275 [BodyText] Establish systematic methods for monitoring surrogate performance and detecting degradation before it compromises decision support. These methods integrate UQ, statistical drift detection, and residual analysis into a continuous monitoring process that runs passively during routine surrogate operation.
276 [BodyText] Provide efficient recalibration procedures that restore surrogate accuracy to within defined acceptance criteria upon detection of predictive degradation. Such recalibration employs continual-learning methods to mitigate knowledge forgetting across successive training cycles, and active-learning strategies to identify maximally informative simulation candidates and thereby minimize the computational burden of new physics-model simulations.
277 [BodyText] Operationalize these capabilities through a structured workflow with defined steps, decision points, acceptance criteria, and documentation requirements that support auditability, reproducibility, and independent review.
278 [BodyText] The framework is organized around three pillars – Governance, Modeling Engines, and Recalibration Mechanisms – that together address the coupled technical and institutional requirements of deploying adaptive models in compliance-driven environments.
279 [Heading2] Report Organization

286 [Heading1] Framework Overview 
287 [Heading2] Three Pillars Structure
288 [BodyText] The proposed recalibration framework is organized around three core pillars that together provide the foundation for maintaining DL surrogate accuracy over operational timescales. These pillars function as management domains – organizational containers that hold and maintain specific concerns essential to the framework’s operation. Each pillar constitutes a distinct functional area with defined responsibilities: Pillar A establishes the rules and policies that govern operations, Pillar B manages the computational physics-based and surrogate models that generate predictions and ground truth, and Pillar C provides the adaptive monitoring and recalibration mechanism that monitor performance degradation and restore surrogate accuracy within accepted thresholds.
289 [BodyText] The three pillars define what the framework manages, while the recalibration workflow described in Section 2.2 defines how these elements interact during operations. Each workflow step draws resources, methods, or constraints from one or more pillars, but no single pillar owns the workflow. This separation of concerns ensures that governance policies, modeling capabilities, and adaptive methods can evolve independently while remaining integrated through the operational workflow.
290 [Heading3] Pillar A: Conceptual Design & Governance
291 [BodyText] Pillar A serves as the design authority for the framework, defining the rules under which all operations proceed. This pillar establishes the objectives, performance thresholds, success metrics, and regulatory requirements that guide surrogate development, deployment, evaluation, and maintenance. It specifies the conditions and thresholds that initiate recalibration, defines what constitutes acceptable performance, and prescribes how decisions must be documented for regulatory compliance and independent review.
292 [BodyText] The contents of Pillar A include trigger policies that specify criteria indicating when recalibration should be evaluated, including thresholds on predictive uncertainty, prediction–observation residual, and drift metrics. These policies define the conditions under which the framework transitions from routine monitoring to active evaluation of update needs. Pillar A also contains acceptance criteria – quantitative standards that define acceptable performance for operational deployment. These typically include error statistics at priority monitoring locations such as root mean square error (RMSE) targets, calibration of uncertainty estimates with prediction interval coverage probability, and spatiotemporal consistency with observed system behavior.
293 [BodyText] Rollback procedures form another essential component of Pillar A, providing protocols for responding when an attempted surrogate update fails to meet acceptance criteria. As defined in Section 3.1.5, rollback in this framework doesn’t mean reversion to a prior surrogate version; instead, it means withdrawing the surrogate from operational use and escalating to full retraining. This distinction reflects the conservative approach required for high-consequence environmental applications. Audit requirements ensure that all recalibration decisions are traceable and defensible through documentation standards that include logging trigger rationale, data sources used, evaluation results, and verification outcomes, consistent with DOE quality assurance requirements.
294 [BodyText] Data policies within Pillar A specify which measurements to assimilate, quality assurance and quality control standards, and data provenance requirements, ensuring that only validated data influence surrogate updates. Interpretability standards establish requirements for model explainability that support governance review and stakeholder confidence, following principles articulated in environmental modeling guidance. Success metrics provide alignment criteria that connect surrogate performance to DOE mission goals and site-specific compliance needs.
295 [BodyText] Governance does not only constrain the framework – it defines what the other pillars do. Pillar A determines the trigger thresholds that Pillar C monitors, selects which learning strategies are appropriate for different situations, and decides whether results from Pillar B are acceptable for operational use. It is the design authority, not simply a set of guardrails.
296 [Heading3] Pillar B: Modeling Engines
297 [BodyText] Pillar B provides the computational foundation for the framework, managing the models that generate predictions and establish ground truth. This pillar comprises both the high-fidelity physics-based simulator that serves as the authoritative reference and the DL surrogate that enables fast operational inference.
298 [BodyText] The physics simulator represents the high-fidelity numerical model – such as PFLOTRAN, MODFLOW, STOMP, or TOUGH2 – that serves as the truth generator for the framework. These simulators provide physically consistent predictions but require substantial computational resources, with individual runs potentially taking hours to days depending on model complexity. When surrogate correction is required, the physics model generates new training data at conditions where predictive degradation has been detected. All recalibration updates are driven by high-fidelity simulation outputs rather than extrapolation.
299 [BodyText] The DL surrogate is the fast inference model trained to emulate the physics simulator’s input-output behavior. Architectures may include U-Net (encoder-decoder convolutional network, named for its U-shaped architecture), U-FNO (U-Net Fourier neural operator), long short-term memory (LSTM), or diffusion models, depending on the prediction task. Surrogates can generate predictions orders of magnitude faster than direct physics simulation, enabling real-time decision support. However, surrogates interpolate within their training domain and are subject to distribution drift when conditions evolve beyond their original training envelope.
300 [BodyText] Pillar B also needs to maintain clear input-output contracts that define the interface between physics models and surrogates to ensure that data formats, coordinate systems, and variable definitions are consistent across model types. Versioned artifacts provide model version control and management systems that track surrogate versions, training configurations, and associated metadata, enabling reproducibility and supporting rollback procedures when needed. Training data archives serve as repositories of physics simulation outputs and field observations used for surrogate training, organized with provenance metadata to support audit requirements.
301 [BodyText] The physics model remains the authoritative truth source throughout the framework’s operation. The surrogate is a sophisticated emulator that provides computational efficiency, but its validity is always assessed relative to physics-based predictions and field observations. When the surrogate predictions extend beyond the conditions represented in its training domain, the physics model provides the authoritative reference simulations needed to support correction and retraining.
302 [Heading3] Pillar C: Recalibration Mechanisms
303 [BodyText] Pillar C supplies the adaptive intelligence for the framework, managing the techniques used for monitoring surrogate performance, detecting degradation, and orchestrating corrections. This pillar comprises the methods library that the operational workflow draws upon to maintain surrogate accuracy over time.
304 [BodyText] UQ methods within Pillar C provide techniques for estimating predictive uncertainty, including Monte Carlo dropout (MC-Dropout), deep ensembles, Bayesian neural networks, and conformal prediction. These methods provide the diagnostic self-awareness needed to identify when surrogate predictions become unreliable. Drift detection algorithms offer statistical methods for identifying performance degradation, including residual monitoring, uncertainty inflation tracking, distributional drift tests such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and representation-level drift detection in latent spaces. These algorithms implement the trigger logic that initiates governance review.
305 [BodyText] Active learning strategies enable scenario selection methods that identify the most informative new physics simulations to run when correction is needed. Rather than random sampling, active learning targets conditions at decision boundaries, rare scenarios, or regions of high uncertainty where new data will maximally improve model performance. Continual learning methods provide techniques for updating the surrogate without full retraining, including elastic weight consolidation, experience replay, and layer freezing. These methods enable incremental updates that preserve previously learned knowledge while adapting to new conditions, thereby avoiding catastrophic forgetting.
306 [BodyText] Multi-fidelity data fusion approaches combine information from multiple sources – field observations, physics model outputs at different resolutions, and surrogate predictions – to refine estimates and reduce uncertainty. Probability calibration encompasses post-hoc methods for ensuring that surrogate uncertainty estimates are well-calibrated, including temperature scaling, isotonic regression, and reliability diagram analysis. These techniques ensure that stated confidence intervals accurately reflect true prediction reliability.
307 [BodyText] In general, Pillar C provides methods that the operational workflow uses. It serves as a toolkit from which workflow steps draw appropriate techniques based on the site situation. The workflow (Section 2.2) orchestrates when and how these methods are applied; Pillar C ensures the methods are available and properly maintained.
308 [Heading3] Complementary Roles of the Three Pillars
309 [BodyText] The three pillars are co-essential to the framework’s operation, each playing a distinct and complementary role. Pillar A is the design authority: It defines the rules, sets the thresholds, and determines what constitutes acceptable performance. Without governance, the framework would lack the constraints needed for regulatory compliance and responsible deployment. Pillar B provides the computational foundation: It supplies the physics models that establish ground truth and the surrogates that enable fast inference. Without modeling engines, the framework would have nothing to maintain or correct. Pillar C supplies adaptive intelligence: It provides monitoring, detection, and correction methods that keep surrogates accurate over time. Without recalibration mechanisms, the framework could not adapt to changing conditions.
310 [BodyText] No pillar should be viewed as more fundamental than the others; they are interdependent. Governance without models has nothing to govern; models without governance lack accountability; models without adaptation will drift; adaptation without governance may be inappropriate. The framework’s value emerges from their integration. Figure 1 illustrates the high-level architecture.

313 [Heading2] Recalibration Workflow 
314 [BodyText] The workflow is structured as a seven-step cycle organized into two phases. Phase 1, Continuous Monitoring, encompasses Steps 1 through 3 and runs continuously during operations, tracking surrogate performance and determining when intervention may be needed. Phase 2, Triggered Response, encompasses Steps 4 through 7 and is activated only when Phase 1 indicates that an update is warranted and governance approves the intervention.
315 [BodyText] This two-phase structure reflects a key principle from the governance framework described in Section 2.1.1: The presence of a recalibration trigger does not automatically mandate recalibration. Instead, the framework requires formal justification that an update is necessary, proportional, and expected to improve performance. The explicit DECIDE step (Step 3) implements this governance requirement. 
316 [Figure] 
317 [Caption-Fig] Figure 2.Seven-step, two-phase recalibration workflow. Phase 1 (green) operates continuously during routine surrogate use and consists of three steps: MONITOR, DETECT, and DECIDE. Phase 2 (red) activates only when the DECIDE step determines that observed degradation warrants recalibration, and consists of four steps: PREPARE, UPDATE, CALIBRATE, and DEPLOY. The DECIDE step (filled circle) serves as the governance gate connecting both phases, ensuring that recalibration is initiated only through a documented, criteria-based decision. Upon successful deployment, the workflow returns to Phase 1 continuous monitoring. The “Fallback” path (black) represents escalation to complete surrogate retraining under governance procedures defined in Pillar A.
318 [Heading3] Phase 1: Continuous Monitoring
319 [BodyText] Phase 1 runs continuously during site operations, providing ongoing assessment of surrogate performance without modifying the operational model. These steps implement the monitor-detect-decide logic that determines whether intervention is needed.
320 [BodyText] Step 1: MONITOR 
321 [BodyText] The purpose of the MONITOR step is to track surrogate predictions against available monitoring data and compute diagnostic metrics. This step compares surrogate predictions to field monitoring data as observations become available, calculates prediction-observation residuals at priority monitoring locations, and computes uncertainty metrics using embedded UQ methods such as MC-Dropout or deep ensembles. The step also tracks prediction interval coverage and calibration statistics, logging all comparisons for trend analysis and audit purposes.
322 [BodyText] The MONITOR step draws from Pillar A for data policies specifying which observations to assimilate, quality assurance and quality control standards for data validation, and specifications for what metrics to track. It draws from Pillar C for UQ methods used to compute predictive uncertainty and confidence intervals. The outputs of this step are time series of residuals, uncertainty metrics, and coverage statistics that feed into Step 2.
323 [BodyText] Step 2: DETECT
324 [BodyText] The purpose of the DETECT step is to identify when surrogate performance has degraded beyond acceptable thresholds using automated diagnostics. This step applies drift detection algorithms to residual and uncertainty time series and compares current metrics against predefined trigger thresholds from Pillar A. The step evaluates multiple detection criteria, including predictive uncertainty exceeding confidence bounds, systematic bias in prediction-observation residuals, uncertainty inflation relative to historical baselines, distributional drift in inputs or outputs using tests such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and extrapolation beyond the original training domain.
325 [BodyText] The DETECT step draws from Pillar A for trigger thresholds, persistence requirements (e.g., 60-90 days), and criteria for state transitions. It draws from Pillar C for drift detection algorithms, statistical tests, and resilience metrics. The outputs of this step are the current trigger state and an evidence package documenting the detection rationale.
326 [BodyText] Step 3: DECIDE
327 [BodyText] The purpose of the DECIDE step is to conduct human governance review of detection evidence and formally determine whether an update is warranted. This step is critical because it implements the governance requirement that triggers do not automatically mandate recalibration. A human reviewer or governance committee, depending on site procedures, must evaluate the evidence and make a formal determination.
328 [BodyText] The DECIDE step reviews the evidence package from Step 2, including residual trends and uncertainty metrics, drift detection results and statistical significance, documented operational changes such as new wells or pumping schedule changes, and comparison to historical performance patterns. The reviewer assesses whether the observed degradation is meaningful (large enough to affect decision quality), persistent (not a transient anomaly), and addressable (likely to be corrected by an incremental update). Based on this assessment, the step determines the appropriate response. If an update is warranted, the system proceeds to Phase 2. If an incremental update is unlikely to succeed, the system escalates directly to full retraining.
329 [BodyText] When an update is approved, the DECIDE step also selects the update pathway. Pathway A, the observation-driven approach, is selected when recent field observations provide sufficient new information for fine-tuning. Pathway B, the simulation-driven approach, is selected when active learning should identify new physics simulations to generate targeted training data.
330 [BodyText] The DECIDE step draws exclusively from Pillar A for governance policies, formal justification requirements, approval workflows, and documentation standards. The outputs of this step are a formal decision record documenting the determination, rationale, and selected pathway if an update is approved.
331 [Heading3] Phase 2: Triggered Response
332 [BodyText] Phase 2 is activated only when Step 3 (DECIDE) approves an update. These steps implement the prepareupdate-calibrate-deploy sequence that modifies the operational surrogate.
333 [BodyText] Step 4: PREPARE
334 [BodyText] The purpose of the PREPARE step is to assemble the dataset that will be used to update the surrogate. The specific activities depend on the pathway selected in Step 3.
335 [BodyText] For Pathway A (observation-driven updates), the step compiles recent field observations that were not in the original training set, applies quality assurance and quality control procedures per Pillar A data policies, and formats data for compatibility with the surrogate training pipeline.
336 [BodyText] For Pathway B (simulation-driven updates), the step generates a candidate pool of operationally relevant simulation scenarios and applies active learning algorithms to score candidates based on predictive uncertainty in the candidate region, residual patterns relative to recent observations, and diversity constraints covering spatial, temporal, and parameter dimensions. The step then selects 6 to 10 high-value scenarios that maximize expected information gain, executes targeted physics simulations for selected scenarios, and combines new simulation outputs with the replay buffer.
337 [BodyText] The PREPARE step draws from Pillar A for data policies and quality assurance standards, from Pillar B for the physics simulator when executing new runs under Pathway B, and from Pillar C for active learning strategies used in scenario selection. The output of this step is a prepared dataset with provenance documentation. 
338 [BodyText] Step 5: UPDATE
339 [BodyText] The purpose of the UPDATE step is to fine-tune the DL surrogate using continual learning methods to incorporate new information while preserving previously learned knowledge. This step configures the continual learning approach based on the update context, selecting from methods such as elastic weight consolidation to penalize changes to weights important for previous tasks, experience replay to include historical samples in each training batch, and layer freezing to preserve learned representations in early encoder layers.
340 [BodyText] This step sets conservative hyperparameters, including a small learning rate to prevent catastrophic forgetting, L2 regularization anchored to pre-update weights, and early stopping based on validation performance. The step then fine-tunes the prepared dataset while monitoring training for convergence and stability.
341 [BodyText] A key principle of this step is that it performs an incremental update, not full retraining. The goal is to adapt the surrogate to new conditions while preserving its validity in previously learned domains. Full retraining is reserved for cases where incremental updates repeatedly fail. 
342 [BodyText] The UPDATE step draws from Pillar B for the surrogate model to be updated and training infrastructure, and from Pillar C for continual learning methods including elastic weight consolidation, replay, and layer freezing. The output of this step is an updated surrogate model in candidate status, not yet operational.
343 [BodyText] Step 6: CALIBRATE
344 [BodyText] Whereas the UPDATE step optimizes model weights for predictive accuracy, the CALIBRATE step addresses the statistical reliability of the model’s expressed uncertainty.
345 [BodyText] The purpose of the CALIBRATE step is to perform post-hoc calibration of the updated model’s uncertainty estimates to ensure stated confidence intervals accurately reflect true prediction reliability. This step is separate from UPDATE because the constraints applied during continual learning can distort the relationship between ensemble spread and true predictive uncertainty. Probability calibration techniques are post-training activities that correct this distortion without modifying the model’s point predictions.
346 [BodyText] The CALIBRATE step evaluates the updated model’s uncertainty calibration on held-out validation data and generates reliability diagrams comparing stated confidence levels to empirical coverage. The step verifies that calibrated prediction intervals meet acceptance criteria and documents calibration parameters for reproducibility.
347 [BodyText] The CALIBRATE step draws from Pillar A for acceptance criteria for uncertainty calibration such as coverage targets, and from Pillar C for probability calibration methods and reliability diagram analysis. The output of this step is a calibrated surrogate model with documented uncertainty characteristics.
348 [BodyText] Step 7: DEPLOY
349 [BodyText] The purpose of the DEPLOY step is to validate the updated model against acceptance criteria and, if successful, promote it to operational status with full governance documentation.
350 [BodyText] The validation phase evaluates the calibrated model on held-out test data not used in training or calibration. Performance is compared against predefined acceptance criteria from Pillar A, including error statistics at priority monitoring locations, uncertainty calibration metrics, spatial and temporal consistency, and performance relative to the pre-update baseline. All validation results are documented.
351 [BodyText] If the model fails validation, the updated model is not deployed. The step assesses whether failure indicates (a) a need for a larger update batch, in which case the workflow returns to Step 4 with expanded scope; or (b) a fundamental domain shift requiring transition to the ESCALATE state for full retraining. Failure rationale and next steps are documented.
352 [BodyText] If the model passes validation, the validated model is promoted to operational status. The previous operational model is archived but not deleted. Model version records in Pillar B are updated. The step generates comprehensive governance documentation, including trigger rationale from Step 2, decision justification from Step 3, data sources and provenance from Step 4, training configuration from Step 5, calibration parameters from Step 6, and validation results from Step 7. The trigger state machine returns to the OK state.
353 [BodyText] The DEPLOY step draws from Pillar A for acceptance criteria, audit requirements, and rollback procedures, and from Pillar B for model versioning and artifact management. The outputs of this step are a deployed operational model if the model passes validation, or an escalation decision if the model fails validation, along with a complete audit trail. This cycle repeats continuously during site operations, with the frequency of updates determined by the rate of change in site conditions and the availability of new monitoring data. The automated nature ensures that surrogates remain accurate without requiring manual intervention or complete retraining.
354 [Heading3] Update Pathways
355 [BodyText] The framework supports two update pathways selected during Step 3 (DECIDE) based on the nature of the detected degradation. Both pathways proceed through the same Phase 2 sequence (Steps 4–7); the distinction lies in how Step 4 (PREPARE) assembles the update dataset.
356 [BodyText] Pathway A (Observation-Driven Update) is appropriate when recent field observations provide sufficient new information to correct the surrogate without additional physics simulations. This pathway offers faster turnaround, because no physics simulation wait time is required, and lower computational cost. It is appropriate when degradation is due to gradual drift within the existing parameter space and relies on the assumption that field observations adequately represent the conditions causing degradation. The workflow proceeds from Steps 1–3 through Step 4 (compile observations) to Steps 5–7.
357 [BodyText] Pathway B (Simulation-Driven Update) is appropriate when the surrogate has encountered conditions outside its training domain and targeted physics simulations are needed to generate appropriate training data. This pathway requires longer turnaround due to physics simulation execution and incurs higher computational cost. It is appropriate when degradation is due to operational changes such as new wells or changed boundary conditions, or regime shifts. Active learning ensures simulations target the most informative conditions. The workflow proceeds from Steps 1–3 through Step 4 (active learning plus physics runs) to Steps 5–7.
358 [BodyText] The choice between pathways is a governance decision made in Step 3 based on the evidence and operational context.

403 [Heading3] Operational Considerations
404 [BodyText] The continuous monitoring phase (Steps 1–3) operates on a schedule aligned with data availability and site-specific needs. Automated diagnostics run whenever new observations become available, which may be daily or weekly depending on the monitoring network. Summary evaluations are compiled periodically, typically monthly or quarterly, for management review, and formal decision points are evaluated on a predictable schedule, typically quarterly, for governance review.
405 [BodyText] The triggered response phase (Steps 4–7) is activated only when needed. Quarterly evaluation to determine whether updates are warranted is typical, with a minimum persistence period of 60–90 days of sustained degradation required before the UPDATE state is reached. The framework takes a conservative approach in which updates are performed only when necessary to maintain stability.
406 [BodyText] After Step 7 completes, whether through successful deployment or escalation, the workflow returns to Step 1 and continues the monitoring cycle. The framework operates continuously throughout the site’s operational lifetime, adapting the surrogate as conditions evolve while maintaining governance oversight and audit trails.

409 [Heading2] Governance Essentials 
410 [BodyText] This section defines the governance framework controlling how DL surrogate models are developed, evaluated, monitored, and updated for long-term groundwater remediation at EM sites. The purpose of this governance pillar is to ensure that surrogate modeling is scientifically defensible, operationally stable, and aligned with existing regulatory and quality-assurance expectations, while allowing efficient scenario analysis and adaptive planning over multi-year remediation timelines.
411 [BodyText] The governance framework is designed to be applicable across all EM programs and sites, and is grounded in established regulatory and quality standards, including EPA CERCLA guidance, DOE quality assurance orders, and EPA modeling and data quality manuals. Within this framework, surrogate models are treated as decision-support tools that primarily support screening-level analyses, planning, and scenario exploration, while high-fidelity physics-based numerical models remain the authoritative basis for final regulatory and remedy decisions.
412 [Heading3] Regulatory Alignment and Quality Assurance Foundations
413 [BodyText] Governance is anchored in federal environmental modeling and quality-assurance guidance. Under CERCLA, remedial decisions must remain protective of human health and the environment over long operational horizons, and analytical tools used to support these decisions must be transparent, defensible, and periodically evaluated (EPA 2001) . The framework adopts principles from EPA environmental modeling guidance (EPA 2009), which emphasizes clear documentation of model purpose, assumptions, uncertainty, validation, and limitations before models are relied on for decision support. These principles directly inform the governance requirements for surrogate use, update triggers, and performance acceptance.
414 [BodyText] At the current prototyping and drafting stage, governance for surrogate modeling activities is aligned with DOE EM-QA-001 (DOE EM 2019), which incorporates ASME NQA-1 standards and applies them to environmental management activities, including software and modeling systems. DOE EM-QA-001 establishes a management system requiring defined acceptance criteria, performance monitoring, corrective action, and continuous improvement for analytical tools used in mission-critical applications.
415 [BodyText] These regulatory and quality standards establish the expectation that surrogate models must be managed as controlled analytical software, with formal verification, validation, documentation, and configuration management processes that support regulatory confidence and long-term stewardship.
416 [Heading3] Model Usage Scope
417 [BodyText] A central governance principle is the explicit definition of how surrogate models may be used within EM decision workflows. Surrogate models are intended primarily to support screening-level analyses, including rapid evaluation of alternative remediation scenarios, sensitivity analyses, uncertainty exploration, and preliminary planning studies. In these contexts, surrogates provide substantial value by enabling fast exploration of large scenario spaces that would be impractical using high-fidelity numerical models alone.
418 [BodyText] For formal regulatory or final remedy decisions, surrogate model outputs are not used as standalone evidence. Instead, surrogate results may inform and prioritize analyses, guide the selection of scenarios for further evaluation, or support interpretation of system behavior, while final decisions remain grounded in high-fidelity physics-based modeling and empirical data. This distinction aligns with EPA remedial investigation and feasibility study guidance (EPA 1989), which emphasizes that models must be used in a manner consistent with their purpose and limitations
419 [BodyText] Governance therefore ensures that surrogate models function as advisory, efficiency-enhancing tools, rather than replacements for established numerical simulators. Any application of surrogate outputs beyond screening-level support requires explicit documentation of assumptions, uncertainty, and corroboration with physics-based results, consistent with EPA modeling guidance and DOE quality expectations.
420 [Heading3] Acceptance Criteria and Recalibration Triggers
421 [BodyText] The governance framework establishes predefined acceptance criteria and recalibration triggers that determine when surrogate models are considered reliable and when updates are warranted. These criteria are defined prior to deployment and are based on project-specific data quality objectives and decision needs, consistent with EPA quality assurance guidance for modeling applications (EPA 2002).
422 [BodyText] Acceptance criteria define the quantitative performance standards a surrogate model must satisfy to be considered fit for operational use. They represent the bar the model must clear before deployment and after updates. Typical acceptance criteria include error thresholds at priority monitoring locations (for example, RMSE below a site-specific limit), calibration of uncertainty estimates such that prediction interval coverage probability remains near 90%, spatial and temporal consistency between surrogate predictions and physics-model reference simulations, and absence of systematic bias in predicted concentration trends at compliance boundaries. Acceptance criteria are evaluated during initial deployment validation (Step 7 of the workflow) and again after every recalibration cycle to confirm that the updated surrogate remains fit for purpose.
423 [BodyText] Recalibration triggers are defined to detect meaningful degradation in surrogate performance or applicability, including sustained increases in prediction residuals, inflation or miscalibration of uncertainty estimates, and documented changes in boundary conditions or operational regimes that move the system outside the surrogate’s original training domain. These triggers are evaluated using objective, documented thresholds rather than ad hoc judgment, consistent with DOE quality assurance requirements for corrective action when performance expectations are not met (DOE EM 2019). 
424 [BodyText] Importantly, the presence of a recalibration trigger does not automatically mandate recalibration. Instead, governance requires formal justification that an update is necessary, proportional, and expected to improve performance. This conservative approach aligns with best practices for model lifecycle management and reduces the risk of destabilizing otherwise reliable surrogate model behavior.

442 [Heading3] Transparency, Auditability, and Configuration Control
443 [BodyText] To support regulatory confidence and long-term stewardship, the governance framework requires full transparency and auditability for surrogate model deployment and updates. All surrogate model versions are subject to formal configuration control, including version tracking, documentation of training data and assumptions, and preservation of prior approved models.
444 [BodyText] Each recalibration or significant update produces a documented governance record that includes the trigger rationale, data sources used, evaluation against acceptance criteria, and verification results. These records are retained as part of the project’s quality assurance documentation and support independent review or audit when required.
445 [BodyText] Data provenance is also explicitly governed. Monitoring data, numerical model outputs, and surrogate training datasets are tracked with metadata describing source, validation status, and applicability. This ensures that the influence of any dataset on surrogate behavior can be reconstructed, supporting traceability and corrective action if data quality concerns arise (EPA 2002).
446 [BodyText] Rollback is defined within this framework as the formal withdrawal of a surrogate model from operational use, rather than reversion to a previously approved surrogate version. Rollback is invoked when an attempted incremental update fails to meet predefined acceptance criteria, indicating that the surrogate’s training domain or structure is no longer scientifically valid.
447 [BodyText] Under a rollback condition, neither the updated surrogate nor the prior surrogate is permitted for continued screening or planning analyses, and surrogate-based decision support is therefore explicitly suspended. In addition, analytical support reverts exclusively to the high-fidelity physics-based numerical model, and rollback automatically triggers escalation to a governed full retraining process. 
448 [BodyText] Full retraining develops a new surrogate baseline from scratch using updated physics-model ensembles, revised assumptions, and refreshed validation datasets. It should be conducted under configuration control and subject to the same verification, validation, uncertainty calibration, and acceptance criteria as an initial deployment. The retrained surrogate is promoted to operational use only after all governance requirements are satisfied.
449 [BodyText] This definition of rollback reflects DOE expectations for conservative software lifecycle management in high-consequence environmental applications. Incremental updates are preferred when scientifically defensible but continued use of any surrogate is not permitted once validity is in question. By redefining rollback as withdrawal and retraining rather than version reversion, the framework prevents unmanaged risk and preserves regulatory confidence in long-term remediation planning.
450 [Heading3] Governance Essentials Summary
451 [BodyText] In summary, the governance framework defined in this section ensures that DL surrogate models are integrated into EM groundwater remediation workflows in a manner that is controlled, transparent, and regulator-aligned. Governance is grounded in established EPA and DOE quality assurance standards, clearly limits surrogate use to screening- and planning-level decision support, and preserves the primacy of high-fidelity physics-based numerical models for final regulatory and remedy decisions. Objective acceptance criteria, documented recalibration triggers, and formal configuration control ensure that surrogate models are used only within their validated domain. When surrogate validity is in question, governance procedures require explicit withdrawal of surrogate use and escalation to a controlled full retraining process, preventing unmanaged risk. Collectively, this governance foundation enables efficient use of advanced modeling tools to support long-term remediation planning while maintaining scientific rigor, operational stability, and regulatory confidence.
452 [Heading2] Modeling Engines 
453 [BodyText] This section describes the physics-based numerical models and DL surrogates that form Pillar B of the recalibration framework. Physics models provide high-fidelity reference simulations, while surrogates provide fast inference for screening and scenario analysis.
454 [Heading3] Site Challenges and Modeling Needs
455 [BodyText] Numerical groundwater and reactive transport models provide the high‑fidelity representation of site‑specific subsurface processes that underpin Pillar B. At EM sites, these models describe how contaminants move and transform in complex hydrogeologic settings, and how engineered controls, such as pump‑and‑treat systems, can influence plume evolution. 
456 [BodyText] The physical phenomena represented typically include advection and dispersion of dissolved species, sorption onto solid phases, and associated reaction processes (Genuchten 2016; Sethi and Di Molfetta 2019), and non-Fickian plume spreading arising from heterogeneity and diffusion-controlled mass exchange between transmissive and low-permeability zones (Guo et al. 2021). Back diffusion from fine grained or low-permeability units is recognized as a major control on plume persistence after source removal (Yang et al. 2016, 2017). Density-driven flow occurs in systems impacted by dense nonaqueous phase liquid / light nonaqueous phase liquid (Huling 1991; McCaulou 1995; Newell 1995) or by seawater intrusion (Werner et al. 2013; Xu et al. 2019). Engineered operations – pump and treat (P&T) wells, injection/extraction networks, permeable reactive barriers, and hydraulic barriers – must also be represented, enabling planners to evaluate how alternative configurations and engineered controls affect plume pathways and concentrations at compliance points. 
457 [Heading3] Physics-Based Numerical Models
458 [BodyText] Numerical models are indispensable for EM stewardship because the central questions are inherently predictive and counterfactual: Decision-makers need to understand how plumes will evolve over decades under alternative remediation strategies, monitoring designs, and future forcing scenarios (e.g., pumping schedules, land use change, climate variability) questions that observation alone cannot resolve (Neuman et al. 2003). Analytical approximations cannot capture the coupled effects of aquifer heterogeneity, complex geochemistry, and engineered infrastructure (Bear and Cheng 2010; Zheng and Wang 1999). High-fidelity simulators fill this gap by enabling systematic exploration of scenarios and assumptions, quantifying potential outcomes before implementation, and supporting design and regulatory decisions with transparent, physics-based predictions. 482 [BodyText] Translating these simulators into credible site predictions requires calibration: Hydraulic conductivities, recharge rates, boundary conditions, and transport parameters must be adjusted until simulated heads and concentrations adequately reproduce field observations. 483 [BodyText] Despite their fidelity, these simulators impose significant computational constraints. Realistic EM applications typically involve three-dimensional (3D), heterogeneous domains, long simulation horizons, and tight numerical tolerances to preserve mass balance and stability, so individual forward runs can take from hours to days on standard computing resources (Hammond et al. 2014; Maxwell et al. 2015). Many of the planning tasks, however, require ensembles of simulations, Monte Carlo realizations of hydrogeologic variability, scenario sweeps over remediation designs, or sensitivity analyses with respect to uncertain parameters (Rubin 2003). The number of model evaluations needed for robust decision support can easily reach the hundreds or thousands. As a result, it became impractical for planners to interactively explore design space or to fully propagate uncertainty using only the high-fidelity model for large complex sites. These limitations motivate the data-driven surrogate modeling engines that sit on top of the numerical model.

494 [BodyText] Family 1: Convolutional Encoder-Decoder Models
495 [BodyText] Convolutional encoder-decoder architectures such as U-Net and residual network variants treat spatial fields as images and learn direct mappings from input fields, including hydraulic conductivity, boundary conditions, and pumping patterns, to output fields such as hydraulic heads, salinity distributions, and plume extents. Convolutions preserve interfaces, sharp fronts, and channelized structures, making these networks well suited for high-resolution spatial prediction. Representative applications include attention U-Net architectures for steady-state hydraulic head fields (Taccari et al. 2022), image-to-image regression for multiphase flow (Mo et al. 2019), autoregressive residual U-Net for dynamic multiphase flow and solute transport (Jiang et al. 2021), and U-Net surrogates for transient groundwater flow inverse problems (Lauzon 2024) and P&T optimization (Song et al. 2025).
496 [BodyText] Family 2: Operator-Learning Models
497 [BodyText] Architectures such as Fourier neural operator (FNO), U-FNO, and deep operator network (DeepONet) learn the solution operator of a partial differential equation in function space rather than at fixed grid points, yielding resolutioninvariant surrogates that generalize across boundary and parameter configurations (Li et al. 2020). Notable subsurface applications include U-FNO for CO2-water multiphase flow (Wen et al. 2022), DeepONet for confined aquifer systems (Taccari et al. 2024), the GeoFUSE framework for seawater intrusion achieving approximately 105speedup per inference relative to PFLOTRAN (Jiang et al. 2025), and physics-informed U-FNO for climate stress testing at the Savannah River Site (Wang et al. 2022).
498 [BodyText] Family 3: Sequence Models
499 [BodyText] LSTM, gated recurrent unit (GRU), and Transformer variants are designed for low-dimensional time-series outputs such as breakthrough curves and well responses monitored at high frequency. These architectures capture long-range temporal dependencies essential for forecasting contaminant evolution and groundwater level trends. Applications include GRU-based surrogates for groundwater levels and water quality (Chen et al. 2021), encoder-decoder LSTM for contaminant source identification (Anshuman and Eldho 2023), and DSCNN (deep separable convolutional neural network)-GRU surrogates for transient groundwater head fields (Li et al. 2025).
500 [BodyText] Family 4: Generative Models
501 [BodyText] Variational autoencoder (VAE), generative adversarial network (GAN), and diffusion architectures sample entire ensembles of plausible states and parameter fields consistent with physics and observations, making them natural tools for probabilistic inversion and UQ. Applications include VAE and GAN for facies realizations and Bayesian inversion (Graham and Chen 2020), diffusion models for multivariate subsurface property fields (Miele and Linde 2025), pretrained diffusion models for porous-media flow, and conditional diffusion for CO2 storage plume forecasting (Wang et al. 2025).
502 [BodyText] Family 5: Classical Regression Surrogates 
503 [BodyText] Classical regression and statistical surrogates including Gaussian processes, Kriging, and shallow artificial neural networks remain powerful for low-dimensional mappings from design variables to scalar metrics such as total cost, cleanup time, or risk index. These methods are especially valuable when training data are limited and interpretability is important. The literature includes comprehensive reviews of surrogate models in groundwater applications (Asher et al. 2015), reduced-dimensional Gaussian process regression for groundwater allocation (Siade et al. 2020), Gaussian process surrogates for surfactant-enhanced aquifer remediation (Shams et al. 2021), and advances in Gaussian process surrogate construction and validation (Marrel and Iooss 2024).

509 [BodyText] Across the five families, several training practices recur in literature. Training datasets are generated by sampling parameter and boundary condition ranges, commonly using Latin hypercube or quasi-random designs, and running the corresponding high-fidelity simulations to produce input-output pairs (Asher et al. 2015; Razavi et al. 2012). The number of simulations required varies by problem complexity and surrogate architecture but generally falls in the range of hundreds to low thousands of realizations (Mo et al. 2019; Wen et al. 2022; Zhu and Zabaras 2018). Input fields that span orders of magnitude, such as hydraulic conductivity, are typically log-transformed prior to training (Tang et al. 2020), and all features are standardized to stabilize optimization and ensure consistent inference (Mo et al. 2019). Hold-out realizations spanning distinct parameter ranges or time windows are reserved for testing to guard against overfitting (Jiang et al. 2025; Wen et al. 2022). 
510 [BodyText] A growing number of studies incorporate physics-informed loss terms, embedding partial differential equation residuals or conservation constraints alongside data-driven objectives to improve generalization and physical consistency, particularly in sparse-data or extrapolation regimes (Xu and Zhang 2024; Zhu et al. 2019). For transient applications, autoregressive rollout strategies feed the surrogate’s own predictions back as inputs for subsequent time steps, requiring careful attention to error accumulation during both training and validation (Jiang et al. 2021). Most studies report runtime comparisons between the full simulator and the trained surrogate alongside accuracy metrics to quantify practical speedup (Taccari et al. 2022).
511 [Heading3] Modeling Engine Summary
512 [BodyText] The modeling engine layer described in this section links site-specific physics to fast surrogate inference. Site challenges and modeling needs (Section 3.2.1) establish the subsurface processes that must be captured, including advection, dispersion, sorption, back diffusion, density-driven flow, and engineered operations. Physics-based numerical models (Section 3.2.2) encode these processes in high-fidelity simulators that serve as the authoritative reference for Pillar B, but impose computational costs that preclude interactive scenario exploration, particularly for large, complex sites where robust decision support may require hundreds to thousands of forward evaluations. The surrogate model landscape (Section 3.2.3) addresses this bottleneck through five families of data-driven emulators, each matched to a distinct input-output structure and decision use-case, with cross-cutting training practices drawn from the reviewed literature. Together, the numerical simulators supply training data, physical grounding, and validation targets, while the surrogates reproduce key results at a fraction of the computational cost. The recalibration mechanisms described in Section 3.3 govern how these surrogates are kept aligned with evolving site conditions and new observations over time.
513 [Heading2] Recalibration Mechanisms 
514 [BodyText] The recalibration component operates as a single, governed loop designed for stability, transparency, and regulatory credibility. During routine operations, the surrogate remains unchanged; the team conducts monthly monitoring to track accuracy relative to the most recent stable baseline and calibration of stated uncertainty. Operational changes (e.g., revisions to pumping schedules or boundary conditions) are logged because they can move inputs outside the surrogate’s original training setting. 
515 [BodyText] On a fixed decision window (e.g., quarterly), the program determines whether an update is justified. Action is taken only when evidence of drift has persisted for 60–90 days or when a documented regime change has occurred. When action is justified, the framework does not undertake a whole retrain; instead, it executes a small, targeted update supported by a limited number of physics simulations and the most recent field observations. The revised surrogate is then evaluated against pre-registered acceptance criteria, including accuracy, uncertainty coverage, and performance at priority wells or zones. Throughout, the high-fidelity physics model remains the authoritative reference used to inform and validate changes.

548 [Heading3] Trigger Logic
549 [BodyText] As surrogate models operate over time, data distributions, and environmental conditions evolve. A trigger logic framework activates model recalibration to maintain model trustworthiness and regulatory compliance. The framework combines statistical thresholds with domain-informed criteria to support adaptive learning while maintaining model interpretability and performance.
550 [BodyText] The drift monitoring framework consists of multiple complementary metrics organized into four categories (Table 6). By combining complementary signals that capture loss of dynamical stability, increasing model uncertainty, and structural changes in data and learned representations, the framework allows early, interpretable, and statistically robust detection of regime drift to support timely model recalibration.
551 [BodyText] Category 1: Tipping Point-Inspired Resilience Metrics
552 [BodyText] These metrics capture early signs of loss of dynamical stability, motivated by concepts from tipping point science. They are applied to model outputs, residuals, and latent representations, without implying the existence of physical tipping points in the underlying system. Rolling-window estimates of variance and lag-1 autocorrelation are used to detect increasing fluctuation amplitude and memory, consistent with reduced resilience. Higher-order moments, including skewness and kurtosis, are monitored to identify asymmetric error growth and heavy-tailed behavior indicative of excursions toward unstable regions. 
553 [BodyText] Category 2: Performance and Uncertainty-Based Metrics
554 [BodyText] These metrics focus on direct indicators of model reliability under deployment. Prediction residuals are monitored using adaptive thresholds derived from rolling statistics, such as exceedance of multiples of the rolling standard deviation. Persistent threshold violations or clustered spatial–temporal error patterns are treated as evidence of localized or systemic degradation.
555 [BodyText] For probabilistic models, predictive uncertainty is explicitly tracked using measures such as variance or entropy. Sustained increases relative to historical baselines are interpreted as growing epistemic uncertainty and declining confidence in model predictions. 
556 [BodyText] Category 3: Distributional and Representation-Level Drift Detection
557 [BodyText] These diagnostics target statistical and structural changes in data and learned features that may not immediately manifest in errors or uncertainty. Distributional drift is assessed by comparing incoming input and output distributions to reference distributions using nonparametric statistical tests, such as the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Statistically significant deviations indicate changes in the data-generating process that may compromise model validity.
558 [BodyText] In parallel, drift indicators are applied in learned representation spaces, such as latent embeddings from diffusion models or bottleneck features from convolutional architectures. Deviations from the training manifold in latent space provide early detection of regime mismatch in high-dimensional settings where marginal statistics may be insufficient.
559 [BodyText] Category 4: Distributional and Representation-Level Drift Detection
560 [BodyText] The final category integrates all diagnostic signals into a robust decision-making layer. Rather than relying on single-metric thresholds, the framework employs Bayesian inference to combine evidence from resilience, performance, uncertainty, and distributional indicators with prior knowledge and alternative explanations (e.g., data quality changes).
561 [BodyText] Model recalibration or adaptation is triggered through composite gating mechanisms, implemented either as Boolean rules (requiring concurrent exceedance across multiple metric families) or as score-based aggregation into a unified drift risk index. This integration reduces false positives due to noise or transient anomalies and ensures that interventions are driven by consistent, multi-source evidence.

613 [BodyText] Curate a specific subset of indicators from the broader regime drift metrics that are physically and statistically relevant to subsurface flow and transport dynamics.

615 [BodyText] Subject candidates to rigorous stress testing using synthetic test cases derived from physics-based model simulations.
616 [BodyText] Scenario generation. A suite of test cases is generated representing common subsurface regime shifts, such as rapid aquifer depletion, river water and groundwater exchange fronts, or changes in recharge patterns. These simulations explicitly model the transition from stable to unstable regimes.
617 [BodyText] Reliability analysis. The selected candidates are applied to these simulations to quantify their sensitivity (true positive rate) and specificity (true negative rate).

624 [Heading3] Continual Learning 
625 [BodyText] Continual learning for scientific surrogate models focuses on updating a model in response to gradual system change while preserving valid historical behavior. The framework employs a conservative, stability-focused approach that fits DOE operational requirements.
626 [BodyText] Mature continual learning approaches fall into two widely adopted categories: (1) regularization-based techniques, which constrain parameter updates to avoid overwriting previously important knowledge, and (2) replay-based techniques, which maintain stability by including a curated set of historical samples during updating. Elastic weight consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017) is a prototypical regularization strategy, penalizing deviations from weights identified as influential in previous training. Replay-based strategies are widely applied across engineering and environmental forecasting domains to reduce the risk of catastrophic forgetting when conditions shift incrementally (De Lange et al. 2021). 
627 [BodyText] Within the environmental modeling literature, sequential fine-tuning has repeatedly been shown to outperform static surrogates in non-stationary systems such as energy load forecasting (Hurtado et al. 2023) and long-horizon operational forecasting (Sayed et al. 2025). These same studies emphasize that frequent retraining is unnecessary – and often destabilizing – when well-designed continual learning mechanisms are in place. This aligns with findings in hydrologic surrogate modeling, where modest periodic updates informed by new hydrologic states and compact replay buffers maintain performance while avoiding the computational cost of full retraining (Asher et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2023). 
628 [BodyText] Fine-tuning is initiated only after documented indicators of drift demonstrate meaningful degradation – such as sustained increases in predictive error, divergence between modeled and observed temporal trends, or reduced empirical coverage of uncertainty intervals (e.g., under coverage in UQ metrics). Once triggered, the update process proceeds through several structured steps. 
629 [BodyText] Step 1: Assemble update dataset. A compact update batch is assembled from three sources: (1) targeted physics simulations representing the changed regime, (2) recent validated field observations, and (3) a replay sample of historical cases comprising 70–75% of the batch to mitigate catastrophic forgetting, consistent with replay ratios shown effective in continual learning applications (De Lange et al. 2021).
630 [BodyText] Step 2: Selective Layer Updates. Early encoder layers are frozen to preserve structural representations learned during original training, while later layers are updated using a reduced learning rate and a regularization penalty. The penalty may take the form of elastic weight consolidation, which protects weights identified as influential during prior training (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017), or L2 anchoring toward the pre-update parameter values, following recommendations from continual learning studies in dynamic engineering systems (Hurtado et al. 2023).
631 [BodyText] Step 3: Validation and Early Stopping. Validation performance is monitored on a held-out set that includes both recent observations and replayed historical cases. Metrics tracked include RMSE at priority wells, empirical coverage of predictive intervals, and spatial error patterns across the model domain. Training is stopped once improvements plateau over successive evaluation cycles to prevent overfitting to the update batch (Sayed et al. 2025).
632 [BodyText] Step 4: Uncertainty Recalibration. Predictive uncertainty using held-out data or ensemble-based UQ methods ensure that nominal confidence intervals align with empirical coverage. This step applies the surrogate-level UQ methods described in Section 3.3.1, such as deep ensembles or conformal prediction, to verify that the updated model’s uncertainty estimates remain calibrated rather than overconfident in the new regime (Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017; Zhu and Zabaras 2018).
633 [BodyText] Step 5: Governance Review. A governance package needs to be prepared following the configuration control and acceptance criteria defined in Section 3.1. The package includes pre- and post-update performance metrics, the drift indicators that triggered the update, and updated uncertainty diagnostics. The revised surrogate is promoted to operational status only if all predefined acceptance criteria are satisfied; otherwise, the update is rejected and the system retains the prior approved model pending further evaluation.

Each candidate is scored using uncertainty and error metrics, including predictive variance, credible interval width, and residual patterns relative to recent field observations, consistent with methods applied in hydrologic surrogate refinement (Settles 2009; Wang et al. 2023).
653 [BodyText] Step 2: Apply Diversity Constraints and Select Batch. Diversity constraints are applied to ensure that selected scenarios span hydraulic gradients, hydrostratigraphic units, and forcing conditions, preventing clustering in redundant regions of the input space and enhancing surrogate generalization (Wen et al. 2021). A series of high-value simulations selected to collectively maximize expected information gain.
654 [BodyText] Step 3: Execute and Document. Selected simulations are executed using the high-fidelity physics models described in Section 3.2.2. Each simulation is accompanied by a governance record documenting the selection rationale, the drift indicators or uncertainty patterns that motivated inclusion, and the expected contribution to surrogate improvement.
655 [BodyText] Step 4: Integrate with Continual Learning. Completed simulations results are incorporated directly into the fine-tuning batch described in Section 3.3.3, where they supplement the replay buffer and recent field observations. This coupling ensures that active learning and continual learning operate as coordinated mechanisms within the recalibration loop rather than as independent processes. 
656 [Heading3] Multi-fidelity Data Fusion
657 [BodyText] Multi-fidelity modeling enhances surrogate accuracy and computational efficiency by combining information across sources of varying cost and resolution. In groundwater flow and reactive transport applications, fidelity levels correspond to differences in grid resolution, process representation, or data source. Low-fidelity sources – such as coarse-grid PFLOTRAN or MODFLOW simulations with simplified boundary conditions or reduced-process representations – are inexpensive to generate and provide broad characterization of system variability across the parameter and operational space. Highfidelity sources – including fine-grid simulations with full reactive transport chemistry, locally refined meshes around critical features such as source zones or compliance boundaries, and validated field observations – are computationally expensive or limited in number but anchor predictions to physically accurate behavior where precise results are required. 
658 [BodyText] By combining large low-fidelity ensembles with targeted high-fidelity data, the framework covers a broad parameter space at low cost while preserving localized accuracy, yielding strong surrogate performance with orders-of-magnitude reduction in total simulation cost compared to relying on high-fidelity data alone. The fusion strategies described below provide the mathematical machinery for integrating these fidelity levels into a unified surrogate prediction (Asher et al. 2015).

676 [BodyText] The choice among these strategies depends on the dimensionality of the problem, the cost ratio between fidelity levels, and the degree of nonlinearity in the fidelity gap. Co-Kriging and autoregressive Gaussian process models are well-suited for moderate-dimensional problems where the relationship between fidelity levels is approximately linear. Neural discrepancy models and physics-aware networks accommodate stronger nonlinearities and higher-dimensional fields, while transfer learning approaches are particularly practical when large low-fidelity ensembles already exist from prior modeling campaign (Asher et al. 2015; Xu and Zhang 2024).
677 [BodyText] The multi-fidelity fusion procedure integrates coarse simulations, fine simulations, and field data through a structured workflow.
678 [BodyText] Step 1: Fidelity Alignment. Identify fidelity levels and align datasets, ensuring spatial, temporal, and unit consistency across all sources.
679 [BodyText] Step 2: Low-Fidelity Pre-training. Pre-train surrogate models on low-fidelity simulations, exploiting their broad coverage of the parameter and operational space to establish global structure.
680 [BodyText] Step 3: High-Fidelity Correction. Introduce high-fidelity data, whether from simulation or observation, to train a discrepancy model or co-Kriging correction layer. Predictions inherit low-fidelity global structure while achieving high-fidelity local accuracy.
681 [BodyText] Step 4: Fidelity-Conditioned Uncertainty. Quantify uncertainty conditioned on the available fidelity at each prediction location, expanding intervals where only low-fidelity information is available and narrowing them where high-fidelity evidence supports confidence.
682 [BodyText] Step 5: Validation and Integration. Validate fused predictions against withheld high-fidelity runs or independent observational periods to confirm improved performance over single-fidelity surrogates. Integrate the fused surrogate into the continual learning loop described in Section 3.3.3, supporting more accurate drift detection, better-informed active learning selection, and more stable fine-tuning updates.
683 [BodyText] Multi-fidelity methods integrate naturally with active learning and continual learning. When active learning (Section 3.3.4) identifies high-uncertainty regions, the framework can strategically deploy either low-fidelity or high-fidelity simulations based on the expected information gain and available computational budget. Low-fidelity runs can be used to rapidly screen candidate regions before committing expensive high-fidelity resources to the most informative configurations. The fused surrogate provides improved baseline predictions that enhance drift detection sensitivity and reduce false-positive recalibration triggers, supporting the conservative update philosophy established in the governance framework (Section 3.1).
684 [Heading3] Full Retraining
685 [BodyText] Full retraining constructs a new surrogate baseline from scratch when the existing model is no longer representative of the physical system or operational domain. Whereas continual learning (Section 3.3.3) applies targeted, incremental corrections to an otherwise valid surrogate, full retraining acknowledges that the original training domain has been fundamentally superseded and that no sequence of fine-tuning updates can restore reliable performance. This mechanism serves as the final safeguard in the recalibration framework, providing a clean, traceable reset that aligns with DOE expectations for lifecycle documentation and reproducibility in high-consequence environmental applications.
686 [BodyText] Full retraining is warranted when the drift diagnostics and trigger logic described in Section 3.3.2 indicate that incremental updates can no longer restore surrogate performance, or when external changes to the modeling system itself render the original training domain obsolete. Specific conditions include fundamental regime shifts or major forcing changes that move the system outside the original training domain, redefinition of the underlying physics model through revised geologic interpretations or updated reaction networks, major reconfiguration of pumping infrastructure or boundary conditions, recalibration of the numerical model after incorporation of new hydrogeologic data, and repeated fine-tuning failures as described in Section 3.3.3. The concept of drift literature reinforces that full retraining is appropriate when foundational assumptions underlying the surrogate no longer hold.
687 [BodyText] The retraining process proceeds through a formal sequence of steps consistent with the structured workflows established in Sections 3.3.3 through 3.3.5.
688 [BodyText] Step 1: Verify Retraining Triggers. Verify that one or more of the conditions listed above has been documented through the governance and monitoring procedures described in Sections 3.1 and 3.3.2. This step ensures that full retraining is initiated based on objective evidence rather than precautionary judgment alone.
689 [BodyText] Step 2: Redesign the Training Domain. Define updated ranges of hydrofacies, operational scenarios, and boundary forcings, and parameter distributions consistent with the new conceptual understanding. This redesign reflects the changed system state and any revisions to the underlying physics model, ensuring that the new training ensemble spans the conditions the surrogate will encounter during its next operational period.
690 [BodyText] Step 3: Ensemble Generation. Generate a new ensemble of high-fidelity simulations covering the redesigned training domain. Where appropriate, supplement with low-fidelity runs using the multi-fidelity strategies described in Section 3.3.5 to maintain broad parameter-space coverage while managing computational cost. Recent field observations are integrated to anchor the surrogate to current empirical system behavior.
691 [BodyText] Step 4: Surrogate Training and Uncertainty Calibration. Train a new surrogate from scratch, applying the architecture selection, preprocessing, and training practices described in Section 3.2.3. UQ is calibrated using the methods described in Section 3.3.1 to ensure that predictive intervals are well calibrated from initial deployment.
692 [BodyText] Step 5: Validation and Acceptance. Validate the retrained surrogate against withheld high-fidelity simulations and independent observational periods. Performance is evaluated against the predefined acceptance criteria established in Section 3.1.3, including accuracy at priority wells, empirical coverage of uncertainty intervals, and consistency with known physical behavior. The retrained surrogate is promoted to operational use only after all acceptance criteria are satisfied.
693 [BodyText] Full retraining is conducted under the configuration control, acceptance criteria, and archival procedures defined in Sections 3.1.3 and 3.1.5. The retraining governance package follows the same verification, validation, and acceptance review requirements as an initial deployment, with the additional requirement of documenting the specific trigger rationale and archiving the retired surrogate and all associated artifacts.

695 [Heading1] Implementation Roadmap
696 [BodyText] This section describes how the three-pillar framework (Section 2.1) and seven-step workflow (Section 2.2) translate into an operational system. Rather than prescribing specific software tools or platforms, we focus on what capabilities are needed and how they should be developed over time. The goal is to provide a roadmap that can guide implementation while remaining flexible to site-specific constraints and evolving technology.
697 [BodyText] The implementation must satisfy two demands. First, it must support the scientific workflow of monitoring, detecting drift, and executing recalibration when needed. Second, it must meet DOE governance requirements for traceability, reproducibility, and quality assurance. The design outlined here attempts to balance both demands while maintaining consistency with the framework architecture established in Section 2.0.
698 [Heading2] System Requirements
699 [Heading3] Operational Modes
700 [BodyText] The system operates in two distinct modes – offline and online – that place different demands on the architecture and reflect different phases of the surrogate lifecycle.
701 [BodyText] Offline mode encompasses initial surrogate development: generating training ensembles with the physics model, training the surrogate, calibrating uncertainty estimates, and validating against held-out data. This phase is computationally intensive, potentially requiring thousands of physics runs and graphics processing unit (GPU)-hours of training, but is not time-critical. Offline work typically occurs on high-performance computing (HPC) resources with batch scheduling. In terms of the framework, offline mode establishes the initial Pillar B modeling engines before operational deployment. The methods described in Section 3.2 (physics-based models and surrogate architectures) and Section 3.3.1 (UQ) are applied during this phase to produce a validated surrogate ready for deployment.
702 [BodyText] Online mode is the operational phase where the trained surrogate supports decision-making. The surrogate generates predictions on demand, the monitoring system tracks performance through the seven-step workflow, and recalibration is triggered when needed. Online operations require faster turnaround –  predictions in seconds to minutes, diagnostic updates on a regular schedule such as daily or weekly, and recalibration decisions on a governed timeline such as quarterly review. Online components may run on more modest computing resources, though recalibration itself may require temporary access to HPC for new physics runs. The methods from Section 3.3.2 (trigger logic) and Section 3.1 (governance) are continuously active during online operations.
703 [BodyText] The architecture must support smooth transitions between modes. A surrogate developed offline must be deployable online without modification. Data collected during online operations must feed back into offline analysis when full retraining is needed. This bidirectional flow ensures that operational experience improves subsequent surrogate generations.
704 [Heading3] Pillar-Based Capabilities
705 [BodyText] Each pillar of the framework requires specific capabilities for implementation. Understanding these requirements helps ensure that the system architecture supports all aspects of the recalibration framework.
706 [BodyText] Pillar A (Governance) requires infrastructure for configuration management that tracks all surrogate versions with their training data, assumptions, and validation results. The system must maintain audit logging for all decisions, particularly at the DECIDE step, where human judgment authorizes recalibration. Documentation generation capabilities must produce regulatory-compliant records that satisfy DOE quality assurance requirements. Human review interfaces must present diagnostic evidence clearly so that decision-makers can evaluate whether recalibration is warranted. The governance methods detailed in Section 3.1 – including regulatory framework alignment, acceptance criteria, monitoring schedules, and configuration control – define what this infrastructure must support.
707 [BodyText] Pillar B (Modeling Engines) requires a physics model interface capable of communicating with high-fidelity simulators such as PFLOTRAN, MODFLOW, STOMP, or TOUGH2. This interface handles input file generation, job submission including HPC scheduling where needed, output parsing, and result archiving. The system must also provide surrogate training and inference infrastructure that can train neural network architectures (such as those described in Section 3.2.3), store trained models with version control, and generate predictions efficiently during operations. Data management capabilities must handle both simulation outputs and field observations, maintaining provenance for all data used in training or validation.
708 [BodyText] Pillar C (Recalibration Mechanisms) requires infrastructure for UQ computation using the methods described in Section 3.3.1, including MC-Dropout, deep ensembles, or conformal prediction. Drift detection and monitoring capabilities must implement the trigger logic from Section 3.3.2, tracking performance metrics and evaluating drift indicators against governance-defined thresholds. When recalibration is triggered, the system must support active learning for scenario selection (Section 3.3.4) and continual learning for model updates (Section 3.3.3). Multi-fidelity data fusion (Section 3.3.5) and full retraining (Section 3.3.6) capabilities complete the recalibration toolkit.
709 [Heading3] Workflow Support
710 [BodyText] The implementation must support both phases of the seven-step workflow defined in Section 2.2.
711 [BodyText] Phase 1 (Continuous Monitoring) implements Steps 1 through 3 of the workflow. The MONITOR step requires automated comparison of surrogate predictions against field observations, computing residuals and tracking uncertainty metrics over time. The DETECT step evaluates drift indicators against predefined thresholds, distinguishing transient anomalies from persistent degradation using the persistence filtering described in Section 3.3.2. The DECIDE step packages diagnostic evidence for governance review and records the authorization decision. Throughout Phase 1, the system maintains the trigger state logic – OK, WARNING, UPDATE, or ESCALATE – that guides operational decisions.
712 [BodyText] Phase 2 (Triggered Response) activates only when the DECIDE step authorizes an update, implementing Steps 4 through 7. The PREPARE step assembles training data, potentially using active learning to select high-value scenarios for new physics simulations. The UPDATE step fine-tunes surrogate weights using continual learning techniques that preserve previously learned knowledge. The CALIBRATE step ensures that uncertainty estimates remain well-calibrated after the update. The DEPLOY step validates the updated surrogate against acceptance criteria and, if successful, deploys it with full documentation. If validation fails, the system supports rollback procedures as defined in Section 3.1.5.
713 [BodyText] The methods detailed in Section 3.0 provide the technical foundation for all workflow steps. Rather than re-explaining these methods here, the implementation draws on them as needed: Section 3.3.1 for UQ, Section 3.3.2 for trigger logic, Section 3.3.3 for continual learning, Section 3.3.4 for active learning, Section 3.3.5 for multi-fidelity fusion, and Section 3.3.6 for full retraining when incremental updates are insufficient.
714 [Heading2] Human Decision Points
715 [BodyText] Not everything should be automated. The framework includes explicit decision points where human judgment is required, reflecting Pillar A’s emphasis on governance and accountability. The software architecture should make these decision points visible and provide the information needed to make good decisions.
716 [Heading3] Recalibration Approval
717 [BodyText] The most important decision point occurs at Step 3 (DECIDE) of the workflow. Even when automated diagnostics indicate drift, the decision to update a surrogate should involve human review. This is both a governance requirement under DOE quality assurance standards and a practical safeguard against false alarms. The monitoring system provides the evidence – drift metrics, historical trends, uncertainty inflation, and comparison to acceptance criteria – but humans decide whether to proceed with recalibration.
718 [BodyText] The decision options at this point include approving the update and proceeding to Phase 2, deferring pending additional evidence while continuing intensified monitoring, or rejecting with documented justification if the evidence is insufficient or the timing is inappropriate. Each decision must be recorded with rationale for regulatory transparency.
719 [Heading3] Acceptance Criteria Definition
720 [BodyText] Acceptance criteria define what “good enough” means for a given application – the error thresholds, uncertainty coverage requirements, and performance metrics that determine whether a surrogate is fit for purpose. Setting these criteria requires domain expertise that cannot be automated. Humans must understand site-specific risks, regulatory context, and the consequences of prediction errors to define appropriate thresholds.
721 [BodyText] The system evaluates surrogate performance against these criteria, but the criteria themselves emerge from human judgment informed by site characterization, regulatory requirements, and operational experience. Section 3.1.3 describes the types of acceptance criteria (accuracy metrics, uncertainty coverage, spatial and temporal consistency), but the specific threshold values must be determined by qualified personnel for each application.
722 [Heading3] Rollback Decisions
723 [BodyText] When an updated surrogate fails validation at Step 7 (DEPLOY), a rollback decision is required. As defined in Section 3.1.5, rollback means withdrawing the surrogate from operational use and reverting to physics-only analysis – not reverting to a previous surrogate version, since that version has already been flagged as degraded. This is a significant operational change that requires human authorization and documentation.
724 [BodyText] The decision to roll back acknowledges that the recalibration attempt was unsuccessful and that the surrogate cannot currently meet acceptance criteria. This may trigger retraining or, in some cases, a fundamental reassessment of whether a surrogate approach is appropriate for the application.
725 [Heading3] Full Retraining Triggers
726 [BodyText] The most consequential decision arises when incremental updates repeatedly fail or when site conditions change fundamentally. The decision to abandon the current surrogate and retrain from scratch involves judging whether the surrogate’s foundational assumptions remain valid. Perhaps the training distribution no longer represents current site conditions, or perhaps the surrogate architecture is inadequate for the prediction task.
727 [BodyText] Full retraining has major resource implications – potentially thousands of new physics simulations and significant computational effort – and should not be automated. Section 3.3.6 describes the technical process for full retraining, but the decision to initiate it requires human assessment of whether the investment is justified and whether the underlying approach remains sound.
728 [Heading2] Development Phases
729 [BodyText] Implementing the full framework is a multi-year effort. A phased approach delivers value incrementally while building toward the complete vision. Each phase builds on the previous one, with lessons learned informing subsequent development.
730 [Heading3] Phase 1: Prototype Demonstration
731 [BodyText] The immediate goal is to demonstrate that the recalibration concept works by completing one full cycle on a synthetic or simplified test case.
732 [BodyText] The scope of Phase 1 includes developing a single surrogate architecture – such as U-FNO for the two-dimensional (2D) cross-section transport case or U-Net for P&T plume prediction – trained on synthetic physics model outputs. Basic UQ using MC-Dropout or deep ensembles provides predictive uncertainty estimates. Simple threshold-based drift detection identifies when performance has degraded. The recalibration workflow is executed manually: A researcher runs diagnostics, decides an update is needed, generates new training data through targeted physics simulations, fine-tunes the model using continual learning techniques, recalibrates uncertainty estimates, and validates results against acceptance criteria. The work is documented in Jupyter notebooks or Python scripts that capture each step.
733 [BodyText] The key deliverable is a working demonstration showing before-and-after performance when the surrogate is updated in response to simulated drift. This demonstration proves that the seven-step workflow can improve surrogate performance and that the methods from Section 3.0 work together as intended.
734 [BodyText] Success is measured by completing the full MONITOR → DETECT → DECIDE → PREPARE → UPDATE → CALIBRATE → DEPLOY cycle with documented performance improvement. At this phase, there is no persistent software system, just reproducible code that proves the concept.
735 [Heading3] Phase 2: Integrated Framework
736 [BodyText] Building on the prototype, the objective of Phase 2 is to create reusable software components that can operate on realistic site data with governance-compliant documentation.
737 [BodyText] The scope expands to include modular Python packages for each core capability, with documented interfaces and test coverage. These packages can be installed and used independently or together. Automated drift detection runs on a schedule and generates diagnostic reports without manual intervention. A scripted recalibration workflow executes the necessary steps with minimal manual effort once a decision to update has been made. Configuration management tracks model versions, training data provenance, and parameter settings. Multiple surrogate architecture options are supported, allowing comparison of different approaches for a given prediction task.
738 [BodyText] Deliverables include installable packages that implement the framework components, automated diagnostic reports that summarize surrogate performance and drift indicators, and a governance dashboard prototype that presents decision-relevant information for human review. The system produces documentation that meets DOE quality assurance requirements for traceability and reproducibility.
739 [BodyText] Success is measured by the framework operating on representative site data – either from an actual EM site (e.g., Hanford) or from realistic synthetic scenarios – with all governance requirements satisfied. At this phase, the system still requires a technically skilled operator comfortable with Python and commandline tools, but routine operations are largely automated. The operator’s role shifts from executing steps to reviewing outputs and making decisions.
740 [Heading3] Phase 3: Operational System
741 [BodyText] The full vision is a system that can be deployed at an EM site and operated by staff who are not machine learning specialists. The objective is acceptance for operational use under DOE quality assurance requirements.
742 [BodyText] The scope includes a persistent monitoring service that runs continuously, ingests new observations as they arrive, and maintains an up-to-date picture of surrogate performance. A user interface – likely web-based – presents diagnostics, alerts, and decision options in an accessible format so that analysts can review model status, examine historical performance, and initiate recalibration workflows without writing code. The system supports multiple surrogates for different prediction tasks with coordinated monitoring and updating. Integration with site data systems allows observations to flow automatically from field sensors and laboratory results into the monitoring pipeline.
743 [BodyText] Deliverables include a production-quality implementation meeting all DOE software quality requirements, training materials for site operators, and maintenance and support procedures. Full regulatory documentation demonstrates compliance with DOE EM-QA-001 requirements.
744 [BodyText] Success is measured by the framework being accepted for operational use at one or more EM sites, with site staff able to operate the system for routine monitoring and governance personnel able to make informed recalibration decisions based on system outputs. This phase requires significant software engineering effort and should be informed by experience from earlier phases. Specific technology choices should be deferred until requirements are better understood through operational experience.
745 [Heading2] Technical Considerations
746 [Heading3] Computational Requirements
747 [BodyText] The framework spans a wide range of computational demands that must be accommodated by the implementation of the architecture.
748 [BodyText] Physics simulations are the most expensive component. A single PFLOTRAN run for a 2D cross-section case takes minutes; a full 3D site model may take hours or days. Generating training ensembles of hundreds to thousands of runs requires HPC access with batch scheduling. Recalibration updates need fewer runs – typically tens of runs selected by active learning – but still benefit from parallel execution. Surrogate training requires GPU resources for efficient execution. Initial training of a U-FNO or U-Net model may take hours on a modern GPU. Fine-tuning for recalibration is faster, typically minutes to an hour, but still benefits from GPU acceleration. Training can occur on HPC GPU nodes, cloud resources, or dedicated workstations, depending on availability.
749 [BodyText] Inference and monitoring are lightweight by comparison. A trained surrogate generates predictions in milliseconds. Diagnostic calculations – residuals, uncertainty metrics, drift statistics – are similarly fast. These operations can run on standard computing resources without specialized hardware.
750 [BodyText] The architecture should not assume that all components run on the same hardware. Physics simulations may run on one HPC system, training on another, and monitoring on a third. Data must flow between these environments reliably, with appropriate handling of different file systems, network configurations, and security boundaries.
751 [Heading3] Interoperability
752 [BodyText] Simulator coupling presents a practical challenge because physics simulators were not designed with machine learning workflows in mind. The framework must work with simulators as they are, typically through file-based interfaces: generate input files according to simulator specifications, run the simulator as a subprocess or batch job, and parse output files to extract results. This approach is less elegant than API-based coupling but far more practical given the diversity of simulators in use across EM sites. The Physics Model Interface component must accommodate different simulators – PFLOTRAN, MODFLOW, STOMP, TOUGH2, and others – without requiring changes to downstream components.
753 [BodyText] Data formats should follow existing conventions where possible. Simulation outputs are often in domainspecific formats such as PFLOTRAN HDF5 or MODFLOW binary files. The framework should read these directly rather than requiring format conversion that could introduce errors or lose information. For internal data – trained models, diagnostic logs, configuration files – standard formats such as JSON, YAML, and PyTorch checkpoints provide adequate interoperability.
754 [BodyText] Reproducibility requires that every result can be regenerated from archived inputs. This means versioning not just code but also training data, model weights, configuration files, and random seeds. The data management layer must support this requirement without imposing excessive overhead on routine operations. Every prediction should be traceable to the specific surrogate version, training data, and configuration that produced it.
755 [Heading3] Quality Assurance Alignment
756 [BodyText] DOE quality assurance requirements apply to software used in decision support for environmental management. These requirements do not dictate specific technologies but do constrain how the software is developed and operated. The architecture should make compliance straightforward rather than burdensome.
757 [BodyText] Configuration control requires that every deployed component has a defined version, that changes be documented and reviewed before deployment, and that the system be able to report exactly what software versions produced a given result. This applies to surrogate models, monitoring algorithms, and the framework software itself.
758 [BodyText] Verification and validation require that each component be tested against defined acceptance criteria before deployment, with test results documented and retained. For surrogate models, this means validation against properly held-out data and comparison to physics model predictions. For software components, this means unit tests, integration tests, and documented test coverage.
759 [BodyText] Traceability requires that audit logs record what data was used, what computations were performed, and what results were produced. These logs must be retained for the project lifetime – potentially decades for long-term remediation sites. The data management layer must support efficient storage and retrieval of audit information.
760 [BodyText] Corrective action procedures must define how problems are diagnosed, how fixes are implemented, and how effectiveness is verified. When a surrogate fails validation or produces unexpected results, the system must support root cause analysis and documented resolution.
761 [Heading2] Summary
762 [BodyText] This implementation roadmap translates the three-pillar framework and seven-step workflow into operational capabilities through a structured development approach.
763 [BodyText] The system requirements are organized around what each pillar needs for implementation, with clear distinction between offline development and online operations. Pillar A requires governance infrastructure for configuration management, audit logging, and human review interfaces. Pillar B requires physics model interfaces and surrogate training infrastructure. Pillar C requires monitoring, drift detection, and recalibration capabilities. The workflow support ensures that both continuous monitoring (Steps 1-3) and triggered response (Steps 4-7) phases can be executed with appropriate automation and human oversight.
764 [BodyText] Four explicit human decision points preserve governance accountability: recalibration approval at the DECIDE step, acceptance criteria definition, rollback decisions when validation fails, and full retraining triggers when incremental updates are insufficient. These decision points ensure that human judgment remains central to surrogate management despite automation of routine operations.
765 [BodyText] The phased development approach – from prototype demonstration through integrated framework to operational system – provides progressive capability building with lessons learned informing each subsequent phase. This approach manages risk while delivering value incrementally.
766 [BodyText] Technical considerations address computational requirements spanning HPC physics simulations to lightweight inference, interoperability with existing simulators and data systems, and alignment with DOE quality assurance requirements. The architecture accommodates site-specific constraints while maintaining consistent workflow and governance structure.
767 [BodyText] The framework supports adaptive surrogate management that maintains prediction accuracy over multiyear remediation timelines while meeting DOE quality assurance and regulatory requirements. By combining automated monitoring with human decision points, the implementation balances operational efficiency with the accountability required for environmental management applications.

792 [Heading2] Case Study Design
793 [BodyText] This section describes the controlled case study that exercises the recalibration framework on the P&T prototype introduced in Section 5.2.2. The case study simulates a realistic operational challenge: A surrogate trained under routine plume conditions encounters a regime shift in the governing model inputs – such as a modified well-field configuration or a revised source-term characterization derived from updated site-investigation data – that displaces plume dynamics outside the surrogate’s original training distribution. The design evaluates whether the framework’s continual-learning mechanisms (Section 3.3.3) can restore predictive accuracy without full retraining, and how the choice of drift-characterization metric influences detection and update effectiveness.
794 [Heading3] Drift Scenario and Operational Rationale
795 [BodyText] In operational P&T systems, surrogate models are trained on historical plume snapshots that reflect a limited range of extraction configurations and plume dynamics. Over time, well-field modifications, source-strength changes, or aquifer property updates can produce plume behavior that differs substantially from training conditions. The case study replicates this situation by partitioning the 10,000-realization simulation dataset (Section 5.2.2) into two regimes:
796 [ListBullet] Low-change regime. Realizations in which successive plume snapshots exhibit modest spatial and concentration changes, representative of the quasi-steady conditions under which the surrogate was originally trained.
797 [ListBullet] Extreme-change regime. Realizations exhibiting rapid plume expansion, contraction, or redistribution – conditions analogous to those arising from operational perturbations such as well activation, rate increases, or source release events.
798 [BodyText] This partitioning creates a controlled distribution shift: The surrogate is first trained exclusively on lowchange data (establishing a baseline), and its ability to generalize to – and then recover accuracy on – extreme-change conditions is systematically evaluated.
799 [Heading3] Training Paradigms for Recalibration Evaluation
800 [BodyText] Three training paradigms are compared to isolate the value of the framework’s continual-learning approach relative to static training and full retraining.
801 [ListBullet] Model A: Baseline (low-regime only). Trained exclusively on the low-change subset, representing a surrogate deployed before any regime shift occurs. This model establishes the pre-drift performance baseline and is expected to degrade on extreme-change samples.
802 [ListBullet] Model B: Fine-tuned (continual learning). Initialized from Model A and fine-tuned on a combined dataset of low-change and extreme-change samples, following the continual-learning procedures described in Section 3.3.3. This paradigm represents the framework’s primary recalibration pathway (Pathway B, simulation-driven update), demonstrating whether targeted fine-tuning can restore accuracy while preserving previously learned behavior.
803 [ListBullet] Model C: Retrained from scratch. Trained from scratch on the same combined dataset used for Model B, representing the full-retraining fallback described in Section 3.3.6. Model C provides an upper-bound reference for what accuracy is achievable when computational cost is not constrained.
804 [Heading3] Evaluation Protocol
805 [BodyText] All models are evaluated on the extreme-change subset corresponding to the selected partitioning criterion. Performance is reported using denormalized RMSE on held-out extreme samples, enabling direct comparison across training paradigms.
806 [BodyText] Evaluation deliverables include:
807 [ListBullet] Accuracy comparison. RMSE of Models A, B, and C on extreme-change samples, quantifying the accuracy gap between the baseline surrogate (A), the fine-tuned surrogate (B), and the fully retrained surrogate (C).
808 [ListBullet] Forgetting assessment. RMSE of Models B and C on low-change samples, verifying that fine-tuning does not degrade performance on previously learned conditions (catastrophic forgetting).
809 [ListBullet] Visual diagnostics. Side-by-side comparisons of ground truth, prediction, and prediction error for selected extreme samples, providing interpretable evidence of recalibration effectiveness.
810 [BodyText] These evaluation outputs map directly to the acceptance criteria defined in Section 3.1.3 and provide the empirical basis for the governance documentation required at the DEPLOY step (Step 7) of the recalibration workflow.
811 [Heading2] Expected Outcomes
812 [BodyText] The prototype demonstration is expected to produce several quantitative outcomes. Surrogate inference speedups of 10³ to 105 times relative to physics-based models will enable real-time decision support. Recalibration will require fewer than 10 targeted physics simulations selected by active learning, demonstrating efficient use of computational resources. Post-recalibration accuracy will recover within acceptance criteria defined in Section 3.1.3.
813 [BodyText] The demonstration will produce documented deliverables including a complete recalibration cycle with all seven workflow steps executed and recorded, governance documentation meeting the audit trail requirements of Section 3.1.5, and reproducible code capturing each step for subsequent development phases.
814 [BodyText] The demonstration will also generate lessons for Phase 2 development as described in Section 4.3.2. These lessons will inform decisions about automation priorities, user interface requirements, and integration with site data systems. Experience with the prototype will reveal which aspects of the workflow benefit most from automation and which require continued human oversight.

Expected outcomes include quantitative validation of speedup and accuracy, documented governance procedures, and actional findings for subsequent development phases.

843 [Heading1] Discussion and Conclusions
844 [BodyText] This section addresses the current limitations of the framework, identifies areas for further investigation that extend beyond the implementation roadmap presented in Section 4.0 and the prototype demonstration plan in Section 5.0, summarizes the expected impact, and concludes with key takeaways for DOE stakeholders.
845 [Heading2] Limitations
846 [Heading3] Dependence on Physics-Model Quality
847 [BodyText] Surrogate accuracy is fundamentally bound by the quality of the physics-based models used to generate training data. Errors or biases in the numerical models – whether from simplified boundary conditions, incomplete process coupling, or uncertain parameterization – will propagate to the surrogates. The recalibration framework can detect when surrogate predictions deviate from new physics-model runs, but it cannot correct for systematic deficiencies in the physics models themselves. Translating simulators into credible site predictions requires that hydraulic conductivities, recharge rates, boundary conditions, and transport parameters be carefully adjusted until simulated heads and concentrations adequately reproduce field observations. Whether calibration relies on automated inverse methods such as PEST or DAKOTA (Adams et al. 2020; Doherty 1994; Hill and Tiedeman 2007) or ensemble-based DA (Evensen 2009), ensuring that the underlying numerical models remain well-calibrated is a prerequisite for effective surrogate maintenance.
848 [Heading3] Computational Requirements for Ensemble Methods
849 [BodyText] Ensemble-based UQ requires training and maintaining multiple surrogate instances, increasing computational and storage demands relative to a single deterministic model. While surrogate inference remains orders of magnitude faster than physics-based simulation, the aggregate cost of ensemble training, storage of model checkpoints, and periodic retraining across all ensemble members must be accounted for in resource planning. Efficient alternatives such as MC-Dropout or spectral-normalized approaches may reduce this burden but require further evaluation in the groundwater context.
850 [Heading3] Validation Status
851 [BodyText] The framework presented in this report is at the concept and design stage. Validation to date has been limited to illustrative synthetic test cases. Application to real field data – with actual measurement noise, irregular sampling intervals, data gaps, and full 3D site complexity typical of EM applications – remains to be demonstrated through the prototype development outlined in Section 5.0.
852 [Heading2] Areas for Further Investigation
853 [BodyText] The implementation roadmap in Section 4.0 defines the sequencing and deliverables for framework development, and the prototype demonstration plan in Section 5.0 describes the initial proof-of-concept implementations. Several scientific questions that cut across those efforts warrant specific attention.
854 [Heading3] Trigger-Threshold Calibration
855 [BodyText] The drift-detection and recalibration trigger logic described in Section 3.3 relies on thresholds for uncertainty growth, residual divergence, and input-distribution shift. Optimal threshold values are likely site-specific, depending on historical variability, measurement frequency, and the consequence of delayed recalibration. Developing general guidance – or adaptive threshold methods – that reduce the need for manual tuning would improve framework usability across sites. Established drift-detection algorithms provide starting points, but environmental applications may require domain-specific adaptations given the slower timescales and sparse observations typical of groundwater monitoring networks.
856 [Heading3] Multi-Fidelity Fusion
857 [BodyText] Combining information from models of varying fidelity and computational cost – for example, coarsegrid screening models, refined local models, and DL surrogates – offers a path toward more efficient generation of recalibration data. Multi-fidelity methods have shown promise in related domains, but their integration into the recalibration workflow for groundwater transport requires further development, particularly regarding how fidelity levels map to the governance-tier requirements defined in Pillar A.
858 [Heading3] Transfer Learning Across Sites
859 [BodyText] Deploying the framework at a new DOE site currently requires site-specific training data from local physics models. Transfer learning could reduce this data requirement by leveraging surrogate knowledge from previously characterized sites with analogous hydrogeological settings. Key open questions include how to quantify transferability between sites, how to define domain similarity for heterogeneous aquifer systems, and how governance procedures should account for transferred vs. locally validated model components.
860 [Heading3] Scalability
861 [BodyText] Realistic EM applications typically involve 3D, heterogeneous domains, long simulation horizons, and tight numerical tolerances to preserve mass balance and stability. Extension to these fully 3D domains with finer spatial resolution and more complex multi-species reactive transport will stress both the surrogate architectures and the recalibration methods. Whether the continual-learning and active-learning strategies described in Section 3.3 scale gracefully – or require architectural modifications – is an empirical question that the prototype phase will begin to address.
862 [Heading3] Regulatory Acceptance Pathways
863 [BodyText] Adaptive machine-learning models introduce a paradigm shift for regulatory review: The approved model is no longer static. Engagement with regulatory agencies is needed to establish acceptable review and approval procedures for models that update over time. The governance structure in Pillar A (Section 3.1) is designed to facilitate this engagement, but the specific regulatory pathway remains to be negotiated on a site-by-site basis.
864 [Heading2] Expected Impact
865 [Heading3] Operational Benefits
866 [BodyText] The primary operational benefit of the framework is sustained surrogate accuracy over multi-year remediation timelines without requiring costly full retraining. Surrogate inference speedups of 103 to 105 relative to physics-based models enable real-time decision support and interactive scenario exploration that are infeasible with numerical simulation alone – particularly for planning tasks that require ensembles of simulations, Monte Carlo realizations of hydrogeologic variability, or scenario sweeps over remediation designs, where the number of model evaluations needed can easily reach the hundreds or thousands. Targeted recalibration further reduces computational cost by focusing physics-model runs on the scenarios that most improve surrogate performance, rather than regenerating the full training dataset.
867 [Heading3] Scientific Contributions
868 [BodyText] The framework contributes to a novel integration of UQ, drift detection, continual learning, and active learning within a governance-aware architecture. This integration addresses a gap in the scientific ML literature, where these methods are typically developed and evaluated in isolation. The governance layer – rarely addressed in machine learning research – provides a template for deploying adaptive models in regulatory contexts beyond groundwater remediation.
869 [Heading3] Broader Applicability
870 [BodyText] Although developed for EM groundwater applications, the framework’s three-pillar architecture and seven-step workflow are domain-general. The framework systematically updates the surrogate only as needed, maintaining accuracy without incurring the cost of full retraining. Potential applications include other EM sites with different contaminant types, hydrogeological settings, or remediation technologies; long-term climate-resilience modeling; and scientific surrogate maintenance in fields such as materials science and engineering design. Open-source release of code, containers, and demonstration datasets – planned as part of the implementation roadmap in Section 4.0 – will enable community adoption and contribution.
871 [Heading2] Conclusions
872 [BodyText] Five key takeaways emerge from this framework development:
873 [BodyText] Surrogates require systematic maintenance. DL models trained once and deployed indefinitely will degrade as site conditions, remediation operations, or conceptual models evolve. Systematic monitoring and recalibration are essential for multi-year operational use.
874 [BodyText] Physics-based models remain the authoritative reference. Surrogates accelerate prediction but do not replace numerical simulation. Every recalibration cycle is anchored to new physics-model truth data, preserving the scientific foundation of site decisions.
875 [BodyText] Governance is not optional. Embedding oversight, documentation, acceptance criteria, and human decision authority throughout the workflow is what distinguishes an operationally deployable framework from a research prototype. Regulatory confidence depends on this structure.
876 [BodyText] Targeted updates outperform static approaches. Adaptive strategies that detect drift and execute focused recalibration are more efficient than either ignoring degradation or performing periodic full retraining regardless of need.
877 [BodyText] The framework balances accuracy, efficiency, and trust. By integrating technical recalibration methods (Pillar C) with governance procedures (Pillar A) around a shared modeling infrastructure (Pillar B), the framework addresses the coupled technical and institutional requirements of long-term environmental management.
878 [BodyText] This report has established the conceptual design, methodological foundations, implementation roadmap, and prototype demonstration plan for the recalibration framework. The next step is prototype development and validation on representative groundwater modeling problems, as detailed in Section 5.0, providing the empirical basis needed before the framework can be responsibly applied to operational EM site data.

995 [Heading6] – Surrogate Model Literature Context
996 [BodyText] This appendix provides detailed literature context for the surrogate model landscape discussed in Section 3.2.3. The material presented here supports the framework’s modeling engine component (Pillar B) by documenting the current state of deep learning (DL) surrogate models for subsurface flow and transport applications.
997 [BodyText] The surrogate model literature review was conducted using Web of Science with search terms targeting DL applications in groundwater modeling, subsurface flow prediction, and contaminant transport simulation. The search covered publications from 2018 to 2024, reflecting the rapid development of DL methods for scientific computing during this period. Papers were selected based on relevance to groundwater remediation applications, with emphasis on architectures that have demonstrated success in predicting spatiotemporal fields from physics-based model outputs.
998 [BodyText] The review identified six primary architectural families with demonstrated applicability to subsurface modeling: convolutional encoder-decoder models, operator-learning models, sequence models, generative models, regression and classical surrogates, and structured domain surrogates. Each family offers distinct advantages depending on the prediction task, data availability, and computational constraints. Comprehensive reviews by Razavi et al. (2012) and Asher et al. (2015) provide foundational context for surrogate modeling in water resources applications. Surrogate modeling publication trends were quantified using the Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate Analytics). We performed a topic search (12/5/2025) combining groundwater and subsurface keywords with surrogate-modeling terms (TS = ((groundwater OR subsurface OR aquifer OR “reactive transport” OR “porous media”) AND (“surrogate model*” OR emulator* OR “reduced-order” OR metamodel* OR “response surface” OR “Fourier neural operator*” OR “neural operator*” OR “operator learning” OR “deep learning surrogate” OR “physics-informed”)), limited to articles and reviews in English for the period 2005 to 2026.
