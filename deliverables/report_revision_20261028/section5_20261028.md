# Prototype Demonstration and Pump-and-Treat Lifecycle Case Study

This case study connects the seven-step framework to a concrete model-maintenance decision: how to recover accuracy on rare plume behavior while retaining performance on historical conditions, and what evidence is needed before an updated surrogate can be used. The available experiments provide a controlled, offline comparison of U-Net update strategies. They support a quantitative update assessment and a retrospective stress test; prospective monitoring, uncertainty calibration, and deployment remain requirements for the next demonstration.

The quantitative source is the internal manuscript *Adaptive Replay for U-Net Groundwater Plume Surrogates: Learning Rare Pump-and-Treat Dynamics Without Forgetting* (September 9, 2026 draft), referred to below as the adaptive-replay study. Values are transcribed from its displayed results, and percentage changes are calculated from rounded figure labels. They have not been independently reproduced from trajectory-level predictions. Source figures are identified explicitly as manuscript figures to distinguish them from the figures in this report.

## Prototype context and case-study purpose

The study describes a two-dimensional pump-and-treat U-Net on a 128 × 100 grid, with one to five extraction wells, varied pumping rates, and a dataset described as 10,000 physics-based simulation runs. The model builds on the analytical-solution-enhanced surrogate of Song et al. (2025). It combines concentration information with a Thiem-equation representation of pumping influence to predict the subsequent concentration field. Figure 7 summarizes the input–prediction–evaluation relationship without implying a verified implementation of the adaptive-replay controller.

Earlier prototype documentation describes five snapshots at days 0–4, whereas the partially assembled report used years 0–4. The available adaptive-replay execution record does not resolve that discrepancy or establish that the earlier dataset version was used unchanged. This revision therefore uses indexed states t and t + 1 for the update study and makes no calendar-time detector or long-horizon rollout claim. Grid spacing, trajectory identity, preprocessing, and the exact time interval should be recorded together in the final dataset manifest.

The decision question is whether an update can improve predictions for rare response patterns without unacceptable loss of historical performance. That question requires paired historical/rare evaluation, attention to physically meaningful plume quantities, and a separate uncertainty and final-validation gate. A low rare-regime error alone is insufficient to justify promotion.

## MONITOR: the baseline reliability problem

The baseline comparison supplies an offline analogue of the evidence that MONITOR would collect during operations. In manuscript Figure 4, historical root mean square error (RMSE) is 0.58 µg/L and rare-regime RMSE is 10.04 µg/L. The rare-regime result is approximately 17.3 times the historical value. Reported rare plume-mass error is 32.30%, peak-concentration error is 14.20%, and Mass90 error is 7.11%. These results identify a substantial reliability difference between the two selected response regimes.

The plume metrics make the maintenance problem relevant to groundwater interpretation: concentration-field error is accompanied by discrepancies in predicted mass, peak concentration, and plume footprint. Those discrepancies motivate a review of the surrogate's applicability. They do not establish errors at actual compliance wells or demonstrate a measured change in remedy performance.

Operational MONITOR would instead produce ordered prediction–observation comparisons and uncertainty diagnostics as new observations become available. The reported aggregates do not provide that record, trajectory-level error distributions, repeat-run variability, or a verified untouched test partition. They justify investigating the high-change regime while leaving uncertainty around the reported averages unquantified.

## DETECT: identifying the high-change stress condition

The study selects regimes using the relative change in the number of grid cells needed to contain 90% of reference plume mass between consecutive states. Manuscript Figure 1 gives a 40th-percentile cutoff of 0.133 for historical cases and a 97th-percentile cutoff of 0.329 for rare cases. Their ratio is approximately 2.47. The bottom 40% and top 3% of the response-score distribution therefore define the historical and rare subsets used in the update comparison.

This Mass90 change score is a retrospective response descriptor. It requires the reference plume at t + 1 and cannot supply a prospective warning before that state is available. Because it counts cells rather than tracking their locations, it can also miss translation or rearrangement that leaves the footprint size unchanged. Selection by this score does not establish whether the underlying cause was a pumping change, a source change, or another operational or hydrogeological process.

The evidence supports a controlled stress condition for DETECT. It does not provide sensitivity, specificity, false-alarm frequency, or warning lead time. The framework's additional diagnostics—rolling residual variance, lag-1 autocorrelation, skewness and kurtosis, uncertainty inflation, input-distribution distances, and latent-space indicators—remain candidates for prospective testing. The available short sequence and aggregate results cannot substantiate the illustrative 60–90-day persistence rule. Future detector evaluation must use ordered trajectories, known change conditions, thresholds fixed before evaluation, and a documented time axis.

## DECIDE: screening the adaptation–retention tradeoff

DECIDE translates the reliability concern into an explicit requirement for an update. An acceptable candidate must improve performance in the regime under review while retaining enough historical accuracy for its intended use. The adaptive-replay study presents several illustrative acceptance examples rather than one approved, preregistered deployment contract.

For a transparent numerical screen, manuscript Figure 5 uses at most 30% historical RMSE degradation and at least 85% rare-regime RMSE improvement. Table 9 and Figure 8 reproduce the four displayed controller outcomes under those two conditions. These percentages belong to that controller comparison and are not recomputed from the separate manuscript Figure 4 RMSE values.

Table 9. Controller outcomes under the illustrative 30% historical-degradation and 85% rare-improvement screen in manuscript Figure 5. Passing both conditions identifies a candidate for further evaluation; it does not constitute deployment approval.

| Controller | Historical RMSE increase (%) | Rare RMSE reduction (%) | Two-condition screen |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Fixed replay | 80.4 | 88.9 | Fails retention |
| Threshold adaptive forgetting | 26.5 | 85.8 | Passes |
| Loss-balanced | 21.1 | 85.5 | Passes |
| Loss-balanced with floor | 38.1 | 87.3 | Fails retention |

![figure08_acceptance]

Figure 8. Adaptation–retention tradeoff for the four controller outcomes displayed in manuscript Figure 5. Dashed boundaries mark the illustrative 30% historical-degradation limit and 85% rare-improvement requirement; the shaded region satisfies both. Points reproduce rounded aggregate labels. Replicate-level uncertainty is unavailable, and passing this screen does not satisfy the independent uncertainty-calibration and final-test gates.

Threshold adaptive forgetting clears the historical limit by 3.5 percentage points and the rare-improvement target by 0.8 points. Loss-balanced replay clears them by 8.9 and 0.5 points. The small rare-improvement margins make repeated-run and independent-test evaluation consequential. Fixed replay and loss-balanced replay with a floor fail the historical condition despite exceeding the rare-improvement target.

The defensible decision is controller-specific: retain the threshold-adaptive and loss-balanced outcomes as candidates for further evaluation under this illustrative screen. Final acceptance requires a frozen performance contract, common evaluation partitions, documented metric definitions, and uncertainty assessment. No blanket acceptance of adaptive replay follows from these four points.

## PREPARE: assembling the update data

The study prepares rare update cases from the top 3% response regime and historical replay cases from the bottom 40%. Rare cases supply the behavior to be learned, and historical examples constrain forgetting. This provides a controlled dataset for comparing update strategies.

The framework's approximately 70–75% historical-replay guidance is a design starting point. It is not a measured batch composition for every experiment. The controller comparison includes a plotted run that reaches a replay cap of r = 0.8; verifying its actual historical/new exposure requires the executed sampling definition, batch counts, and controller logs.

The preparation is regime-selected sampling. It does not demonstrate active learning: no candidate-pool scores, diversity checks, information-gain values, or selection record for 6–10 new physics simulations are available. The simulation-driven PREPARE pathway remains a subsequent comparison against alternatives such as random selection under a matched simulation budget.

Data preparation must also preserve complete trajectory identities across training, controller selection, uncertainty calibration, and final testing. The manuscript does not provide the manifest needed to verify that separation, the treatment of the middle 57% of cases, or when the regime cutoffs were estimated. The earlier prototype's split cannot substitute for this study-specific record.

## UPDATE: quantitative comparison of candidate updates

The clearest measured evidence concerns the balance between rare-regime recovery and historical retention. Table 10 and Figure 9 reproduce the RMSE labels in manuscript Figure 4. Table 11 reports three additional rare-regime plume metrics from that same comparison. Keeping these results together preserves their source context.

Table 10. Historical and rare RMSE reported in manuscript Figure 4. Values are rounded aggregate labels in µg/L; they are not independently reproduced test estimates.

| Configuration | Historical RMSE (µg/L) | Rare RMSE (µg/L) | Historical increase (%) | Rare reduction (%) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Baseline | 0.58 | 10.04 | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| Simple fine-tuning | 1.11 | 3.92 | 91.4 | 61.0 |
| Fixed replay | 1.03 | 1.21 | 77.6 | 87.9 |
| EWC | 5.45 | 1.22 | 839.7 | 87.8 |

Relative to the displayed baseline, simple fine-tuning reduces rare RMSE by 61.0% while increasing historical RMSE by 91.4%. Fixed replay reduces rare RMSE by 87.9%, rare mass error by 94.4%, rare peak error by 75.8%, and rare Mass90 error by 73.4%, while historical RMSE increases by 77.6%. EWC gives a similar rare RMSE reduction but increases historical RMSE by 839.7% in this configuration. Fixed replay therefore provides a more favorable RMSE balance than simple fine-tuning or EWC within this particular comparison; it does not remove the retention cost.

Manuscript Figure 3 separately reports a full-network historical/rare RMSE pair of 0.64/0.99 µg/L, compared with 1.11/3.92 µg/L for decoder-only fine-tuning. The full-network pair is not merged into the Table 10 ranking because its relationship to the other configurations is unresolved: the manuscript uses conflicting Model C labels, and the earlier framework uses that label for a from-scratch baseline. Model identity must be determined from executed configurations rather than letter labels.

Likewise, the 80.4% fixed-replay historical degradation and 88.9% rare improvement displayed in the controller comparison differ from the 77.6% and 87.9% calculated from Table 10. These differences require panel-specific denominators, splits, and exposure records; rounding alone should not be assumed to explain them.

The displayed adaptive trajectory reaches r = 0.8 for most of its 100 plotted epochs. This demonstrates selection of a high replay ratio in that run, but it does not isolate the effect of adaptive scheduling. A fixed-ratio-0.8 comparison with matched initialization, random seeds, update budget, and historical exposure is needed to determine whether adaptation itself provides additional benefit.

## CALIBRATE: assessing the candidate's uncertainty

Point-prediction improvement does not establish that a candidate expresses uncertainty reliably. CALIBRATE requires independent calibration data, a specified interval procedure, and reporting of empirical coverage, interval width, and reliability across relevant regimes and locations. The framework uses coverage near a nominal 90% level as an illustrative objective; the applicable coverage tolerance and acceptable width must be set for the intended decision.

The adaptive-replay study provides no prediction intervals, ensemble or dropout samples, conformal calibration residuals, calibration parameters, or reliability diagrams. Consequently, the candidate outcomes that pass the Figure 8 screen remain awaiting calibration. A deterministic surrogate could use residual-based conformal calibration if suitable independent data exist. Its usual marginal-coverage guarantees require the relevant exchangeability assumptions and cannot be presumed under distribution shift or within temporally dependent trajectories.

The next evidence package should keep controller-selection data, uncertainty-calibration data, and untouched final-test trajectories distinct. It should report coverage and width separately for historical and rare regimes and at decision-relevant locations. Those requirements establish the next experiment; no numerical calibration result can be inferred from the RMSE reductions already reported.

## DEPLOY: final validation and promotion decision

DEPLOY requires an untouched final test and a versioned acceptance record. Evaluation must address the frozen historical/rare performance contract, priority-location error limits, uncertainty coverage and width, spatial and temporal consistency, and systematic bias near decision boundaries. The record must identify the model, preprocessing, training and calibration configurations, data provenance, reviewer, and promotion decision.

Those promotion artifacts are not available for the adaptive-replay study. The case-study outcome is therefore a documented hold for calibration and final validation, with no demonstrated deployment. Candidate rejection does not authorize use of an invalid incumbent: if operational validity is in question, the withdrawal and governed retraining policy in Section 3.1.5 applies. The offline screening results in this case study are not themselves an operational rollback event.

## Integrated lifecycle result

The seven steps form a connected decision argument. Baseline evaluation identifies a high-change response regime with poor predictions. Retrospective detection labels the stress condition. DECIDE specifies the adaptation–retention tradeoff; PREPARE supplies rare cases and historical replay; UPDATE produces quantitative candidate comparisons. The two controller outcomes that pass the illustrative screen then encounter the unfulfilled calibration and final-validation requirements. The framework produces a clear next action even though operational deployment has not been demonstrated.

The contribution is a controlled update case study placed within an auditable lifecycle. The available results show that rare-regime recovery and historical retention must be evaluated jointly. They also show why an update that improves point predictions remains a candidate until uncertainty, independence, and governance requirements are met.

## Next demonstration and implementation implications

The next increment should first resolve the dataset time axis and trajectory split, archive executed configurations and predictions, and reconcile the figure-specific comparisons. It should then freeze the detector and acceptance rules, compare adaptive scheduling with a matched fixed-replay control, calibrate uncertainty independently, and evaluate an untouched final test. A documented reviewer decision would complete the promotion record. Long-duration monitoring and use on field data remain subsequent validation tasks.

Efficiency should be assessed separately from accuracy. The earlier report's order-of-magnitude speedup expectations and 6–10 targeted-simulation starting point remain context and design guidance, not measured benefits of this controller. Record physics-run count, training and inference time, memory, hardware, and uncertainty-processing cost under a common workload. Compare physics-only analysis, a static surrogate, and the hybrid recalibration workflow to determine whether the lifecycle provides practical value.
