Summary
Deep-learning surrogate models can reduce groundwater simulation times from hours or days to seconds,
making it feasible to rapidly evaluate remediation alternatives, explore uncertainty, and support planning
decisions that would otherwise be constrained by computational cost. For U.S. Department of Energy
(DOE) Office of Environmental Management (EM) sites managing complex, long-duration groundwater
cleanup, this capability offers substantial operational value.
However, surrogate models are trained on data that represent site conditions at a particular point in time.
Over the years and decades typical of EM remediation, those conditions may change because of natural
system evolution, modifications to pumping or treatment systems, new monitoring data, or revisions to
the conceptual site model. As a result, surrogate accuracy may degrade over time. If such degradation is
not detected and addressed, surrogate-informed decisions may no longer be reliable. In regulatory settings
governed by Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) and
DOE quality assurance requirements, that risk is unacceptable.
Although interest in surrogate modeling is growing within the environmental modeling community, no
established methodology currently exists for maintaining surrogate accuracy over multi-year operational
periods while preserving the governance, documentation, and quality assurance standards required for EM
applications.
The purpose of this project is to establish a robust and defensible framework for deploying and
maintaining artificial-intelligence-enabled deep-learning surrogate models as reliable decision-support
tools throughout the lifecycle of EM groundwater remediation. This mid-year report presents the
conceptual design and prototype plan for that framework, which addresses the long-term surrogate
maintenance challenge through three core components:
• Governance defines the rules for surrogate use, including alignment with CERCLA, U.S.
Environmental Protection Agency environmental modeling guidance, and DOE EM-QA-001;
acceptance criteria; recalibration triggers; and configuration control. Within this framework, surrogate
models are limited to screening, scenario exploration, and planning support, while high-fidelity
physics-based models remain the authoritative basis for final regulatory and remedy decisions.
• Modeling engines include both the physics-based numerical simulators that provide reference
predictions and the deep-learning surrogates that provide fast approximate results. Surrogate models
are data-driven approximations of the physics-based models and can reproduce simulator behavior at
speeds 103 to 105 times faster, while the physics-based model remains the ground-truth reference
throughout framework operation.
• Recalibration mechanisms provide the technical methods needed to monitor surrogate performance,
detect degradation, and execute targeted updates that restore reliability without requiring routine full
retraining.
These three components are connected through a structured, seven-step workflow with two phases:
continuous monitoring that runs passively during routine operations, and a triggered response that
activates only when monitoring identifies meaningful degradation and governance review authorizes an
update. Every step includes defined decision points, acceptance criteria, and documentation requirements
to support auditability and independent review.
Two proof-of-concept implementations are in preparation: a Fourier neural operator surrogate for crosssection contaminant transport, and a convolutional encoder-decoder surrogate for pump and treat plume
prediction. These prototypes will exercise the full recalibration workflow on representative groundwater

Overview
This report presents a framework for deploying and maintaining deep-learning (DL) surrogate models as
reliable decision-support tools for long-term groundwater remediation within the U.S. Department of
Energy (DOE) Office of Environmental Management (EM) complex. DL surrogates can accelerate
predictions of groundwater flow and transport by factors of 103 to 105 relative to physics-based numerical
simulators, making it feasible to evaluate large scenario sets, explore uncertainty, and support planning
decisions that would otherwise be computationally impractical. However, surrogates trained at a single
point in time may lose accuracy as site conditions evolve, remediation systems are modified, conceptual
models are refined, or new field data reveal behavior outside the original training domain. Without a
systematic process for detecting and correcting such degradation, surrogate-based decision support may
become unreliable, which is unacceptable in compliance-driven settings governed by the Comprehensive
Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) and DOE EM-QA-001 quality
assurance requirements.
The proposed framework is organized around three pillars:
• Pillar A, Governance, establishes the regulatory alignment, acceptance criteria, configuration
control, and decision authority needed to manage adaptive models in compliance-driven
environments. It is grounded in U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) environmental
modeling guidance and DOE EM-QA-001, which incorporates ASME NQA-1 quality assurance
principles for environmental management software and modeling activities.
• Pillar B, Modeling Engines, encompasses both the physics-based numerical simulators that remain
the authoritative source of truth and the DL surrogate models that provide fast approximate
predictions. Physics-based models such as PFLOTRAN, MODFLOW, STOMP, and TOUGH2
provide the high-fidelity reference standard, while surrogate architectures such as Fourier neural
operators and diffusion models enable rapid inference for screening and planning.
• Pillar C, Recalibration Mechanisms, provides the technical methods needed to maintain surrogate
reliability over time, including uncertainty quantification, drift detection, continual learning, and
active learning.
Within this framework, surrogate models are explicitly limited to screening-level analyses, scenario
exploration, and planning support. High-fidelity physics-based models remain the authoritative basis for
final regulatory and remedy decisions. When surrogate validity is in question, governance procedures
require withdrawal of surrogate use and escalation to a controlled retraining process, thereby preventing
unmanaged risk. This conservative posture is consistent with CERCLA protectiveness expectations and
DOE quality assurance requirements.
The framework has four objectives. First, it defines a governance structure that aligns surrogate
deployment and maintenance with established regulatory and quality assurance requirements. Second, it
establishes systematic methods for monitoring surrogate performance and detecting degradation before
decision support is compromised. Third, it provides efficient recalibration procedures to restore surrogate
accuracy within predefined acceptance criteria while minimizing the burden of new high-fidelity
simulations. Fourth, it operationalizes these capabilities through a structured workflow with explicit
decision points, documentation requirements, and configuration control to support auditability,
reproducibility, and independent review.
These objectives are implemented through a seven-step, two-phase workflow. The first phase, continuous
monitoring, runs during routine surrogate operation and includes monitoring performance indicators,
detecting potential drift, and determining whether observed changes warrant action. The second phase,

triggered response, is activated only when monitoring indicates meaningful degradation and governance
review approves an update. It includes preparing new training data, updating surrogate parameters,
validating the revised model against predefined acceptance criteria, and deploying the approved version
under formal configuration control. Throughout this process, governance records document trigger
rationale, data provenance, validation results, and deployment decisions to support transparency and
auditability.
The report describes the methods underlying each pillar, including governance procedures aligned with
EPA and DOE quality assurance standards, surrogate and physics-model architectures, and recalibration

maintaining surrogate models over long operational periods, addressing a gap in both the machinelearning and environmental-modeling literature. Second, it integrates uncertainty quantification, drift
detection, and continual learning into a single operational workflow rather than treating them as isolated
technical methods. Third, it establishes a governance template for applying adaptive machine-learning
models in regulatory contexts, helping address a major barrier to operational adoption at EM sites.
The framework remains at the concept and design stage. Current limitations include dependence on the
quality of the underlying physics-based models, the computational cost of some uncertainty quantification
methods, and the need for validation under real field conditions with measurement noise, data gaps, and
site complexity representative of EM applications. Prototype development and testing on representative
groundwater remediation problems are the next steps toward eventual operational deployment.

1.0 Introduction
1.1 Motivation and Problem Statement
Cleanup programs at U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Office
of Environmental Management (EM) sites such as Hanford,
Savannah River, and Los Alamos operate on timelines spanning
decades, during which hydrogeological conditions evolve,
remediation systems are modified, monitoring networks are
updated, and conceptual site models are revised in response to
new data. Throughout these extended campaigns, site managers
must evaluate alternative remediation strategies, assess system
performance, and demonstrate continued protectiveness of
human health and the environment under various regulatory
frameworks such as requirements established under the
Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and
Liability Act (CERCLA) and reinforced by EM quality
assurance expectations (NRC 2013; EPA 2001). The purpose of
this project is to establish a robust, defensible framework for
deploying and maintaining artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled
deep learning (DL) surrogate models as reliable decision-support
tools over the long-term lifecycle of EM cleanup activities.

Deep learning surrogate models are
AI-enabled, data-driven
approximations of complex physical,
numerical, or computational models,
built using deep neural networks to
emulate the behavior of high-fidelity
simulations. Instead of repeatedly
running computationally expensive
models (e.g., groundwater flow,
contaminant transport, or reactive
chemistry), a surrogate model is trained
on simulation or observational data to
learn the input–output relationships and
then provide rapid predictions.
These models are particularly valuable
for tasks like uncertainty quantification,
optimization, and real-time decision
support, where thousands to millions of
model evaluations may be required. By
significantly reducing computational
cost while maintaining acceptable
accuracy, deep learning surrogates
enable faster scenario analysis,
integration into AI-driven workflows,
and scalable system-level optimization.

Physics-based numerical models are the established foundation
for developing the technical basis for supporting decisionmaking throughout the long cleanup timeframes. High-fidelity
simulators coupling groundwater flow, contaminant transport,
and geochemical reactions provide the defensible, process-based
predictions on which regulatory and remedy decisions depend. However, these models are
computationally expensive to develop, run, maintain, and update. These computational burdens limit the
pace and scope of analyses that site teams can practically perform within project timelines and budgets.

DL surrogate models offer a path to overcoming these computational constraints and significantly
accelerating and improving modeling efforts, and ultimately, decision-making at sites. By learning the
input-output relationships of physics-based simulators from training data, surrogates can approximate
numerical model predictions at inference speeds 103 to 105 times faster than the original simulators, which
may require several days or longer for complex model configurations. This capability enables rapid
scenario evaluation, interactive planning, and large-scale uncertainty exploration that would be
impractical using physics-based simulation alone. Recent advances in neural operator architectures,
convolutional encoder-decoder networks, and physics-informed training strategies have demonstrated
surrogate accuracy sufficient for meaningful decision support across a range of subsurface modeling
applications.
While surrogate models offer significant improvements in computational efficiency, they—like purely
physics-based models—must still be rigorously verified, calibrated, and continuously updated as new data
become available. A surrogate trained at a single point in time encodes the conditions, parameters, and
conceptual understanding reflected in its training data. As site conditions change — through natural
system evolution, operational modifications, updated boundary conditions, or revised parameter estimates
— the surrogate’s training distribution diverges from the conditions it is asked to predict. This
divergence, termed distribution shift or concept drift, causes surrogate accuracy to degrade progressively.

Without systematic detection and correction, this degradation can proceed silently, producing predictions
that appear reasonable but are no longer reliable.
Despite this clear need, no established framework exists for systematically maintaining surrogate model
accuracy over multi-year operational periods while preserving regulatory confidence. The machinelearning literature offers individual methods for uncertainty quantification (UQ), drift detection, continual
learning, and active learning, but these techniques are typically developed and evaluated in isolation,
outside the governance and quality assurance context required for environmental compliance applications.
The environmental modeling community, in turn, has well-established governance practices for physicsbased models but lacks guidance for managing adaptive machine-learning models that update over time.
Therefore, this report addresses the gap between the technical capability to build surrogates and the
operational infrastructure to maintain them.
The objective of the report is to define a structured, end-to-end framework for the deployment,
monitoring, and lifecycle management of DL surrogate models within EM decision processes. It
translates emerging machine learning capabilities into a practical, auditable system aligned with site
technical needs, and regulatory and quality assurance expectations.

1.2 Framework Objectives
This report presents a recalibration framework designed to maintain DL surrogate accuracy over the
multi-year to multi-decade timelines typical of EM groundwater remediation. The framework has four
objectives:
1. Define governance structures that align surrogate model deployment with established regulatory
and quality assurance requirements, including CERCLA expectations, U.S. Environmental
Protection Agency (EPA) environmental modeling guidance, and DOE EM-QA-001. Within this
governance structure, surrogate models are explicitly limited to screening-level analyses, scenario
exploration, and planning support. High-fidelity physics-based numerical models remain the
authoritative basis for final regulatory and remedy decisions.
2. Establish systematic methods for monitoring surrogate performance and detecting
degradation before it compromises decision support. These methods integrate UQ, statistical
drift detection, and residual analysis into a continuous monitoring process that runs passively
during routine surrogate operation.
3. Provide efficient recalibration procedures that restore surrogate accuracy to within defined
acceptance criteria upon detection of predictive degradation. Such recalibration employs
continual-learning methods to mitigate knowledge forgetting across successive training cycles,
and active-learning strategies to identify maximally informative simulation candidates and
thereby minimize the computational burden of new physics-model simulations.
4. Operationalize these capabilities through a structured workflow with defined steps, decision
points, acceptance criteria, and documentation requirements that support auditability,
reproducibility, and independent review.
The framework is organized around three pillars – Governance, Modeling Engines, and Recalibration
Mechanisms – that together address the coupled technical and institutional requirements of deploying
adaptive models in compliance-driven environments.

2.0 Framework Overview
2.1 Three Pillars Structure
The proposed recalibration framework is organized around three core pillars that together provide the
foundation for maintaining DL surrogate accuracy over operational timescales. These pillars function as
management domains – organizational containers that hold and maintain specific concerns essential to the
framework’s operation. Each pillar constitutes a distinct functional area with defined responsibilities:
Pillar A establishes the rules and policies that govern operations, Pillar B manages the computational
physics-based and surrogate models that generate predictions and ground truth, and Pillar C provides the
adaptive monitoring and recalibration mechanism that monitor performance degradation and restore
surrogate accuracy within accepted thresholds.
The three pillars define what the framework manages, while the recalibration workflow described in
Section 2.2 defines how these elements interact during operations. Each workflow step draws resources,
methods, or constraints from one or more pillars, but no single pillar owns the workflow. This separation
of concerns ensures that governance policies, modeling capabilities, and adaptive methods can evolve
independently while remaining integrated through the operational workflow.

2.1.1

Pillar A: Conceptual Design & Governance

Pillar A serves as the design authority for the framework, defining the rules under which all operations
proceed. This pillar establishes the objectives, performance thresholds, success metrics, and regulatory
requirements that guide surrogate development, deployment, evaluation, and maintenance. It specifies the
conditions and thresholds that initiate recalibration, defines what constitutes acceptable performance, and
prescribes how decisions must be documented for regulatory compliance and independent review.
The contents of Pillar A include trigger policies that specify criteria indicating when recalibration should
be evaluated, including thresholds on predictive uncertainty, prediction–observation residual, and drift
metrics. These policies define the conditions under which the framework transitions from routine
monitoring to active evaluation of update needs. Pillar A also contains acceptance criteria – quantitative
standards that define acceptable performance for operational deployment. These typically include error
statistics at priority monitoring locations such as root mean square error (RMSE) targets, calibration of
uncertainty estimates with prediction interval coverage probability, and spatiotemporal consistency with
observed system behavior.
Rollback procedures form another essential component of Pillar A, providing protocols for responding
when an attempted surrogate update fails to meet acceptance criteria. As defined in Section 3.1.5, rollback
in this framework doesn’t mean reversion to a prior surrogate version; instead, it means withdrawing the
surrogate from operational use and escalating to full retraining. This distinction reflects the conservative
approach required for high-consequence environmental applications. Audit requirements ensure that all
recalibration decisions are traceable and defensible through documentation standards that include logging
trigger rationale, data sources used, evaluation results, and verification outcomes, consistent with DOE
quality assurance requirements.
Data policies within Pillar A specify which measurements to assimilate, quality assurance and quality
control standards, and data provenance requirements, ensuring that only validated data influence surrogate
updates. Interpretability standards establish requirements for model explainability that support
governance review and stakeholder confidence, following principles articulated in environmental

2.1.2

Pillar B: Modeling Engines

Pillar B provides the computational foundation for the framework, managing the models that generate
predictions and establish ground truth. This pillar comprises both the high-fidelity physics-based
simulator that serves as the authoritative reference and the DL surrogate that enables fast operational
inference.
The physics simulator represents the high-fidelity numerical model – such as PFLOTRAN, MODFLOW,
STOMP, or TOUGH2 – that serves as the truth generator for the framework. These simulators provide
physically consistent predictions but require substantial computational resources, with individual runs
potentially taking hours to days depending on model complexity. When surrogate correction is required,
the physics model generates new training data at conditions where predictive degradation has been
detected. All recalibration updates are driven by high-fidelity simulation outputs rather than extrapolation.
The DL surrogate is the fast inference model trained to emulate the physics simulator’s input-output
behavior. Architectures may include U-Net (encoder-decoder convolutional network, named for its Ushaped architecture), U-FNO (U-Net Fourier neural operator), long short-term memory (LSTM), or
diffusion models, depending on the prediction task. Surrogates can generate predictions orders of
magnitude faster than direct physics simulation, enabling real-time decision support. However, surrogates
interpolate within their training domain and are subject to distribution drift when conditions evolve
beyond their original training envelope.
Pillar B also needs to maintain clear input-output contracts that define the interface between physics
models and surrogates to ensure that data formats, coordinate systems, and variable definitions are
consistent across model types. Versioned artifacts provide model version control and management
systems that track surrogate versions, training configurations, and associated metadata, enabling
reproducibility and supporting rollback procedures when needed. Training data archives serve as
repositories of physics simulation outputs and field observations used for surrogate training, organized
with provenance metadata to support audit requirements.
The physics model remains the authoritative truth source throughout the framework’s operation. The
surrogate is a sophisticated emulator that provides computational efficiency, but its validity is always
assessed relative to physics-based predictions and field observations. When the surrogate predictions
extend beyond the conditions represented in its training domain, the physics model provides the
authoritative reference simulations needed to support correction and retraining.

2.1.3

Pillar C: Recalibration Mechanisms

Pillar C supplies the adaptive intelligence for the framework, managing the techniques used for
monitoring surrogate performance, detecting degradation, and orchestrating corrections. This pillar
comprises the methods library that the operational workflow draws upon to maintain surrogate accuracy
over time.

5

PNNL-39195
UQ methods within Pillar C provide techniques for estimating predictive uncertainty, including Monte
Carlo dropout (MC-Dropout), deep ensembles, Bayesian neural networks, and conformal prediction.
These methods provide the diagnostic self-awareness needed to identify when surrogate predictions
become unreliable. Drift detection algorithms offer statistical methods for identifying performance
degradation, including residual monitoring, uncertainty inflation tracking, distributional drift tests such as
the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and representation-level drift detection in latent spaces. These algorithms
implement the trigger logic that initiates governance review.
Active learning strategies enable scenario selection methods that identify the most informative new
physics simulations to run when correction is needed. Rather than random sampling, active learning
targets conditions at decision boundaries, rare scenarios, or regions of high uncertainty where new data
will maximally improve model performance. Continual learning methods provide techniques for updating
the surrogate without full retraining, including elastic weight consolidation, experience replay, and layer
freezing. These methods enable incremental updates that preserve previously learned knowledge while
adapting to new conditions, thereby avoiding catastrophic forgetting.
Multi-fidelity data fusion approaches combine information from multiple sources – field observations,
physics model outputs at different resolutions, and surrogate predictions – to refine estimates and reduce
uncertainty. Probability calibration encompasses post-hoc methods for ensuring that surrogate uncertainty
estimates are well-calibrated, including temperature scaling, isotonic regression, and reliability diagram
analysis. These techniques ensure that stated confidence intervals accurately reflect true prediction
reliability.
In general, Pillar C provides methods that the operational workflow uses. It serves as a toolkit from which
workflow steps draw appropriate techniques based on the site situation. The workflow (Section 2.2)
orchestrates when and how these methods are applied; Pillar C ensures the methods are available and
properly maintained.

2.1.4

Complementary Roles of the Three Pillars

The three pillars are co-essential to the framework’s operation, each playing a distinct and complementary
role. Pillar A is the design authority: It defines the rules, sets the thresholds, and determines what
constitutes acceptable performance. Without governance, the framework would lack the constraints
needed for regulatory compliance and responsible deployment. Pillar B provides the computational
foundation: It supplies the physics models that establish ground truth and the surrogates that enable fast
inference. Without modeling engines, the framework would have nothing to maintain or correct. Pillar C
supplies adaptive intelligence: It provides monitoring, detection, and correction methods that keep
surrogates accurate over time. Without recalibration mechanisms, the framework could not adapt to
changing conditions.
No pillar should be viewed as more fundamental than the others; they are interdependent. Governance
without models has nothing to govern; models without governance lack accountability; models without
adaptation will drift; adaptation without governance may be inappropriate. The framework’s value
emerges from their integration. Figure 1 illustrates the high-level architecture.

2.2 Recalibration Workflow
The workflow is structured as a seven-step cycle (Figure 2) organized into two phases. Phase 1,
Continuous Monitoring, encompasses Steps 1 through 3 and runs continuously during operations,
tracking surrogate performance and determining when intervention may be needed. Phase 2, Triggered
Response, encompasses Steps 4 through 7 and is activated only when Phase 1 indicates that an update is
warranted and governance approves the intervention.
This two-phase structure reflects a key principle from the governance framework described in Section
2.1.1: The presence of a recalibration trigger does not automatically mandate recalibration. Instead, the
framework requires formal justification that an update is necessary, proportional, and expected to improve
performance. The explicit DECIDE step (Step 3) implements this governance requirement.

2.2.1

Phase 1: Continuous Monitoring

Phase 1 runs continuously during site operations, providing ongoing assessment of surrogate performance
without modifying the operational model. These steps implement the monitor-detect-decide logic that
determines whether intervention is needed.
Step 1: MONITOR
The purpose of the MONITOR step is to track surrogate predictions against available monitoring data and
compute diagnostic metrics. This step compares surrogate predictions to field monitoring data as
observations become available, calculates prediction-observation residuals at priority monitoring
locations, and computes uncertainty metrics using embedded UQ methods such as MC-Dropout or deep
ensembles. The step also tracks prediction interval coverage and calibration statistics, logging all
comparisons for trend analysis and audit purposes.
The MONITOR step draws from Pillar A for data policies specifying which observations to assimilate,
quality assurance and quality control standards for data validation, and specifications for what metrics to
track. It draws from Pillar C for UQ methods used to compute predictive uncertainty and confidence
intervals. The outputs of this step are time series of residuals, uncertainty metrics, and coverage statistics
that feed into Step 2.

Framework Overview

8

PNNL-39195
Step 2: DETECT
The purpose of the DETECT step is to identify when surrogate performance has degraded beyond
acceptable thresholds using automated diagnostics. This step applies drift detection algorithms to residual
and uncertainty time series and compares current metrics against predefined trigger thresholds from Pillar
A. The step evaluates multiple detection criteria, including predictive uncertainty exceeding confidence
bounds, systematic bias in prediction-observation residuals, uncertainty inflation relative to historical
baselines, distributional drift in inputs or outputs using tests such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and
extrapolation beyond the original training domain.
The DETECT step draws from Pillar A for trigger thresholds, persistence requirements (e.g., 60-90 days),
and criteria for state transitions. It draws from Pillar C for drift detection algorithms, statistical tests, and
resilience metrics. The outputs of this step are the current trigger state and an evidence package
documenting the detection rationale.
Step 3: DECIDE
The purpose of the DECIDE step is to conduct human governance review of detection evidence and
formally determine whether an update is warranted. This step is critical because it implements the
governance requirement that triggers do not automatically mandate recalibration. A human reviewer or
governance committee, depending on site procedures, must evaluate the evidence and make a formal
determination.
The DECIDE step reviews the evidence package from Step 2, including residual trends and uncertainty
metrics, drift detection results and statistical significance, documented operational changes such as new
wells or pumping schedule changes, and comparison to historical performance patterns. The reviewer
assesses whether the observed degradation is meaningful (large enough to affect decision quality),
persistent (not a transient anomaly), and addressable (likely to be corrected by an incremental update).
Based on this assessment, the step determines the appropriate response. If an update is warranted, the
system proceeds to Phase 2. If an incremental update is unlikely to succeed, the system escalates directly
to full retraining.
When an update is approved, the DECIDE step also selects the update pathway. Pathway A, the
observation-driven approach, is selected when recent field observations provide sufficient new
information for fine-tuning. Pathway B, the simulation-driven approach, is selected when active learning
should identify new physics simulations to generate targeted training data.
The DECIDE step draws exclusively from Pillar A for governance policies, formal justification
requirements, approval workflows, and documentation standards. The outputs of this step are a formal
decision record documenting the determination, rationale, and selected pathway if an update is approved.

2.2.2

Phase 2: Triggered Response

Phase 2 is activated only when Step 3 (DECIDE) approves an update. These steps implement the
prepare-update-calibrate-deploy sequence that modifies the operational surrogate.
Step 4: PREPARE
The purpose of the PREPARE step is to assemble the dataset that will be used to update the surrogate.
The specific activities depend on the pathway selected in Step 3.

PNNL-39195
For Pathway A (observation-driven updates), the step compiles recent field observations that were not in
the original training set, applies quality assurance and quality control procedures per Pillar A data
policies, and formats data for compatibility with the surrogate training pipeline.
For Pathway B (simulation-driven updates), the step generates a candidate pool of operationally relevant
simulation scenarios and applies active learning algorithms to score candidates based on predictive
uncertainty in the candidate region, residual patterns relative to recent observations, and diversity
constraints covering spatial, temporal, and parameter dimensions. The step then selects 6 to 10 high-value
scenarios that maximize expected information gain, executes targeted physics simulations for selected
scenarios, and combines new simulation outputs with the replay buffer.
The PREPARE step draws from Pillar A for data policies and quality assurance standards, from Pillar B
for the physics simulator when executing new runs under Pathway B, and from Pillar C for active learning
strategies used in scenario selection. The output of this step is a prepared dataset with provenance
documentation.
Step 5: UPDATE
The purpose of the UPDATE step is to fine-tune the DL surrogate using continual learning methods to
incorporate new information while preserving previously learned knowledge. This step configures the
continual learning approach based on the update context, selecting from methods such as elastic weight
consolidation to penalize changes to weights important for previous tasks, experience replay to include
historical samples in each training batch, and layer freezing to preserve learned representations in early
encoder layers.
This step sets conservative hyperparameters, including a small learning rate to prevent catastrophic
forgetting, L2 regularization anchored to pre-update weights, and early stopping based on validation
performance. The step then fine-tunes the prepared dataset while monitoring training for convergence and
stability.
A key principle of this step is that it performs an incremental update, not full retraining. The goal is to
adapt the surrogate to new conditions while preserving its validity in previously learned domains. Full
retraining is reserved for cases where incremental updates repeatedly fail.
The UPDATE step draws from Pillar B for the surrogate model to be updated and training infrastructure,
and from Pillar C for continual learning methods including elastic weight consolidation, replay, and layer
freezing. The output of this step is an updated surrogate model in candidate status, not yet operational.
Step 6: CALIBRATE
Whereas the UPDATE step optimizes model weights for predictive accuracy, the CALIBRATE step
addresses the statistical reliability of the model’s expressed uncertainty.
The purpose of the CALIBRATE step is to perform post-hoc calibration of the updated model’s
uncertainty estimates to ensure stated confidence intervals accurately reflect true prediction reliability.
This step is separate from UPDATE because the constraints applied during continual learning can distort
the relationship between ensemble spread and true predictive uncertainty. Probability calibration
techniques are post-training activities that correct this distortion without modifying the model’s point
predictions.

The CALIBRATE step evaluates the updated model’s uncertainty calibration on held-out validation data
and generates reliability diagrams comparing stated confidence levels to empirical coverage. The step
verifies that calibrated prediction intervals meet acceptance criteria and documents calibration parameters
for reproducibility.
The CALIBRATE step draws from Pillar A for acceptance criteria for uncertainty calibration such as
coverage targets, and from Pillar C for probability calibration methods and reliability diagram analysis.
The output of this step is a calibrated surrogate model with documented uncertainty characteristics.
Step 7: DEPLOY
The purpose of the DEPLOY step is to validate the updated model against acceptance criteria and, if
successful, promote it to operational status with full governance documentation.
The validation phase evaluates the calibrated model on held-out test data not used in training or
calibration. Performance is compared against predefined acceptance criteria from Pillar A, including error
statistics at priority monitoring locations, uncertainty calibration metrics, spatial and temporal
consistency, and performance relative to the pre-update baseline. All validation results are documented.
If the model fails validation, the updated model is not deployed. The step assesses whether failure
indicates (a) a need for a larger update batch, in which case the workflow returns to Step 4 with expanded
scope; or (b) a fundamental domain shift requiring transition to the ESCALATE state for full retraining.
Failure rationale and next steps are documented.
If the model passes validation, the validated model is promoted to operational status. The previous
operational model is archived but not deleted. Model version records in Pillar B are updated. The step
generates comprehensive governance documentation, including trigger rationale from Step 2, decision
justification from Step 3, data sources and provenance from Step 4, training configuration from Step 5,
calibration parameters from Step 6, and validation results from Step 7. The trigger state machine returns
to the OK state.
The DEPLOY step draws from Pillar A for acceptance criteria, audit requirements, and rollback
procedures, and from Pillar B for model versioning and artifact management. The outputs of this step are
a deployed operational model if the model passes validation, or an escalation decision if the model fails
validation, along with a complete audit trail. This cycle repeats continuously during site operations, with
the frequency of updates determined by the rate of change in site conditions and the availability of new
monitoring data. The automated nature ensures that surrogates remain accurate without requiring manual
intervention or complete retraining.

2.2.3

Update Pathways

The framework supports two update pathways selected during Step 3 (DECIDE) based on the nature of
the detected degradation. Both pathways proceed through the same Phase 2 sequence (Steps 4–7); the
distinction lies in how Step 4 (PREPARE) assembles the update dataset.
Pathway A (Observation-Driven Update) is appropriate when recent field observations provide sufficient
new information to correct the surrogate without additional physics simulations. This pathway offers
faster turnaround, because no physics simulation wait time is required, and lower computational cost. It is
appropriate when degradation is due to gradual drift within the existing parameter space and relies on the
assumption that field observations adequately represent the conditions causing degradation. The workflow
proceeds from Steps 1–3 through Step 4 (compile observations) to Steps 5–7.

Pathway B (Simulation-Driven Update) is appropriate when the surrogate has encountered conditions
outside its training domain and targeted physics simulations are needed to generate appropriate training

changed boundary conditions, or regime shifts. Active learning ensures simulations target the most
informative conditions. The workflow proceeds from Steps 1–3 through Step 4 (active learning plus

The choice between pathways is a governance decision made in Step 3 based on the evidence and

periodically, typically monthly or quarterly, for management review, and formal decision points are
evaluated on a predictable schedule, typically quarterly, for governance review.

3.0 Methods
This section presents the technical methods underlying the recalibration framework, organized according
to the three-pillar architecture introduced in Section 2.0. Section 3.1 describes the governance framework
(Pillar A) that controls surrogate model deployment and updates. Section 3.2 presents the physics-based
numerical models and DL surrogates that form the modeling engine layer (Pillar B). Section 3.3 details
the recalibration mechanisms (Pillar C), including UQ, trigger logic, continual learning, active learning,
multi-fidelity data fusion, and full retraining procedures.

3.1 Governance Essentials
This section defines the governance framework controlling how DL surrogate models are developed,
evaluated, monitored, and updated for long-term groundwater remediation at EM sites. The purpose of
this governance pillar is to ensure that surrogate modeling is scientifically defensible, operationally stable,
and aligned with existing regulatory and quality-assurance expectations, while allowing efficient scenario
analysis and adaptive planning over multi-year remediation timelines.
The governance framework is designed to be applicable across all EM programs and sites, and is
grounded in established regulatory and quality standards, including EPA CERCLA guidance, DOE
quality assurance orders, and EPA modeling and data quality manuals. Within this framework, surrogate
models are treated as decision-support tools that primarily support screening-level analyses, planning, and
scenario exploration, while high-fidelity physics-based numerical models remain the authoritative basis
for final regulatory and remedy decisions.

3.1.1

Regulatory Alignment and Quality Assurance Foundations

Governance is anchored in federal environmental modeling and quality-assurance guidance. Under
CERCLA, remedial decisions must remain protective of human health and the environment over long
operational horizons, and analytical tools used to support these decisions must be transparent, defensible,
and periodically evaluated (EPA 2001) . The framework adopts principles from EPA environmental
modeling guidance (EPA 2009), which emphasizes clear documentation of model purpose, assumptions,
uncertainty, validation, and limitations before models are relied on for decision support. These principles
directly inform the governance requirements for surrogate use, update triggers, and performance
acceptance.
At the current prototyping and drafting stage, governance for surrogate modeling activities is aligned with
DOE EM-QA-001 (DOE EM 2019), which incorporates ASME NQA-1 standards and applies them to
environmental management activities, including software and modeling systems. DOE EM-QA-001
establishes a management system requiring defined acceptance criteria, performance monitoring,
corrective action, and continuous improvement for analytical tools used in mission-critical applications.
These regulatory and quality standards establish the expectation that surrogate models must be managed
as controlled analytical software, with formal verification, validation, documentation, and configuration
management processes that support regulatory confidence and long-term stewardship.

3.1.2

Model Usage Scope

A central governance principle is the explicit definition of how surrogate models may be used within EM
decision workflows. Surrogate models are intended primarily to support screening-level analyses,
including rapid evaluation of alternative remediation scenarios, sensitivity analyses, uncertainty

enabling fast exploration of large scenario spaces that would be impractical using high-fidelity numerical

For formal regulatory or final remedy decisions, surrogate model outputs are not used as standalone
evidence. Instead, surrogate results may inform and prioritize analyses, guide the selection of scenarios

Governance therefore ensures that surrogate models function as advisory, efficiency-enhancing tools,
rather than replacements for established numerical simulators. Any application of surrogate outputs
beyond screening-level support requires explicit documentation of assumptions, uncertainty, and
corroboration with physics-based results, consistent with EPA modeling guidance and DOE quality
expectations.

3.1.3

Acceptance Criteria and Recalibration Triggers

The governance framework establishes predefined acceptance criteria and recalibration triggers that
determine when surrogate models are considered reliable and when updates are warranted. These criteria
are defined prior to deployment and are based on project-specific data quality objectives and decision
needs, consistent with EPA quality assurance guidance for modeling applications (EPA 2002).
Acceptance criteria define the quantitative performance standards a surrogate model must satisfy to be
considered fit for operational use. They represent the bar the model must clear before deployment and
after updates. Typical acceptance criteria include error thresholds at priority monitoring locations (for
example, RMSE below a site-specific limit), calibration of uncertainty estimates such that prediction
interval coverage probability remains near 90%, spatial and temporal consistency between surrogate
predictions and physics-model reference simulations, and absence of systematic bias in predicted
concentration trends at compliance boundaries. Acceptance criteria are evaluated during initial
deployment validation (Step 7 of the workflow) and again after every recalibration cycle to confirm that
the updated surrogate remains fit for purpose.
Recalibration triggers are defined to detect meaningful degradation in surrogate performance or
applicability, including sustained increases in prediction residuals, inflation or miscalibration of
uncertainty estimates, and documented changes in boundary conditions or operational regimes that move
the system outside the surrogate’s original training domain. These triggers are evaluated using objective,
documented thresholds rather than ad hoc judgment, consistent with DOE quality assurance requirements
for corrective action when performance expectations are not met (DOE EM 2019).
Importantly, the presence of a recalibration trigger does not automatically mandate recalibration. Instead,
governance requires formal justification that an update is necessary, proportional, and expected to
improve performance. This conservative approach aligns with best practices for model lifecycle
management and reduces the risk of destabilizing otherwise reliable surrogate model behavior.

3.1.5

Transparency, Auditability, and Configuration Control

To support regulatory confidence and long-term stewardship, the governance framework requires full
transparency and auditability for surrogate model deployment and updates. All surrogate model versions
are subject to formal configuration control, including version tracking, documentation of training data and
assumptions, and preservation of prior approved models.
Each recalibration or significant update produces a documented governance record that includes the
trigger rationale, data sources used, evaluation against acceptance criteria, and verification results. These
records are retained as part of the project’s quality assurance documentation and support independent
review or audit when required.
Data provenance is also explicitly governed. Monitoring data, numerical model outputs, and surrogate
training datasets are tracked with metadata describing source, validation status, and applicability. This
ensures that the influence of any dataset on surrogate behavior can be reconstructed, supporting
traceability and corrective action if data quality concerns arise (EPA 2002).
Rollback is defined within this framework as the formal withdrawal of a surrogate model from
operational use, rather than reversion to a previously approved surrogate version. Rollback is invoked
when an attempted incremental update fails to meet predefined acceptance criteria, indicating that the
surrogate’s training domain or structure is no longer scientifically valid.
Under a rollback condition, neither the updated surrogate nor the prior surrogate is permitted for
continued screening or planning analyses, and surrogate-based decision support is therefore explicitly
suspended. In addition, analytical support reverts exclusively to the high-fidelity physics-based numerical
model, and rollback automatically triggers escalation to a governed full retraining process.
Full retraining develops a new surrogate baseline from scratch using updated physics-model ensembles,
revised assumptions, and refreshed validation datasets. It should be conducted under configuration control
and subject to the same verification, validation, uncertainty calibration, and acceptance criteria as an
initial deployment. The retrained surrogate is promoted to operational use only after all governance
requirements are satisfied.

3.1.6

Governance Essentials Summary

In summary, the governance framework defined in this section ensures that DL surrogate models are
integrated into EM groundwater remediation workflows in a manner that is controlled, transparent, and
regulator-aligned. Governance is grounded in established EPA and DOE quality assurance standards,
clearly limits surrogate use to screening- and planning-level decision support, and preserves the primacy
of high-fidelity physics-based numerical models for final regulatory and remedy decisions. Objective
acceptance criteria, documented recalibration triggers, and formal configuration control ensure that
surrogate models are used only within their validated domain. When surrogate validity is in question,
governance procedures require explicit withdrawal of surrogate use and escalation to a controlled full
retraining process, preventing unmanaged risk. Collectively, this governance foundation enables efficient
use of advanced modeling tools to support long-term remediation planning while maintaining scientific
rigor, operational stability, and regulatory confidence.

3.2 Modeling Engines
This section describes the physics-based numerical models and DL surrogates that form Pillar B of the
recalibration framework. Physics models provide high-fidelity reference simulations, while surrogates
provide fast inference for screening and scenario analysis.

3.2.1

Site Challenges and Modeling Needs

Numerical groundwater and reactive transport models provide the high‑fidelity representation of
site‑specific subsurface processes that underpin Pillar B. At EM sites, these models describe how
contaminants move and transform in complex hydrogeologic settings, and how engineered controls, such
as pump‑and‑treat systems, can influence plume evolution.
The physical phenomena represented typically include advection and dispersion of dissolved species,
sorption onto solid phases, and associated reaction processes (Genuchten 2016; Sethi and Di Molfetta
2019), and non-Fickian plume spreading arising from heterogeneity and diffusion-controlled mass
exchange between transmissive and low-permeability zones (Guo et al. 2021). Back diffusion from
fine- grained or low-permeability units is recognized as a major control on plume persistence after source
removal (Yang et al. 2016, 2017). Density-driven flow occurs in systems impacted by dense nonaqueous
phase liquid / light nonaqueous phase liquid (Huling 1991; McCaulou 1995; Newell 1995) or by seawater
intrusion (Werner et al. 2013; Xu et al. 2019). Engineered operations – pump and treat (P&T) wells,
injection/extraction networks, permeable reactive barriers, and hydraulic barriers – must also be
represented, enabling planners to evaluate how alternative configurations and engineered controls affect
plume pathways and concentrations at compliance points.

applications typically involve three-dimensional (3D), heterogeneous domains, long simulation horizons,
and tight numerical tolerances to preserve mass balance and stability, so individual forward runs can take
from hours to days on standard computing resources (Hammond et al. 2014; Maxwell et al. 2015). Many
of the planning tasks, however, require ensembles of simulations, Monte Carlo realizations of
hydrogeologic variability, scenario sweeps over remediation designs, or sensitivity analyses with respect
to uncertain parameters (Rubin 2003). The number of model evaluations needed for robust decision
support can easily reach the hundreds or thousands. As a result, it became impractical for planners to
interactively explore design space or to fully propagate uncertainty using only the high-fidelity model for
large complex sites. These limitations motivate the data-driven surrogate modeling engines that sit on top
of the numerical model.

3.2.4

Modeling Engine Summary

The modeling engine layer described in this section links site-specific physics to fast surrogate inference.
Site challenges and modeling needs (Section 3.2.1) establish the subsurface processes that must be
captured, including advection, dispersion, sorption, back diffusion, density-driven flow, and engineered
operations. Physics-based numerical models (Section 3.2.2) encode these processes in high-fidelity
simulators that serve as the authoritative reference for Pillar B, but impose computational costs that
preclude interactive scenario exploration, particularly for large, complex sites where robust decision
support may require hundreds to thousands of forward evaluations. The surrogate model landscape
(Section 3.2.3) addresses this bottleneck through five families of data-driven emulators, each matched to a
distinct input-output structure and decision use-case, with cross-cutting training practices drawn from the
reviewed literature. Together, the numerical simulators supply training data, physical grounding, and
validation targets, while the surrogates reproduce key results at a fraction of the computational cost. The
recalibration mechanisms described in Section 3.3 govern how these surrogates are kept aligned with
evolving site conditions and new observations over time.

3.3 Recalibration Mechanisms
The recalibration component operates as a single, governed loop designed for stability, transparency, and
regulatory credibility. During routine operations, the surrogate remains unchanged; the team conducts
monthly monitoring to track accuracy relative to the most recent stable baseline and calibration of stated
uncertainty. Operational changes (e.g., revisions to pumping schedules or boundary conditions) are
logged because they can move inputs outside the surrogate’s original training setting.
On a fixed decision window (e.g., quarterly), the program determines whether an update is justified.
Action is taken only when evidence of drift has persisted for 60–90 days or when a documented regime
change has occurred. When action is justified, the framework does not undertake a whole retrain; instead,
it executes a small, targeted update supported by a limited number of physics simulations and the most
recent field observations. The revised surrogate is then evaluated against pre-registered acceptance
criteria, including accuracy, uncertainty coverage, and performance at priority wells or zones.
Throughout, the high-fidelity physics model remains the authoritative reference used to inform and
validate changes.

3.3.2

Trigger Logic

As surrogate models operate over time, data distributions, and environmental conditions evolve. A trigger
logic framework activates model recalibration to maintain model trustworthiness and regulatory
compliance. The framework combines statistical thresholds with domain-informed criteria to support
adaptive learning while maintaining model interpretability and performance.

The drift monitoring framework consists of multiple complementary metrics organized into four
categories (Table 6). By combining complementary signals that capture loss of dynamical stability,
increasing model uncertainty, and structural changes in data and learned representations, the framework
allows early, interpretable, and statistically robust detection of regime drift to support timely model
recalibration.
Category 1: Tipping Point-Inspired Resilience Metrics
These metrics capture early signs of loss of dynamical stability, motivated by concepts from tipping point
science. They are applied to model outputs, residuals, and latent representations, without implying the
existence of physical tipping points in the underlying system. Rolling-window estimates of variance and
lag-1 autocorrelation are used to detect increasing fluctuation amplitude and memory, consistent with
reduced resilience. Higher-order moments, including skewness and kurtosis, are monitored to identify
asymmetric error growth and heavy-tailed behavior indicative of excursions toward unstable regions.
Category 2: Performance and Uncertainty-Based Metrics
These metrics focus on direct indicators of model reliability under deployment. Prediction residuals are
monitored using adaptive thresholds derived from rolling statistics, such as exceedance of multiples of the
rolling standard deviation. Persistent threshold violations or clustered spatial–temporal error patterns are
treated as evidence of localized or systemic degradation.
For probabilistic models, predictive uncertainty is explicitly tracked using measures such as variance or
entropy. Sustained increases relative to historical baselines are interpreted as growing epistemic
uncertainty and declining confidence in model predictions.
Category 3: Distributional and Representation-Level Drift Detection
These diagnostics target statistical and structural changes in data and learned features that may not
immediately manifest in errors or uncertainty. Distributional drift is assessed by comparing incoming
input and output distributions to reference distributions using nonparametric statistical tests, such as the
Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Statistically significant deviations indicate changes in the data-generating
process that may compromise model validity.
In parallel, drift indicators are applied in learned representation spaces, such as latent embeddings from
diffusion models or bottleneck features from convolutional architectures. Deviations from the training
manifold in latent space provide early detection of regime mismatch in high-dimensional settings where
marginal statistics may be insufficient.
Category 4: Distributional and Representation-Level Drift Detection
The final category integrates all diagnostic signals into a robust decision-making layer. Rather than
relying on single-metric thresholds, the framework employs Bayesian inference to combine evidence
from resilience, performance, uncertainty, and distributional indicators with prior knowledge and
alternative explanations (e.g., data quality changes).
Model recalibration or adaptation is triggered through composite gating mechanisms, implemented either
as Boolean rules (requiring concurrent exceedance across multiple metric families) or as score-based
aggregation into a unified drift risk index. This integration reduces false positives due to noise or transient
anomalies and ensures that interventions are driven by consistent, multi-source evidence.

3.3.3

Continual Learning

Continual learning for scientific surrogate models focuses on updating a model in response to gradual
system change while preserving valid historical behavior. The framework employs a conservative,
stability-focused approach that fits DOE operational requirements.
Mature continual learning approaches fall into two widely adopted categories: (1) regularization-based
techniques, which constrain parameter updates to avoid overwriting previously important knowledge, and
(2) replay-based techniques, which maintain stability by including a curated set of historical samples
during updating. Elastic weight consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017) is a prototypical regularization
strategy, penalizing deviations from weights identified as influential in previous training. Replay-based
strategies are widely applied across engineering and environmental forecasting domains to reduce the risk
of catastrophic forgetting when conditions shift incrementally (De Lange et al. 2021).
Within the environmental modeling literature, sequential fine-tuning has repeatedly been shown to
outperform static surrogates in non-stationary systems such as energy load forecasting (Hurtado et al.
2023) and long-horizon operational forecasting (Sayed et al. 2025). These same studies emphasize that
frequent retraining is unnecessary – and often destabilizing – when well-designed continual learning
mechanisms are in place. This aligns with findings in hydrologic surrogate modeling, where modest
periodic updates informed by new hydrologic states and compact replay buffers maintain performance
while avoiding the computational cost of full retraining (Asher et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2023).
Fine-tuning is initiated only after documented indicators of drift demonstrate meaningful degradation –
such as sustained increases in predictive error, divergence between modeled and observed temporal
trends, or reduced empirical coverage of uncertainty intervals (e.g., under coverage in UQ metrics). Once
triggered, the update process proceeds through several structured steps.
Step 1: Assemble update dataset. A compact update batch is assembled from three sources: (1) targeted
physics simulations representing the changed regime, (2) recent validated field observations, and (3) a
replay sample of historical cases comprising 70–75% of the batch to mitigate catastrophic forgetting,
consistent with replay ratios shown effective in continual learning applications (De Lange et al. 2021).
Step 2: Selective Layer Updates. Early encoder layers are frozen to preserve structural representations
learned during original training, while later layers are updated using a reduced learning rate and a
regularization penalty. The penalty may take the form of elastic weight consolidation, which protects
weights identified as influential during prior training (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017), or L2 anchoring toward the
pre-update parameter values, following recommendations from continual learning studies in dynamic
engineering systems (Hurtado et al. 2023).
Step 3: Validation and Early Stopping. Validation performance is monitored on a held-out set that
includes both recent observations and replayed historical cases. Metrics tracked include RMSE at priority
wells, empirical coverage of predictive intervals, and spatial error patterns across the model domain.
Training is stopped once improvements plateau over successive evaluation cycles to prevent overfitting to
the update batch (Sayed et al. 2025).

Step 5: Governance Review. A governance package needs to be prepared following the configuration

further evaluation.

3.3.5

Multi-fidelity Data Fusion

Multi-fidelity modeling enhances surrogate accuracy and computational efficiency by combining
information across sources of varying cost and resolution. In groundwater flow and reactive transport
applications, fidelity levels correspond to differences in grid resolution, process representation, or data
source. Low-fidelity sources – such as coarse-grid PFLOTRAN or MODFLOW simulations with
simplified boundary conditions or reduced-process representations – are inexpensive to generate and
provide broad characterization of system variability across the parameter and operational space.
High-fidelity sources – including fine-grid simulations with full reactive transport chemistry, locally
refined meshes around critical features such as source zones or compliance boundaries, and validated
field observations – are computationally expensive or limited in number but anchor predictions to
physically accurate behavior where precise results are required.
By combining large low-fidelity ensembles with targeted high-fidelity data, the framework covers a broad

with orders-of-magnitude reduction in total simulation cost compared to relying on high-fidelity data
alone. The fusion strategies described below provide the mathematical machinery for integrating these
fidelity levels into a unified surrogate prediction (Asher et al. 2015).

The choice among these strategies depends on the dimensionality of the problem, the cost ratio between

The multi-fidelity fusion procedure integrates coarse simulations, fine simulations, and field data through
a structured workflow.
Step 1: Fidelity Alignment. Identify fidelity levels and align datasets, ensuring spatial, temporal, and
unit consistency across all sources.
Step 2: Low-Fidelity Pre-training. Pre-train surrogate models on low-fidelity simulations, exploiting
their broad coverage of the parameter and operational space to establish global structure.
Step 3: High-Fidelity Correction. Introduce high-fidelity data, whether from simulation or observation,
to train a discrepancy model or co-Kriging correction layer. Predictions inherit low-fidelity global
structure while achieving high-fidelity local accuracy.
Step 4: Fidelity-Conditioned Uncertainty. Quantify uncertainty conditioned on the available fidelity at
each prediction location, expanding intervals where only low-fidelity information is available and
narrowing them where high-fidelity evidence supports confidence.
Step 5: Validation and Integration. Validate fused predictions against withheld high-fidelity runs or
independent observational periods to confirm improved performance over single-fidelity surrogates.
Integrate the fused surrogate into the continual learning loop described in Section 3.3.3, supporting more
accurate drift detection, better-informed active learning selection, and more stable fine-tuning updates.
Multi-fidelity methods integrate naturally with active learning and continual learning. When active
learning (Section 3.3.4) identifies high-uncertainty regions, the framework can strategically deploy either
low-fidelity or high-fidelity simulations based on the expected information gain and available
computational budget. Low-fidelity runs can be used to rapidly screen candidate regions before
committing expensive high-fidelity resources to the most informative configurations. The fused surrogate
provides improved baseline predictions that enhance drift detection sensitivity and reduce false-positive

3.3.6

Full Retraining

Full retraining constructs a new surrogate baseline from scratch when the existing model is no longer
representative of the physical system or operational domain. Whereas continual learning (Section 3.3.3)
applies targeted, incremental corrections to an otherwise valid surrogate, full retraining acknowledges that
the original training domain has been fundamentally superseded and that no sequence of fine-tuning
updates can restore reliable performance. This mechanism serves as the final safeguard in the
recalibration framework, providing a clean, traceable reset that aligns with DOE expectations for lifecycle
documentation and reproducibility in high-consequence environmental applications.
Full retraining is warranted when the drift diagnostics and trigger logic described in Section 3.3.2 indicate
that incremental updates can no longer restore surrogate performance, or when external changes to the
modeling system itself render the original training domain obsolete. Specific conditions include
fundamental regime shifts or major forcing changes that move the system outside the original training
domain, redefinition of the underlying physics model through revised geologic interpretations or updated
reaction networks, major reconfiguration of pumping infrastructure or boundary conditions, recalibration
of the numerical model after incorporation of new hydrogeologic data, and repeated fine-tuning failures
as described in Section 3.3.3. The concept of drift literature reinforces that full retraining is appropriate
when foundational assumptions underlying the surrogate no longer hold.
The retraining process proceeds through a formal sequence of steps consistent with the structured
workflows established in Sections 3.3.3 through 3.3.5.
Step 1: Verify Retraining Triggers. Verify that one or more of the conditions listed above has been
documented through the governance and monitoring procedures described in Sections 3.1 and 3.3.2. This
step ensures that full retraining is initiated based on objective evidence rather than precautionary
judgment alone.
Step 2: Redesign the Training Domain. Define updated ranges of hydrofacies, operational scenarios,
and boundary forcings, and parameter distributions consistent with the new conceptual understanding.
This redesign reflects the changed system state and any revisions to the underlying physics model,
ensuring that the new training ensemble spans the conditions the surrogate will encounter during its next
operational period.
Step 3: Ensemble Generation. Generate a new ensemble of high-fidelity simulations covering the
redesigned training domain. Where appropriate, supplement with low-fidelity runs using the multi-fidelity
strategies described in Section 3.3.5 to maintain broad parameter-space coverage while managing
computational cost. Recent field observations are integrated to anchor the surrogate to current empirical
system behavior.
Step 4: Surrogate Training and Uncertainty Calibration. Train a new surrogate from scratch, applying
the architecture selection, preprocessing, and training practices described in Section 3.2.3. UQ is
calibrated using the methods described in Section 3.3.1 to ensure that predictive intervals are well
calibrated from initial deployment.
Step 5: Validation and Acceptance. Validate the retrained surrogate against withheld high-fidelity
simulations and independent observational periods. Performance is evaluated against the predefined
acceptance criteria established in Section 3.1.3, including accuracy at priority wells, empirical coverage

4.0 Implementation Roadmap
This section describes how the three-pillar framework (Section 2.1) and seven-step workflow (Section
2.2) translate into an operational system. Rather than prescribing specific software tools or platforms, we
focus on what capabilities are needed and how they should be developed over time. The goal is to provide
a roadmap that can guide implementation while remaining flexible to site-specific constraints and
evolving technology.
The implementation must satisfy two demands. First, it must support the scientific workflow of
monitoring, detecting drift, and executing recalibration when needed. Second, it must meet DOE
governance requirements for traceability, reproducibility, and quality assurance. The design outlined here
attempts to balance both demands while maintaining consistency with the framework architecture
established in Section 2.0.

4.1 System Requirements
4.1.1

Operational Modes

The system operates in two distinct modes – offline and online – that place different demands on the
architecture and reflect different phases of the surrogate lifecycle.
Offline mode encompasses initial surrogate development: generating training ensembles with the physics
model, training the surrogate, calibrating uncertainty estimates, and validating against held-out data. This
phase is computationally intensive, potentially requiring thousands of physics runs and graphics
processing unit (GPU)-hours of training, but is not time-critical. Offline work typically occurs on highperformance computing (HPC) resources with batch scheduling. In terms of the framework, offline mode
establishes the initial Pillar B modeling engines before operational deployment. The methods described in
Section 3.2 (physics-based models and surrogate architectures) and Section 3.3.1 (UQ) are applied during
this phase to produce a validated surrogate ready for deployment.
Online mode is the operational phase where the trained surrogate supports decision-making. The
surrogate generates predictions on demand, the monitoring system tracks performance through the sevenstep workflow, and recalibration is triggered when needed. Online operations require faster turnaround –
predictions in seconds to minutes, diagnostic updates on a regular schedule such as daily or weekly, and
recalibration decisions on a governed timeline such as quarterly review. Online components may run on
more modest computing resources, though recalibration itself may require temporary access to HPC for
new physics runs. The methods from Section 3.3.2 (trigger logic) and Section 3.1 (governance) are
continuously active during online operations.
The architecture must support smooth transitions between modes. A surrogate developed offline must be
deployable online without modification. Data collected during online operations must feed back into
offline analysis when full retraining is needed. This bidirectional flow ensures that operational experience
improves subsequent surrogate generations.

4.1.2

Pillar-Based Capabilities

Each pillar of the framework requires specific capabilities for implementation. Understanding these
requirements helps ensure that the system architecture supports all aspects of the recalibration framework.
Pillar A (Governance) requires infrastructure for configuration management that tracks all surrogate
versions with their training data, assumptions, and validation results. The system must maintain audit
logging for all decisions, particularly at the DECIDE step, where human judgment authorizes
recalibration. Documentation generation capabilities must produce regulatory-compliant records that
satisfy DOE quality assurance requirements. Human review interfaces must present diagnostic evidence
clearly so that decision-makers can evaluate whether recalibration is warranted. The governance methods
detailed in Section 3.1 – including regulatory framework alignment, acceptance criteria, monitoring
schedules, and configuration control – define what this infrastructure must support.
Pillar B (Modeling Engines) requires a physics model interface capable of communicating with highfidelity simulators such as PFLOTRAN, MODFLOW, STOMP, or TOUGH2. This interface handles
input file generation, job submission including HPC scheduling where needed, output parsing, and result
archiving. The system must also provide surrogate training and inference infrastructure that can train
neural network architectures (such as those described in Section 3.2.3), store trained models with version
control, and generate predictions efficiently during operations. Data management capabilities must handle
both simulation outputs and field observations, maintaining provenance for all data used in training or
validation.
Pillar C (Recalibration Mechanisms) requires infrastructure for UQ computation using the methods
described in Section 3.3.1, including MC-Dropout, deep ensembles, or conformal prediction. Drift
detection and monitoring capabilities must implement the trigger logic from Section 3.3.2, tracking
performance metrics and evaluating drift indicators against governance-defined thresholds. When
recalibration is triggered, the system must support active learning for scenario selection (Section 3.3.4)
and continual learning for model updates (Section 3.3.3). Multi-fidelity data fusion (Section 3.3.5) and
full retraining (Section 3.3.6) capabilities complete the recalibration toolkit.

4.1.3

Workflow Support

The implementation must support both phases of the seven-step workflow defined in Section 2.2.
Phase 1 (Continuous Monitoring) implements Steps 1 through 3 of the workflow. The MONITOR step
requires automated comparison of surrogate predictions against field observations, computing residuals
and tracking uncertainty metrics over time. The DETECT step evaluates drift indicators against
predefined thresholds, distinguishing transient anomalies from persistent degradation using the
persistence filtering described in Section 3.3.2. The DECIDE step packages diagnostic evidence for
governance review and records the authorization decision. Throughout Phase 1, the system maintains the
trigger state logic – OK, WARNING, UPDATE, or ESCALATE – that guides operational decisions.
Phase 2 (Triggered Response) activates only when the DECIDE step authorizes an update,
implementing Steps 4 through 7. The PREPARE step assembles training data, potentially using active
learning to select high-value scenarios for new physics simulations. The UPDATE step fine-tunes
surrogate weights using continual learning techniques that preserve previously learned knowledge. The
CALIBRATE step ensures that uncertainty estimates remain well-calibrated after the update. The
DEPLOY step validates the updated surrogate against acceptance criteria and, if successful, deploys it
with full documentation. If validation fails, the system supports rollback procedures as defined in Section
3.1.5.

4.2 Human Decision Points
Not everything should be automated. The framework includes explicit decision points where human
judgment is required, reflecting Pillar A’s emphasis on governance and accountability. The software
architecture should make these decision points visible and provide the information needed to make good
decisions.

4.2.1

Recalibration Approval

The most important decision point occurs at Step 3 (DECIDE) of the workflow. Even when automated
diagnostics indicate drift, the decision to update a surrogate should involve human review. This is both a
governance requirement under DOE quality assurance standards and a practical safeguard against false
alarms. The monitoring system provides the evidence – drift metrics, historical trends, uncertainty
inflation, and comparison to acceptance criteria – but humans decide whether to proceed with
recalibration.
The decision options at this point include approving the update and proceeding to Phase 2, deferring
pending additional evidence while continuing intensified monitoring, or rejecting with documented
justification if the evidence is insufficient or the timing is inappropriate. Each decision must be recorded
with rationale for regulatory transparency.

4.2.2

Acceptance Criteria Definition

Acceptance criteria define what “good enough” means for a given application – the error thresholds,
uncertainty coverage requirements, and performance metrics that determine whether a surrogate is fit for
purpose. Setting these criteria requires domain expertise that cannot be automated. Humans must
understand site-specific risks, regulatory context, and the consequences of prediction errors to define
appropriate thresholds.
The system evaluates surrogate performance against these criteria, but the criteria themselves emerge
from human judgment informed by site characterization, regulatory requirements, and operational
experience. Section 3.1.3 describes the types of acceptance criteria (accuracy metrics, uncertainty
coverage, spatial and temporal consistency), but the specific threshold values must be determined by
qualified personnel for each application.

4.2.3

Rollback Decisions

When an updated surrogate fails validation at Step 7 (DEPLOY), a rollback decision is required. As
defined in Section 3.1.5, rollback means withdrawing the surrogate from operational use and reverting to
physics-only analysis – not reverting to a previous surrogate version, since that version has already been
flagged as degraded. This is a significant operational change that requires human authorization and
documentation.

The decision to roll back acknowledges that the recalibration attempt was unsuccessful and that the
surrogate cannot currently meet acceptance criteria. This may trigger retraining or, in some cases, a
fundamental reassessment of whether a surrogate approach is appropriate for the application.

4.2.4

Full Retraining Triggers

The most consequential decision arises when incremental updates repeatedly fail or when site conditions
change fundamentally. The decision to abandon the current surrogate and retrain from scratch involves
judging whether the surrogate’s foundational assumptions remain valid. Perhaps the training distribution
no longer represents current site conditions, or perhaps the surrogate architecture is inadequate for the
prediction task.
Full retraining has major resource implications – potentially thousands of new physics simulations and
significant computational effort – and should not be automated. Section 3.3.6 describes the technical
process for full retraining, but the decision to initiate it requires human assessment of whether the
investment is justified and whether the underlying approach remains sound.

4.3 Development Phases
Implementing the full framework is a multi-year effort. A phased approach delivers value incrementally
while building toward the complete vision. Each phase builds on the previous one, with lessons learned
informing subsequent development.

4.3.1

Phase 1: Prototype Demonstration

The immediate goal is to demonstrate that the recalibration concept works by completing one full cycle
on a synthetic or simplified test case.
The scope of Phase 1 includes developing a single surrogate architecture – such as U-FNO for the twodimensional (2D) cross-section transport case or U-Net for P&T plume prediction – trained on synthetic
physics model outputs. Basic UQ using MC-Dropout or deep ensembles provides predictive uncertainty
estimates. Simple threshold-based drift detection identifies when performance has degraded. The
recalibration workflow is executed manually: A researcher runs diagnostics, decides an update is needed,
generates new training data through targeted physics simulations, fine-tunes the model using continual
learning techniques, recalibrates uncertainty estimates, and validates results against acceptance criteria.
The work is documented in Jupyter notebooks or Python scripts that capture each step.
The key deliverable is a working demonstration showing before-and-after performance when the
surrogate is updated in response to simulated drift. This demonstration proves that the seven-step
workflow can improve surrogate performance and that the methods from Section 3.0 work together as
intended.
Success is measured by completing the full MONITOR → DETECT → DECIDE → PREPARE →
UPDATE → CALIBRATE → DEPLOY cycle with documented performance improvement. At this
phase, there is no persistent software system, just reproducible code that proves the concept.

4.4 Technical Considerations
4.4.1

Computational Requirements

The framework spans a wide range of computational demands that must be accommodated by the
implementation of the architecture.
Physics simulations are the most expensive component. A single PFLOTRAN run for a 2D cross-section
case takes minutes; a full 3D site model may take hours or days. Generating training ensembles of
hundreds to thousands of runs requires HPC access with batch scheduling. Recalibration updates need
fewer runs – typically tens of runs selected by active learning – but still benefit from parallel execution.
Surrogate training requires GPU resources for efficient execution. Initial training of a U-FNO or U-Net
model may take hours on a modern GPU. Fine-tuning for recalibration is faster, typically minutes to an
hour, but still benefits from GPU acceleration. Training can occur on HPC GPU nodes, cloud resources,
or dedicated workstations, depending on availability.
Inference and monitoring are lightweight by comparison. A trained surrogate generates predictions in
milliseconds. Diagnostic calculations – residuals, uncertainty metrics, drift statistics – are similarly fast.
These operations can run on standard computing resources without specialized hardware.
The architecture should not assume that all components run on the same hardware. Physics simulations
may run on one HPC system, training on another, and monitoring on a third. Data must flow between
these environments reliably, with appropriate handling of different file systems, network configurations,
and security boundaries.

4.4.2

Interoperability

Simulator coupling presents a practical challenge because physics simulators were not designed with
machine learning workflows in mind. The framework must work with simulators as they are, typically
through file-based interfaces: generate input files according to simulator specifications, run the simulator
as a subprocess or batch job, and parse output files to extract results. This approach is less elegant than
API-based coupling but far more practical given the diversity of simulators in use across EM sites. The
Physics Model Interface component must accommodate different simulators – PFLOTRAN,
MODFLOW, STOMP, TOUGH2, and others – without requiring changes to downstream components.
Data formats should follow existing conventions where possible. Simulation outputs are often in
domain-specific formats such as PFLOTRAN HDF5 or MODFLOW binary files. The framework should
read these directly rather than requiring format conversion that could introduce errors or lose information.
For internal data – trained models, diagnostic logs, configuration files – standard formats such as JSON,
YAML, and PyTorch checkpoints provide adequate interoperability.
Reproducibility requires that every result can be regenerated from archived inputs. This means versioning
not just code but also training data, model weights, configuration files, and random seeds. The data
management layer must support this requirement without imposing excessive overhead on routine
operations. Every prediction should be traceable to the specific surrogate version, training data, and
configuration that produced it.

4.4.3

Quality Assurance Alignment

DOE quality assurance requirements apply to software used in decision support for environmental
management. These requirements do not dictate specific technologies but do constrain how the software
is developed and operated. The architecture should make compliance straightforward rather than
burdensome.
Configuration control requires that every deployed component has a defined version, that changes be
documented and reviewed before deployment, and that the system be able to report exactly what software
versions produced a given result. This applies to surrogate models, monitoring algorithms, and the
framework software itself.
Verification and validation require that each component be tested against defined acceptance criteria
before deployment, with test results documented and retained. For surrogate models, this means
validation against properly held-out data and comparison to physics model predictions. For software
components, this means unit tests, integration tests, and documented test coverage.
Traceability requires that audit logs record what data was used, what computations were performed, and
what results were produced. These logs must be retained for the project lifetime – potentially decades for
long-term remediation sites. The data management layer must support efficient storage and retrieval of
audit information.
Corrective action procedures must define how problems are diagnosed, how fixes are implemented, and
how effectiveness is verified. When a surrogate fails validation or produces unexpected results, the
system must support root cause analysis and documented resolution.

4.5 Summary
This implementation roadmap translates the three-pillar framework and seven-step workflow into
operational capabilities through a structured development approach.
The system requirements are organized around what each pillar needs for implementation, with clear
distinction between offline development and online operations. Pillar A requires governance
infrastructure for configuration management, audit logging, and human review interfaces. Pillar B
requires physics model interfaces and surrogate training infrastructure. Pillar C requires monitoring, drift
detection, and recalibration capabilities. The workflow support ensures that both continuous monitoring
(Steps 1-3) and triggered response (Steps 4-7) phases can be executed with appropriate automation and
human oversight.
Four explicit human decision points preserve governance accountability: recalibration approval at the
DECIDE step, acceptance criteria definition, rollback decisions when validation fails, and full retraining
triggers when incremental updates are insufficient. These decision points ensure that human judgment
remains central to surrogate management despite automation of routine operations.
The phased development approach – from prototype demonstration through integrated framework to
operational system – provides progressive capability building with lessons learned informing each
subsequent phase. This approach manages risk while delivering value incrementally.

5.3 Case Study Design
This section describes the controlled case study that exercises the recalibration framework on the P&T
prototype introduced in Section 5.2.2. The case study simulates a realistic operational challenge: A
surrogate trained under routine plume conditions encounters a regime shift in the governing model inputs
– such as a modified well-field configuration or a revised source-term characterization derived from
updated site-investigation data – that displaces plume dynamics outside the surrogate’s original training
distribution. The design evaluates whether the framework’s continual-learning mechanisms (Section
3.3.3) can restore predictive accuracy without full retraining, and how the choice of drift-characterization
metric influences detection and update effectiveness.

5.3.1

Drift Scenario and Operational Rationale

In operational P&T systems, surrogate models are trained on historical plume snapshots that reflect a
limited range of extraction configurations and plume dynamics. Over time, well-field modifications,
source-strength changes, or aquifer property updates can produce plume behavior that differs substantially
from training conditions. The case study replicates this situation by partitioning the 10,000-realization
simulation dataset (Section 5.2.2) into two regimes:
• Low-change regime. Realizations in which successive plume snapshots exhibit modest spatial and
concentration changes, representative of the quasi-steady conditions under which the surrogate was
originally trained.
• Extreme-change regime. Realizations exhibiting rapid plume expansion, contraction, or
redistribution – conditions analogous to those arising from operational perturbations such as well
activation, rate increases, or source release events.
This partitioning creates a controlled distribution shift: The surrogate is first trained exclusively on
low-change data (establishing a baseline), and its ability to generalize to – and then recover accuracy on –
extreme-change conditions is systematically evaluated.

5.3.2

Training Paradigms for Recalibration Evaluation

Three training paradigms are compared to isolate the value of the framework’s continual-learning
approach relative to static training and full retraining.
• Model A: Baseline (low-regime only). Trained exclusively on the low-change subset, representing a
surrogate deployed before any regime shift occurs. This model establishes the pre-drift performance
baseline and is expected to degrade on extreme-change samples.
• Model B: Fine-tuned (continual learning). Initialized from Model A and fine-tuned on a combined
dataset of low-change and extreme-change samples, following the continual-learning procedures
described in Section 3.3.3. This paradigm represents the framework’s primary recalibration pathway
(Pathway B, simulation-driven update), demonstrating whether targeted fine-tuning can restore
accuracy while preserving previously learned behavior.
• Model C: Retrained from scratch. Trained from scratch on the same combined dataset used for
Model B, representing the full-retraining fallback described in Section 3.3.6. Model C provides an
upper-bound reference for what accuracy is achievable when computational cost is not constrained.

5.3.3

Evaluation Protocol

All models are evaluated on the extreme-change subset corresponding to the selected partitioning
criterion. Performance is reported using denormalized RMSE on held-out extreme samples, enabling
direct comparison across training paradigms.
Evaluation deliverables include:
• Accuracy comparison. RMSE of Models A, B, and C on extreme-change samples, quantifying the
accuracy gap between the baseline surrogate (A), the fine-tuned surrogate (B), and the fully retrained
surrogate (C).
• Forgetting assessment. RMSE of Models B and C on low-change samples, verifying that fine-tuning
does not degrade performance on previously learned conditions (catastrophic forgetting).
• Visual diagnostics. Side-by-side comparisons of ground truth, prediction, and prediction error for
selected extreme samples, providing interpretable evidence of recalibration effectiveness.
These evaluation outputs map directly to the acceptance criteria defined in Section 3.1.3 and provide the
empirical basis for the governance documentation required at the DEPLOY step (Step 7) of the
recalibration workflow.

5.4 Expected Outcomes
The prototype demonstration is expected to produce several quantitative outcomes. Surrogate inference
speedups of 10³ to 105 times relative to physics-based models will enable real-time decision support.
Recalibration will require fewer than 10 targeted physics simulations selected by active learning,
demonstrating efficient use of computational resources. Post-recalibration accuracy will recover within
acceptance criteria defined in Section 3.1.3.
The demonstration will produce documented deliverables including a complete recalibration cycle with
all seven workflow steps executed and recorded, governance documentation meeting the audit trail
requirements of Section 3.1.5, and reproducible code capturing each step for subsequent development
phases.

6.0 Discussion and Conclusions
This section addresses the current limitations of the framework, identifies areas for further investigation
that extend beyond the implementation roadmap presented in Section 4.0 and the prototype demonstration
plan in Section 5.0, summarizes the expected impact, and concludes with key takeaways for DOE
stakeholders.

6.1 Limitations
6.1.1

Dependence on Physics-Model Quality

Surrogate accuracy is fundamentally bound by the quality of the physics-based models used to generate
training data. Errors or biases in the numerical models – whether from simplified boundary conditions,
incomplete process coupling, or uncertain parameterization – will propagate to the surrogates. The
recalibration framework can detect when surrogate predictions deviate from new physics-model runs, but
it cannot correct for systematic deficiencies in the physics models themselves. Translating simulators into
credible site predictions requires that hydraulic conductivities, recharge rates, boundary conditions, and
transport parameters be carefully adjusted until simulated heads and concentrations adequately reproduce
field observations. Whether calibration relies on automated inverse methods such as PEST or DAKOTA
(Adams et al. 2020; Doherty 1994; Hill and Tiedeman 2007) or ensemble-based DA (Evensen 2009),
ensuring that the underlying numerical models remain well-calibrated is a prerequisite for effective
surrogate maintenance.

6.1.2

Computational Requirements for Ensemble Methods

Ensemble-based UQ requires training and maintaining multiple surrogate instances, increasing
computational and storage demands relative to a single deterministic model. While surrogate inference
remains orders of magnitude faster than physics-based simulation, the aggregate cost of ensemble
training, storage of model checkpoints, and periodic retraining across all ensemble members must be
accounted for in resource planning. Efficient alternatives such as MC-Dropout or spectral-normalized
approaches may reduce this burden but require further evaluation in the groundwater context.

6.1.3

Validation Status

The framework presented in this report is at the concept and design stage. Validation to date has been
limited to illustrative synthetic test cases. Application to real field data – with actual measurement noise,
irregular sampling intervals, data gaps, and full 3D site complexity typical of EM applications – remains
to be demonstrated through the prototype development outlined in Section 5.0.

6.2 Areas for Further Investigation
The implementation roadmap in Section 4.0 defines the sequencing and deliverables for framework
development, and the prototype demonstration plan in Section 5.0 describes the initial proof-of-concept
implementations. Several scientific questions that cut across those efforts warrant specific attention.

6.2.1

Trigger-Threshold Calibration

The drift-detection and recalibration trigger logic described in Section 3.3 relies on thresholds for
uncertainty growth, residual divergence, and input-distribution shift. Optimal threshold values are likely
site-specific, depending on historical variability, measurement frequency, and the consequence of delayed
recalibration. Developing general guidance – or adaptive threshold methods – that reduce the need for
manual tuning would improve framework usability across sites. Established drift-detection algorithms
provide starting points, but environmental applications may require domain-specific adaptations given the
slower timescales and sparse observations typical of groundwater monitoring networks.

6.2.2

Multi-Fidelity Fusion

Combining information from models of varying fidelity and computational cost – for example,
coarse-grid screening models, refined local models, and DL surrogates – offers a path toward more
efficient generation of recalibration data. Multi-fidelity methods have shown promise in related domains,
but their integration into the recalibration workflow for groundwater transport requires further
development, particularly regarding how fidelity levels map to the governance-tier requirements defined
in Pillar A.

6.2.3

Transfer Learning Across Sites

Deploying the framework at a new DOE site currently requires site-specific training data from local
physics models. Transfer learning could reduce this data requirement by leveraging surrogate knowledge
from previously characterized sites with analogous hydrogeological settings. Key open questions include
how to quantify transferability between sites, how to define domain similarity for heterogeneous aquifer
systems, and how governance procedures should account for transferred vs. locally validated model
components.

6.2.4

Scalability

Realistic EM applications typically involve 3D, heterogeneous domains, long simulation horizons, and
tight numerical tolerances to preserve mass balance and stability. Extension to these fully 3D domains
with finer spatial resolution and more complex multi-species reactive transport will stress both the
surrogate architectures and the recalibration methods. Whether the continual-learning and active-learning
strategies described in Section 3.3 scale gracefully – or require architectural modifications – is an
empirical question that the prototype phase will begin to address.

6.2.5

Regulatory Acceptance Pathways

Adaptive machine-learning models introduce a paradigm shift for regulatory review: The approved model
is no longer static. Engagement with regulatory agencies is needed to establish acceptable review and
approval procedures for models that update over time. The governance structure in Pillar A (Section 3.1)
is designed to facilitate this engagement, but the specific regulatory pathway remains to be negotiated on
a site-by-site basis.

6.3 Expected Impact
6.3.1

Operational Benefits

The primary operational benefit of the framework is sustained surrogate accuracy over multi-year
remediation timelines without requiring costly full retraining. Surrogate inference speedups of 103 to 105
relative to physics-based models enable real-time decision support and interactive scenario exploration
that are infeasible with numerical simulation alone – particularly for planning tasks that require ensembles
of simulations, Monte Carlo realizations of hydrogeologic variability, or scenario sweeps over
remediation designs, where the number of model evaluations needed can easily reach the hundreds or
thousands. Targeted recalibration further reduces computational cost by focusing physics-model runs on
the scenarios that most improve surrogate performance, rather than regenerating the full training dataset.

6.3.2

Scientific Contributions

The framework contributes to a novel integration of UQ, drift detection, continual learning, and active
learning within a governance-aware architecture. This integration addresses a gap in the scientific ML
literature, where these methods are typically developed and evaluated in isolation. The governance layer –
rarely addressed in machine learning research – provides a template for deploying adaptive models in
regulatory contexts beyond groundwater remediation.

6.3.3

Broader Applicability

Although developed for EM groundwater applications, the framework’s three-pillar architecture and
seven-step workflow are domain-general. The framework systematically updates the surrogate only as
needed, maintaining accuracy without incurring the cost of full retraining. Potential applications include
other EM sites with different contaminant types, hydrogeological settings, or remediation technologies;
long-term climate-resilience modeling; and scientific surrogate maintenance in fields such as materials
science and engineering design. Open-source release of code, containers, and demonstration datasets –
planned as part of the implementation roadmap in Section 4.0 – will enable community adoption and
contribution.

6.4 Conclusions
Five key takeaways emerge from this framework development:
1. Surrogates require systematic maintenance. DL models trained once and deployed indefinitely
will degrade as site conditions, remediation operations, or conceptual models evolve. Systematic
monitoring and recalibration are essential for multi-year operational use.
2. Physics-based models remain the authoritative reference. Surrogates accelerate prediction but do
not replace numerical simulation. Every recalibration cycle is anchored to new physics-model
truth data, preserving the scientific foundation of site decisions.
3. Governance is not optional. Embedding oversight, documentation, acceptance criteria, and human
decision authority throughout the workflow is what distinguishes an operationally deployable
framework from a research prototype. Regulatory confidence depends on this structure.
4. Targeted updates outperform static approaches. Adaptive strategies that detect drift and execute
focused recalibration are more efficient than either ignoring degradation or performing periodic
full retraining regardless of need.

Appendix A – Surrogate Model Literature Context
This appendix provides detailed literature context for the surrogate model landscape discussed in Section
3.2.3. The material presented here supports the framework’s modeling engine component (Pillar B) by
documenting the current state of deep learning (DL) surrogate models for subsurface flow and transport
applications.
The surrogate model literature review was conducted using Web of Science with search terms targeting
DL applications in groundwater modeling, subsurface flow prediction, and contaminant transport
simulation. The search covered publications from 2018 to 2024, reflecting the rapid development of DL
methods for scientific computing during this period. Papers were selected based on relevance to
groundwater remediation applications, with emphasis on architectures that have demonstrated success in
predicting spatiotemporal fields from physics-based model outputs.
The review identified six primary architectural families with demonstrated applicability to subsurface
modeling: convolutional encoder-decoder models, operator-learning models, sequence models, generative
models, regression and classical surrogates, and structured domain surrogates. Each family offers distinct
advantages depending on the prediction task, data availability, and computational constraints.
Comprehensive reviews by Razavi et al. (2012) and Asher et al. (2015) provide foundational context for
surrogate modeling in water resources applications. Surrogate modeling publication trends were
quantified using the Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate Analytics). We performed a topic search
(12/5/2025) combining groundwater and subsurface keywords with surrogate-modeling terms (TS =
((groundwater OR subsurface OR aquifer OR “reactive transport” OR “porous media”) AND (“surrogate
model*” OR emulator* OR “reduced-order” OR metamodel* OR “response surface” OR “Fourier neural
operator*” OR “neural operator*” OR “operator learning” OR “deep learning surrogate” OR “physicsinformed”)), limited to articles and reviews in English for the period 2005 to 2026.
